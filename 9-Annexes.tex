\section{Additional problem families}\label{app:extra-problems}

The experiments of \S\ref{sec:bench-nondpp} and \S\ref{sec:bench-sensitivity} use two
problem families beyond the suite, and rescaled versions of the suite problems. Each family
is generated from a size and a random seed, with standard normal data unless stated
otherwise.

The \texttt{lasso} family minimizes
\BEQ\label{eq:lasso-family}
\lVert \mmat{A}\mvec{x} - \mvec{b} \rVert_2^2 + \lambda \lVert \mvec{x} \rVert_1
\EEQ
over $\mvec{x} \in \reals^n$, with $\mmat{A} \in \reals^{2n \times n}$. In the \ac{DPP}
variant, $\mvec{b}$ and $\lambda$ are parameters. In the non-\ac{DPP} variant,
$\lambda$ is replaced by the product $\alpha\lambda$ of two parameters, which \ac{DPP}
forbids, with $\alpha = 1$. In the parameter-free variant, both are constants.
The \texttt{factor\_cov} family is FactorCovarianceModel with $n$ assets and
$\max(\lfloor n/100 \rfloor, 2)$ factors. Its risk aversion $\gamma$ is a parameter in the
\ac{DPP} variant, the product $\gamma\delta$ of two parameters in the non-\ac{DPP} variant,
and a constant in the parameter-free variant.

The sensitivity experiment rescales nine problems of the suite: HuberRegression,
FactorCovarianceModel, OptimalAdvertising, SVMWithL1Regularization, LeastSquares, SimpleQP,
Yitzhaki, CVaR and SemidefiniteProgramming. Each problem fixes its sizes as literals in its
source, and the rescaled version multiplies these literals by a factor
$s \in \{1/4, 1/2, 2, 4\}$ before the problem is built, so that its data has about $s$ times
as many nonzeros. The parameter-free \texttt{lasso} family is measured at
$n \in \{250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000\}$. The density experiment multiplies the data matrix of
four problems, LeastSquares, HuberRegression, SimpleQP and
SVMWithL1Regularization, and the matrix $\mmat{A}$ of \texttt{lasso} with $n = 1000$,
entrywise by a random $0/1$ mask with a fraction $\rho$ of ones, drawn from its own
generator so that the other data is unchanged. The masked matrix stays a dense array.

\section{Ablation}
\label{sec:bench-ablation}

The branch of \texttt{CVXPY} used in Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme2} has two switches that turn parts of
\texttt{diffengine} off. The first stores every constant matrix in the sparse format, so
that no dense block is built from constant data. The second discards the engine's problem at
every parameter update and repeats the conversion and the symbolic pass, which removes the
split. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports each switch on the measurement it affects.

Without the dense blocks, the first canonicalization is $1.27$ times slower in geometric
mean, with a significant difference on $23$ of the $25$ problems, and the peak memory
changes by a factor of $0.87$. The switch does not slow down every problem: without the
dense blocks, \texttt{diffengine} is faster on $8$ problems, among them ConvexPlasticity by a
factor of $5.1$ and CVaR by a factor of $1.6$. Without the dense blocks, \texttt{diffengine} is
$1.95$ times faster than the best \texttt{CVXPY} backend in geometric mean. Without the
split, the re-solves of the seven \ac{DPP} problems are $2.56$ times slower.
Tables~\ref{tab:app-cold}, \ref{tab:app-warm} and~\ref{tab:app-memory} give both
contrasts on every problem.

\section{Sensitivity to size and density}
\label{sec:bench-sensitivity}

The problems of the suite have fixed sizes. To see how the comparison depends on the
characteristics of the problem, we vary them on the suite problems themselves, as
Appendix~\ref{app:extra-problems} describes. Each problem is rescaled by a factor $s$ on its
dimensions, which multiplies the number of nonzeros of its data by about $s$. Nine
problems and the parameter-free \texttt{lasso} family are measured at
$s \in \{1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4\}$, and all $25$ problems at $s \in \{1/2, 1, 2\}$. On five problems with a dense data
matrix, a fraction $1 - \rho$ of the entries of the matrix is set to zero, with
$\rho \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1\}$. Every point is the median of five runs.

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity-size} reports the ratio of \texttt{diffengine} to the best
\texttt{CVXPY} backend as the size grows. On seven of the ten families, the ratio stays
between $0.13$ and $0.22$ over a factor of $16$ in size, and on CVaR and
SemidefiniteProgramming between $0.55$ and $0.67$. OptimalAdvertising is the exception: its
ratio grows from $0.78$ to $1.67$ between $s = 1/4$ and $s = 1$. At the larger sizes,
\texttt{diffengine} exceeds the memory limit on CVaR and OptimalAdvertising, and on two more
instances the correctness check does not fit in the limit, so their times are not reported.

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity-size25} reports the same ratio on all $25$ problems. On $18$ of the
$20$ problems on which \texttt{diffengine} is the fastest at $s = 1$, the ratio changes by at
most $0.04$ between $s = 1/2$ and $s = 2$. CVaR goes from $0.55$ at $s = 1/2$ to the memory
limit at $s = 2$, and Murray, at $0.95$ for $s = 1$, is slower at the other two sizes, at $1.14$ and $1.01$. On the
five problems on which \texttt{diffengine} is slower, it stays slower at every size it
completes, and on ConvexPlasticity the ratio grows from $5.29$ to $8.68$.

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity-density} reports the ratio as the density falls. On all five
problems, \texttt{diffengine}'s advantage shrinks, from a ratio of $0.14$ to $0.17$ on dense
data to a ratio between $0.40$ and $2.56$ at $\rho = 0.01$. The masked data stays a dense
array, so much of the dense block that the matrix product builds from it is zero.
Above a density of $20\,\%$, \texttt{diffengine} is faster on every problem.
Below it, it is slower on HuberRegression, SVMWithL1Regularization and LeastSquares at
$\rho = 0.05$, and on HuberRegression at $\rho = 0.01$.
