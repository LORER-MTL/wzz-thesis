\paragraph{UnconstrainedQP.} The problem is
\BEQ
\mbox{minimize} \quad \bigl\lVert \mmat{F}^{\mathsf{H}} \mmat{E}(\mvec{z}) \mmat{F} \mvec{x}
- \mvec{y} \bigr\rVert_2, \qquad \mmat{E}(\mvec{z}) = \mmat{I}_{14} \otimes \diag(\mvec{z}),
\EEQ
over $\mvec{z} \in \complex^{18}$, where $\mmat{F}$ is the $252 \times 252$ discrete Fourier
transform matrix, $\mmat{F}^{\mathsf{H}}$ its conjugate transpose, and $\mvec{x}$ and $\mvec{y}$ are constant vectors. Evaluated from the left,
the product $(\mmat{F}^{\mathsf{H}} \mmat{E}) \mmat{F}$ has a dense Jacobian of
$63\,504 \times 36$ entries, which the numeric pass fills one entry at a time. Evaluated from
$\mvec{x}$, as pull request \#3524 does, every intermediate is a vector, and the
canonicalization takes $16$ milliseconds.

\paragraph{OptimalAdvertising.} The problem is
\BEQ
\mbox{maximize} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{250} \min\bigl(r_i \mvec{p}_i^\top \mvec{d}_i,\; b_i\bigr)
\quad \mbox{subject to} \quad \mmat{D} \ge \zeros, \quad \mmat{D}^\top \ones \le \mvec{t}, \quad
\mmat{D} \ones \ge \mvec{c},
\EEQ
over $\mmat{D} \in \reals^{250 \times 1000}$ with rows $\mvec{d}_i^\top$. Each minimum term
depends on one row of $\mmat{D}$, but its Jacobian spans all $250\,000$ variables, and the
symbolic pass visits every column of it. Its cost grows with the number of terms times the
number of variables, which is why the ratio grows with the size of the problem
(\S\ref{sec:bench-sensitivity}).

\paragraph{SDPSegfault1132.} With $\mmat{K} = \mmat{V} \mmat{G} \mmat{V}^\top$, the problem is
\BEQ
\mbox{maximize} \quad \Tr \mmat{G} - \bigl\lVert \ones \diag(\mmat{K})^\top + \diag(\mmat{K})
\ones^\top - 2 \mmat{K} - \mmat{D} \bigr\rVert_F
\EEQ
over $\mmat{G} \in \symm^{99}_+$, with constant $\mmat{V} \in \reals^{100 \times 99}$ and
$\mmat{D} \in \reals^{100 \times 100}$. Three dense blocks of $49.5$ million entries cancel
in a sum with $1.01$ million nonzeros, and \texttt{diffengine} carries the cancelled entries
through both passes.

\paragraph{ConvexPlasticity.} The problem is
\BEQ
\begin{array}{ll}
\mbox{minimize} & \tfrac{1}{2} \mvec{e}^\top \mmat{S} \mvec{e} + \tfrac{1}{2}
(\mvec{p} - \mvec{p}_0)^\top (h \mmat{I}) (\mvec{p} - \mvec{p}_0) \\
\mbox{subject to} & \sqrt{3/2}\, \lVert \mmat{Q} \gvec{\sigma}_j \rVert_2 \le \sigma_0 + h p_j,
\quad j = 1, \ldots, 3000,
\end{array}
\EEQ
over the stresses $\gvec{\sigma}_j \in \reals^4$ and $\mvec{p} \ge \zeros$, where
$\mvec{e}$ stacks the differences $\gvec{\sigma}_j - \gvec{\sigma}_{\mathrm{el},j}$,
$\mmat{S}$ is block diagonal and $\mmat{Q}$ extracts the deviatoric part. The diagonal $h \mmat{I}$ is given as a
dense array and becomes a dense $3000 \times 3000$ block of $\mmat{P}$.

\subsection{Problems that are not DPP}\label{app:nondpp}

The tensor of \texttt{CVXPY} exists only for \ac{DPP} problems. Table~\ref{tab:nondpp}
compares the re-solves of two problem families, each written in three variants with the same
problem data. The
\texttt{lasso} family minimizes
$\lVert \mmat{A}\mvec{x} - \mvec{b} \rVert_2^2 + \lambda \lVert \mvec{x} \rVert_1$ over
$\mvec{x} \in \reals^n$, with a standard normal $\mmat{A} \in \reals^{2n \times n}$, and
\texttt{factor\_cov} is FactorCovarianceModel with $n$ assets and
$\max(\lfloor n/100 \rfloor, 2)$ factors. In the \ac{DPP} variant, $\mvec{b}$ and $\lambda$,
or the risk aversion $\gamma$, are parameters. The non-\ac{DPP} variant writes $\lambda$ or
$\gamma$ as the product of two parameters, one of them equal to $1$, which \ac{DPP} forbids,
and the parameter-free variant makes them constants.

milliseconds, $18$ times faster than \texttt{CVXPY}.

\section{Ablation}
\label{sec:bench-ablation}

The branch of \texttt{CVXPY} used in Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme2} has two switches that turn parts
of \texttt{diffengine} off. The first stores every constant matrix in the sparse format, so
that no dense block is built from constant data. The second discards the engine's problem at
every parameter update and repeats the conversion and the symbolic pass, which removes the
split. Table~\ref{tab:app-nodense} reports the first switch, and the last column of
Table~\ref{tab:app-warm} the second. Their geometric means are given in
\S\ref{sec:bench-oneshot} and \S\ref{sec:bench-param}.

Without the dense blocks, the first canonicalization differs significantly on $23$ of the
$25$ problems, and it uses $0.85$ times the peak memory in geometric mean
(Table~\ref{tab:app-memory}). A dense block stores every entry, including the zeros of a
constant that is sparse in fact, such as the diagonal of ConvexPlasticity, whose peak memory
falls from $1\,330$ to $25.6$ megabytes without the dense blocks. Without them,
\texttt{diffengine} is significantly faster on $7$ problems, among them UnconstrainedQP by a
factor of $47$,
ConvexPlasticity by a factor of $5.1$ and CVaR by a factor of $1.6$.

\section{Sensitivity to size and density}
\label{sec:bench-sensitivity}

The problems of the suite have fixed sizes. To see how the comparison depends on the
characteristics of the problem, we vary them on the suite problems themselves. Each problem
fixes its sizes as literals in its source, and a factor $s$ multiplies these literals before
the problem is built, which multiplies the number of nonzeros of its data by about $s$ on most
problems. Nine
problems and the parameter-free \texttt{lasso} family of \S\ref{app:nondpp},
with $n = 1000$ at $s = 1$, are measured at $s \in \{1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4\}$
(Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity-size}), and all $25$ problems at $s \in \{1/2, 1, 2\}$. On the
five problems of Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity-density}, the data matrix is multiplied entrywise by
a random $0/1$ mask with a fraction $\rho \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1\}$ of ones, drawn from
its own generator so that the other data is unchanged. Every point is the median of five
runs, reported without a significance test.

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity-size} reports the ratio of \texttt{diffengine} to the best
\texttt{CVXPY} backend on the ten families measured at five sizes. On seven of them, the ratio
stays between $0.13$ and $0.22$ over a factor of $16$ in size, and on CVaR and
SemidefiniteProgramming between $0.55$ and $0.67$. On OptimalAdvertising it grows from $0.81$
to $3.40$. At the largest sizes, \texttt{diffengine} exceeds the memory limit on CVaR and
OptimalAdvertising.

On all $25$ problems (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity-size-all}), the ratio stays within $0.04$ of
its value at $s = 1$ on $18$ of the $21$ problems on which \texttt{diffengine} is faster. The
exceptions are Murray and QuantumHilbertMatrix, which are slower at $s = 1/2$, and CVaR, which
with QuantumHilbertMatrix exceeds the memory limit at $s = 2$. The four slower problems stay
slower at every size they complete, and the gap widens with the size on UnconstrainedQP,
OptimalAdvertising and ConvexPlasticity.

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity-density} reports the ratio as the density falls. On all five
problems, \texttt{diffengine}'s advantage shrinks, from a ratio of $0.14$ to $0.17$ on dense
data to a ratio between $0.40$ and $2.56$ at $\rho = 0.01$. The masked data stays a dense
array, so much of the dense block that the matrix product builds from it is zero.
Above a density of $20\,\%$, \texttt{diffengine} is faster on every problem.
Below it, it is slower on HuberRegression, SVMWithL1Regularization and LeastSquares at
$\rho = 0.05$, and on HuberRegression at $\rho = 0.01$. The ratio is not monotone, because
\texttt{diffengine} stores a constant factor of a matrix product with fewer than $5\,\%$
nonzeros in the sparse format. At $\rho = 0.01$ the masked matrix builds no dense block, and
the ratio is lower than at $\rho = 0.05$ on three of the five problems.
