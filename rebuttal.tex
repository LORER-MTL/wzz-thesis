\paragraph{A change of baseline.}
The version sent to the jury reported a $2.8\times$ geometric-mean speedup. That comparison
gave \texttt{diffengine} CVXPY PR~\#3524, which reassociates chains of matrix products ending
in a constant, while the other backends were timed on stock CVXPY. PR~\#3524 benefits every
backend, so all four are now timed without it. \texttt{diffengine} is the fastest of the four
on 21 of the 25 problems, significantly on all 21, with a geometric-mean ratio of $0.43$ to
the best CVXPY backend, about $2.3\times$. UnconstrainedQP is now $11.8\times$ slower
(§4.1 explains why, and gives the 16~ms it takes with PR~\#3524).

\section{Replies to the defence questions}

\question{1}{Why materialize dense blocks rather than leave them as operators, as in the
matrix-free approach of Diamond and Boyd? When is each approach preferable?}
The conic solvers that CVXPY calls by default factorize a matrix built from $A$ and $P$, so they
need $A$ and $P$ explicitly. Matrix-free modelling pays off for very large problems built on
fast transforms and solved by first-order methods. A permuted dense block lies between the two:
it is a materialized matrix, but it keeps the structure of the operator, namely its selectors
and its dense block. §2.2 now makes this comparison.

\question{2}{If the permuted dense representations are disabled, what fraction of the speedup
remains?}
Most of it. Without the dense blocks, \texttt{diffengine} is $1.07\times$ slower in geometric
mean, which is not significant over the suite (Wilcoxon signed-rank, $p = 0.07$), and it is
still $2.17\times$ faster than the best CVXPY backend. The split plays no part in a first
canonicalization, since each pass runs once. Most of the speedup therefore comes from the rest
of the implementation: C, one Jacobian per node over all the variables, no materialized views
and no tensor. The dense blocks make the engine $1.4$ to $3.0\times$ faster on ten problems
with dense data, but $47$ and $5.1\times$ slower on UnconstrainedQP and ConvexPlasticity, so
over the suite they are not a net gain (§4.1, App.~A.4).

\question{3}{How does re-solving behave on a non-DPP parametric problem?}
On the two families with a product of two parameters (\texttt{lasso} and
\texttt{factor\_cov}), the cached re-solve is $12$ to $13\times$ faster than CVXPY, which has
to recompile. ConvexPlasticity, the suite's only natural non-DPP parametric problem, re-solves
in 204~ms against 42~ms for CVXPY. The split is not the cause: 88\,\% of that re-solve
assembles the dense $3000 \times 3000$ block of $P$, built from a diagonal given as a dense
array. Without dense blocks, the same cached re-solve takes 2.3~ms, $18\times$ faster than
CVXPY. The split therefore carries over to a real non-DPP problem; what fails there is the
dense storage of a constant that is sparse in fact (§4.2, App.~A.3.1).

\question{4}{Why not re-evaluate only the subtrees that depend on the updated parameters?}
This is now implemented in the measured engine (SparseDiffEngine \#125): after a parameter
update, the numeric pass re-evaluates only the subtrees that contain a parameter (§3.4.1).
With it, the numeric pass takes 3 to 20\,\% of a re-solve on the five problems where the
tensor is faster. The rest is the conversion to compressed sparse columns and to the cone
order, which is now the future-work item (§5.2).

\question{5}{When does the global layout become a handicap relative to CVXPY's local layout, in
particular for memory?}
The global layout costs $O(\text{nodes} \times n)$ integers, so with many variables and deep
trees the local layout is better; this is now stated in the Limitations (§5.1).
OptimalAdvertising shows it in time: each of its 250 minimum terms has a Jacobian over all
250\,000 variables, and the symbolic pass visits every column (App.~A.2.1). Its peak memory
(6~GB, against 945~MB without dense blocks) likely comes from per-node caches and work arrays
sized by the number of variables. \confirm{the OptimalAdvertising memory cause; the profile was
of time, not memory}

\question{6}{What part of SparseDiffEngine pre-existed the thesis or was developed for DNLP, and
what part is specific to this work?}
SparseDiffEngine is joint work with Daniel Cederberg and serves both uses. DNLP differentiates
nonlinear atoms at every iterate; this thesis evaluates the affine atoms and the quadratic form
to obtain the problem data. Specific to the thesis are the \texttt{diffengine} backend, its
re-solve path and the benchmarks of Chapter~4 (§1.5, §3.1).

\question{7}{What is the strategy for BLAS and binary distribution of sparsediffpy?}
sparsediffpy is a compiled C extension, so wheels are still built per platform. It links
against the platform BLAS: Accelerate on macOS, and OpenBLAS on Linux through CMake's FindBLAS.
Unlike cvxcore, it needs no C++ toolchain and no Eigen. \confirm{the wheel strategy}

\section{Summary of the new results}

\paragraph{Methodology.}
Every measurement is repeated 20 times, each run in a fresh process, on an Apple M2 (24~GB,
macOS 26.5.1) with Accelerate, single-threaded. Each comparison is tested with a two-sided
Mann--Whitney test, Holm-adjusted over the problems (Ch.~4 intro).

\paragraph{First canonicalization.}
\texttt{diffengine} is the fastest backend on 21 of the 25 problems, with a geometric-mean
ratio of $0.43$ to the best CVXPY backend. Over the suite, that ratio has a bootstrap 95\,\%
confidence interval of $0.28$--$0.70$, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the log-ratios gives
$p = 0.003$. On every problem the runs of the two backends do not overlap, so every adjusted
$p$-value is $1.7 \times 10^{-6}$, the smallest attainable with 20 runs (§4.1, Table 4.1).

\paragraph{Ablation.}
Without dense blocks, the first canonicalization is $1.07\times$ slower in geometric mean
(confidence interval $0.71$--$1.47$, $p = 0.07$), and peak memory is $0.85\times$: a dense block
stores every entry, including the zeros of a constant that is sparse in fact. Without the
split, that is, repeating the conversion and the symbolic pass at every update, re-solves are
$2.56\times$ slower in geometric mean. Most of that cost is the Python-to-C conversion
(App.~A.4, Table A.7).

\paragraph{Non-DPP problems.}
See Q3 above (App.~A.3.1, Table A.6).

\paragraph{The four slower problems.}
App.~A.2.1 and Table A.4 give the phase shares and the mechanism for each.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item UnconstrainedQP (98\,\% numeric pass): a chain of matrix products is evaluated away
        from its constant and fills a dense Jacobian; CVXPY PR~\#3524 removes it (16~ms).
  \item OptimalAdvertising (90\,\% symbolic pass): the global layout, as in Q5.
  \item SDPSegfault1132 and ConvexPlasticity: dense constant data stored as dense blocks.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sensitivity to size and density.}
On seven of the ten families measured at five sizes, the ratio stays between $0.13$ and $0.22$
over a factor of 16 in size. On all 25 problems at three sizes, it stays within $0.04$ of its
value at the nominal size on 18 of the 21 problems where \texttt{diffengine} is faster. The four
slower problems stay slower at every size. As the density falls, the advantage shrinks, from a
ratio of $0.14$--$0.17$ on dense data to $0.40$--$2.56$ at 1\,\% density, and above 20\,\%
density \texttt{diffengine} is faster on every problem (App.~A.5, Tables A.8--A.10).

\paragraph{What the results imply.}
The dense blocks help when the data is dense and hurt when a constant that is sparse in fact is
stored dense. The backend already stores a constant factor sparse below 5\,\% nonzeros, but the
quadratic objective has no such test. Setting the threshold from the cost of the kernels and
applying it to the quadratic objective would remove the ConvexPlasticity and low-density
slowdowns; this is now listed as future work (§5.2).
