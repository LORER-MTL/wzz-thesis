\section*{Message au jury}

Bonjour chers membres du jury,

Je voulais tout d'abord vous remercier pour vos commentaires sur ma thèse. Après avoir reçu de
fortes objections sur la qualité des résultats, j'ai pris le temps de les rendre plus complets,
et j'ai aussi découvert quelques erreurs dans les affirmations que j'avais faites sur les
contributions.

Premièrement, il y a maintenant quatre problèmes qui sont plus lents. J'ai ajouté une section
dans la thèse pour les décrire (annexe A.2.1). Trois des quatre problèmes peuvent être beaucoup
plus rapides. Deux d'entre eux le deviennent avec des changements dans le «~frontend~» (par
frontend, je fais référence à la manipulation des arbres d'expression avant le calcul des
dérivées ; voir les pull requests \href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}{\#3524} et
\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3570}{\#3570}). Par exemple, pouvoir détecter que
\texttt{D @ ones} (où \texttt{D} est une matrice quelconque) équivaut à
\texttt{D.sum(axis=1)}, la somme sur les colonnes, est un grand avantage. Le troisième,
ConvexPlasticity, le devient avec un test de densité
(\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3571}{\#3571}). Ces changements ne règlent pas tout :
une partie du ralentissement vient du moteur lui-même, en particulier de la mémoire de la
disposition globale (question 5).

Deuxièmement, j'ai utilisé un ordinateur portable plus récent (MacBook M2 de 2022, avec
Apple Accelerate) pour cette série de résultats. Pour la première canonicalisation, je pense que les
ratios et les temps sont assez similaires. Mais pour les temps de re-résolution paramétrique, la
situation a changé : maintenant, seulement deux des problèmes sont plus rapides. J'ai modifié les
affirmations de ma conclusion et de la discussion des résultats pour dire seulement qu'il y a
des bénéfices, mais qu'il ne remplace pas encore le tenseur pour les re-résolutions.

Troisièmement, le problème UnconstrainedQP n'était pas réellement 100 fois plus rapide. C'était
dû à un changement de frontend
(\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}{\#3524}) que j'avais appliqué sur la branche
du backend diffengine, mais pas sur celles des autres. En l'appliquant à tous, cet
avantage disparaît. J'avoue que c'est une grave erreur de ma
part, et je l'ai corrigée. Je n'avais pas fait attention aux changements et aux versions du code
que j'exécutais pour mes résultats, et surtout, j'avais oublié que ce changement pouvait aussi
s'appliquer aux autres méthodes. J'ai fait de mon mieux pour faire preuve de diligence
raisonnable, afin que les résultats, cette fois-ci, n'aient pas de problème semblable.

À part ces trois points, qui sont les changements les plus sérieux et les plus importants, j'ai
aussi fait les études d'ablation et exécuté plus d'instances de benchmark, avec des mesures de
signification statistique, ce qui répond à tous vos commentaires.

Le reste de ce document a été rédigé par Claude à partir de beaucoup de mes indications, surtout
pour les réponses à vos questions.

\paragraph{A change of baseline.}
The version sent to the jury reported a $2.8\times$ geometric-mean speedup. Two things changed
since. All experiments were rerun on an Apple M2 with current versions. And that comparison gave
\texttt{diffengine} CVXPY PR~\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}{\#3524}, which reassociates
chains of matrix products ending in a constant, while the other backends were timed on stock
CVXPY. PR~\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}{\#3524} benefits every backend, so all four are now
timed without it. \texttt{diffengine} is the fastest of the four
on 21 of the 25 problems, significantly on all 21, with a geometric-mean ratio of $0.43$ to
the best CVXPY backend, about $2.3\times$. UnconstrainedQP is now $11.8\times$ slower
(§4.1 explains why, and gives the 16~ms it takes with
PR~\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}{\#3524}).

\section{Replies to the defence questions}

\question{1}{Why materialize dense blocks rather than leave them as operators, as in the
matrix-free approach of Diamond and Boyd? When is each approach preferable?}
The conic solvers that CVXPY calls by default factorize a matrix built from $A$ and $P$, so they
need $A$ and $P$ explicitly. Matrix-free modelling pays off for very large problems built on
fast transforms and solved by first-order methods. A permuted dense block lies between the two:
it is a materialized matrix, but it keeps the structure of the operator, namely its selectors
and its dense block. §2.2 now makes this comparison.

\question{2}{If the permuted dense representations are disabled, what fraction of the speedup
remains?}
Without the dense blocks, \texttt{diffengine} is $2.17\times$ faster than the best CVXPY
backend in geometric mean, against $2.33\times$ with them. The difference between the two,
$1.07\times$, is not significant over the suite (Wilcoxon signed-rank, $p = 0.07$). Per problem
the effect is large in both directions. On six problems with dense data, the dense blocks take
the engine from about $3$ to about $7\times$ faster than CVXPY. On UnconstrainedQP they take it
from $4\times$ faster to $11.8\times$ slower, and on ConvexPlasticity from $1.4$ to $6.9\times$
slower (§4.1, App.~A.4, Table A.7, which now gives both ratios to CVXPY). The split plays no
part in a first canonicalization, since each pass runs once. We do not attribute the remaining
speedup to one component: the engine is one design, and only the dense blocks and the split
have switches that can be turned off.

\question{3}{How does re-solving behave on a non-DPP parametric problem?}
On the two families with a product of two parameters (\texttt{lasso} and
\texttt{factor\_cov}), the cached re-solve is $12$ to $13\times$ faster than CVXPY, which has
to recompile. ConvexPlasticity, the suite's only natural non-DPP parametric problem, re-solves
in 204~ms against 42~ms for CVXPY. The split is not the cause: 88\,\% of that re-solve
assembles the dense $3000 \times 3000$ block of $P$, built from a diagonal given as a dense
array. Without dense blocks, the same cached re-solve takes 2.3~ms, $18\times$ faster than
CVXPY. The split therefore carries over to a real non-DPP problem; what fails there is the
dense storage of a constant that is sparse in fact (§4.2, App.~A.3.1).

\question{4}{Why not re-evaluate only the subtrees that depend on the updated parameters?}
This is now implemented in the measured engine (SparseDiffEngine \#125): after a parameter
update, the numeric pass re-evaluates only the subtrees that contain a parameter (§3.4.1).
With it, the numeric pass takes 3 to 20\,\% of a re-solve on the five problems where the
tensor is faster. The rest is the conversion to compressed sparse columns and to the cone
order, which is now the future-work item (§5.2).

\question{5}{When does the global layout become a handicap relative to CVXPY's local layout, in
particular for memory?}
In the global layout, each node also keeps work arrays sized by the total number of variables
$n$, whatever variables its own subtree contains. Their memory is therefore
$O(\text{nodes} \times n)$ integers, and with many variables and many nodes the local layout is
better; this is now stated in the Limitations (§5.1). OptimalAdvertising shows it in time: each
of its 250 minimum terms has a Jacobian over all 250\,000 variables, and the symbolic pass
visits every column (App.~A.2.1). Without dense blocks, its peak memory is 945~MB, against 173
to 197~MB for the CVXPY backends; that difference is the per-node cost.

With dense blocks it reaches 6~GB, and most of the difference comes from the aggregate
constraints $\mathbf{D}^\top \mathbf{1} \le \mathbf{t}$ and $\mathbf{D} \mathbf{1} \ge \mathbf{c}$.
Canonicalized alone, $\mathbf{D}^\top \mathbf{1} \le \mathbf{t}$ takes 4~GB with dense blocks and
112~MB without. Its Jacobian is stored as 1000 dense blocks of 250 entries, but each block also
keeps an inverse map of its columns with one integer per variable of the problem, 250\,000 of
them. This is the cost of the global layout again, paid once per dense block rather than once
per node. Storing these maps sparsely, or sharing one workspace across the blocks, should
remove most of it.

In OptimalAdvertising, both constraints are sums written as products with a vector of ones. A
small change to the CVXPY converter, submitted as pull request
\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3570}{\#3570}, lowers such products to
the engine's sum, which builds no dense blocks. On OptimalAdvertising it reduces the peak memory
from 6.1~GB to 1.4~GB and the canonicalization time from 1.10~s to 0.46~s (App.~A.2.1). These
are medians of three runs on CVXPY master, outside the measurements of Chapter~4, which are
unchanged. The remaining overhead is the
per-node cost above, and a weighted sum $\mathbf{D}\mathbf{w}$ with a general dense
$\mathbf{w}$ still needs the fix in the engine.

\question{6}{What part of SparseDiffEngine pre-existed the thesis or was developed for DNLP, and
what part is specific to this work?}
SparseDiffEngine is joint work with Daniel Cederberg and serves both uses. DNLP differentiates
nonlinear atoms at every iterate; this thesis evaluates the affine atoms and the quadratic form
to obtain the problem data. Specific to the thesis are the \texttt{diffengine} backend, its
re-solve path and the benchmarks of Chapter~4 (§1.5, §3.1).

\question{7}{What is the strategy for BLAS and binary distribution of sparsediffpy?}
cvxcore is a C++ extension, built with a copy of Eigen, that lives inside the CVXPY repository,
so CVXPY has to compile and ship it. sparsediffpy is a separate package: CVXPY only declares it
as a dependency and calls it, and the CVXPY side of the backend is much simpler. sparsediffpy is
still compiled, in C, and its own wheels are built for Linux, macOS and Windows and published on
PyPI. They link Accelerate on macOS and bundle OpenBLAS on Linux and Windows, so users install
nothing beyond the package.

\section{Summary of the new results}

\paragraph{Methodology.}
Every timed comparison is repeated 20 times, each run in a fresh process, on an Apple M2
(24~GB, macOS 26.5.1) with Accelerate, single-threaded, and tested with a two-sided
Mann--Whitney test, Holm-adjusted over the problems (Ch.~4 intro). The sensitivity points of
App.~A.5 are medians of five runs, reported without a test.

\paragraph{First canonicalization.}
\texttt{diffengine} is the fastest backend on 21 of the 25 problems, with a geometric-mean
ratio of $0.43$ to the best CVXPY backend. Over the suite, that ratio has a bootstrap 95\,\%
confidence interval of $0.28$--$0.70$, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the log-ratios gives
$p = 0.003$. On every problem the runs of the two backends do not overlap, so every adjusted
$p$-value is $1.7 \times 10^{-6}$, the smallest attainable with 20 runs (§4.1, Table 4.1).

\paragraph{Ablation.}
Without dense blocks, the first canonicalization is $1.07\times$ slower in geometric mean
(confidence interval $0.71$--$1.47$, $p = 0.07$), and peak memory is $0.85\times$: a dense block
stores every entry, including the zeros of a constant that is sparse in fact (App.~A.4,
Table A.7). The split needs no separate switch. Without it, a re-solve repeats the conversion
and the symbolic pass of the first canonicalization, so it cannot be faster, and Table 4.2
already gives the first canonicalization and the re-solve of every DPP problem. The
parameter-to-data tensor is a different mechanism, which can only be compared with the split on
re-solves, as Table 4.2 does.

\paragraph{Non-DPP problems.}
See Q3 above (App.~A.3.1, Table A.6).

\paragraph{The four slower problems.}
App.~A.2.1 and Table A.4 give the phase shares and the mechanism for each.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item UnconstrainedQP (98\,\% numeric pass): a chain of matrix products is evaluated away
        from its constant and fills a dense Jacobian; CVXPY
        PR~\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}{\#3524} removes it (16~ms).
  \item OptimalAdvertising (90\,\% symbolic pass): the global layout, as in Q5;
        PR~\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3570}{\#3570} removes the dense blocks of its aggregate
        constraints (App.~A.2.1).
  \item ConvexPlasticity: a diagonal given as a dense array becomes a dense block, which
        PR~\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3571}{\#3571} stores sparse (App.~A.2.1).
  \item SDPSegfault1132: three dense blocks of 49.5 million entries cancel in a sum with 1.01
        million nonzeros, and \texttt{diffengine} carries the cancelled entries through both
        passes. The cancellation depends on the values of the data, so it cannot be seen before
        the blocks are built.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sensitivity to size and density.}
On seven of the ten families measured at five sizes, the ratio stays between $0.13$ and $0.22$
over a factor of 16 in size. On all 25 problems at three sizes, it stays within $0.04$ of its
value at the nominal size on 18 of the 21 problems where \texttt{diffengine} is faster. The four
slower problems stay slower at every size they complete. As the density falls, the advantage shrinks, from a
ratio of $0.14$--$0.17$ on dense data to $0.40$--$2.56$ at 1\,\% density, and above 20\,\%
density \texttt{diffengine} is faster on every problem (App.~A.5, Tables A.8--A.10).

\paragraph{What the results imply.}
The dense blocks help when the data is dense and hurt when a constant that is sparse in fact is
stored dense. The backend already stores a constant factor of a matrix product sparse below
5\,\% nonzeros, but the quadratic objective had no such test. Pull request
\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3571}{\#3571} applies it there. On ConvexPlasticity,
whose diagonal is given as a dense array, it makes canonicalization 3 times faster (194 against
566~ms, medians of 10 runs on CVXPY master, outside the measurements of Chapter~4) with the same
problem data, as App.~A.2.1 now notes. Most of the rest of its slowdown, like the memory of OptimalAdvertising (Q5),
comes from the inverse column maps of the dense blocks in the engine. Setting the threshold from the cost
of the kernels, for the slowdowns at low density, remains future work (§5.2).
