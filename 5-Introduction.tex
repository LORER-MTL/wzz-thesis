\Chapter{INTRODUCTION}\label{sec:Introduction}

Since the early days of linear programming, practitioners of optimization
have needed to translate mathematical formulations into data that solvers can process.
At first, this meant encoding each coefficient of the problem matrices by hand, a process
that can both be tedious and error-prone~\cite{fourer1978modern}.
\emph{Matrix generators} were introduced in the late 1960s to automate this task: they compute the
coefficients through explicit loops and write them out in solver-readable formats like
MPS~\cite{marceau1971matrix, ibm1976mpsx}.

Matrix generators were able to remove most of the manual labor, but a gap still remained.
Modelers naturally think in terms of sets, indices, and algebraic expressions,
while procedural code constructs the coefficient matrices entry by entry.
This mismatch between how a model is expressed and how it is implemented made them difficult to
verify, modify, and communicate with others \cite{fourer1983modeling}.
\emph{Algebraic modeling languages} such as \texttt{AMPL} \cite{fourer1990ampl},
\texttt{AIMMS} \cite{bisschop2006aimms} and \texttt{GAMS} \cite{brooke1988gams} closed this gap by
accepting problem descriptions that closely follow the math.
The survey by Orchard-Hays \cite{orchard-hays1984history} gives a more complete overview
of the early history of modeling languages.

More recently, modeling languages embedded in general-purpose programming languages have
gained popularity. These include \texttt{Pyomo} \cite{bynum2021pyomo, hart2011pyomo} in Python,
\texttt{JuMP} \cite{dunning2017jump} in Julia, \texttt{CasADi} \cite{andersson2019} in C++, and
\texttt{YALMIP} \cite{lofberg2004yalmip} in MATLAB.
Other domain-specific modeling languages specialized for convex optimization \cite{boyd2004convex}
are built on \emph{disciplined convex programming} (DCP), the composition rules under which the
convexity of a problem can be verified automatically
\cite{grant2004disciplined, grant2006disciplined}.
These DCP-based languages include \texttt{CVX} \cite{cvx} in MATLAB, \texttt{CVXR} \cite{Fu_2020} in
R, \texttt{CVXPY} \cite{diamond2016cvxpy} in Python, and \texttt{Convex.jl}
\cite{udell2014convex} in Julia.

In the meantime, modern general purpose solvers \cite{mosekSolver, ge2022copt, clarabel} 
have mostly transitioned towards conic programming which generalizes linear programming and
can represent nearly every convex program used in practice \cite{bental2001lectures}.
The translation task remains a responsibility of the modeling languages which now target
conic problem data instead of LP coefficient matrices. In DCP-based languages, this
translation process is called \emph{canonicalization}.
While the time taken to solve an optimization problem is often dominated by the solver, the
time taken to \emph{canonicalize} (or \emph{compile}) it can also be significant.
The main goal of this thesis is to reduce the compilation time of \texttt{CVXPY}.

\section{Problem statement}\label{sec:problem-statement}

The standard form of a convex optimization problem is
\BEQ \label{e:parametric-family}
\begin{array}{ll}
\mbox{minimize}   & f_0(x; \theta) \\
\mbox{subject to} & f_i(x; \theta) \le 0, \quad i = 1,\ldots,m_1 \\
                  & g_j(x; \theta) = 0, \quad j = 1,\ldots,m_2
\end{array}
\EEQ
where $x \in \reals^n$ is the optimization variable, the objective $f_0$ and inequality constraints
$f_1,\ldots,f_{m_1}$ are convex and the equality constraints $g_1,\ldots,g_{m_2}$ are affine
\cite{boyd2004convex}.
The parameter $\theta \in \reals^p$ specifies data that can change between solves but is fixed
and given when solving an instance of the problem. We call the parametrized problem \eqref{e:parametric-family}
a problem \emph{family}; for a fixed value of $\theta$, we call it a problem \emph{instance}.

\paragraph{Quadratic conic form.}
Canonicalization converts the high-level description \eqref{e:parametric-family} to a
\emph{quadratic conic form} used by many general-purpose convex solvers
\BEQ \label{e:quad-conic-form}
\begin{array}{ll}
\mbox{minimize} & \frac{1}{2} x^T P(\theta) x + c(\theta)^T x\\
\mbox{subject to} & b(\theta)-A(\theta)x \in K
\end{array}
\EEQ
where the \emph{problem data} are the symmetric positive semidefinite matrix $P(\theta) \in \symm^n_+$,
the matrix $A(\theta) \in \reals^{m \times n}$, the vectors $b(\theta) \in \reals^m$ and
$c(\theta) \in \reals^n$ and the non-empty closed convex cone $K \subseteq \reals^m$.
Problem \eqref{e:quad-conic-form} generalizes both quadratic programs (QPs), when
$K = \reals^m_+$ is the nonnegative orthant, and standard conic programs, when $P(\theta) = 0$.

\paragraph{Canonicalization in \texttt{CVXPY}.}
Most of the cost of canonicalization lies in the final step, where symbolic
expression trees are converted to numerical problem data.
This step is often called \emph{matrix stuffing} or the \emph{backend} of canonicalization.
During the first solve of a problem family, \texttt{CVXPY} forms and caches a
\emph{parameter tensor} that maps the parameter vector to the problem data.
On subsequent solves, new problem data can be obtained with a single matrix-vector product
\cite{agrawal2019dpp}.

For large-scale problems, forming the parameter tensor can be prohibitively expensive, so
\texttt{CVXPY} lets users opt out of this option entirely.
Users must thus choose between paying a large upfront cost on the first solve and repeating
the full canonicalization, with nothing reused, at every solve.
We review \texttt{CVXPY} and the frontend of canonicalization in \S\ref{sec:intro-canon},
as well as the parameter tensor and matrix stuffing in \S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}.

\paragraph{Matrix stuffing as a derivative.}
The constraint matrix $A$ is the Jacobian of the stacked constraint expressions and $P$ is the
Hessian of the objective, so matrix stuffing amounts to computing a sparse Jacobian and Hessian.
Classical methods build these matrices column by column with multiple passes of algorithmic
differentiation (AD), using graph coloring to group columns that can be recovered in a single
pass \cite{curtis1974estimation, gebremedhin2005color}.
These methods treat the expression as a black box: the sparsity pattern must first be
detected and the columns grouped before a single entry of the matrix is obtained.
We review the basics of computing derivatives in \S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives} and present
sparse AD and graph coloring methods in \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}.

\paragraph{Other approaches.}
A modeling language, by contrast, knows which variables enter each constraint.
\texttt{AMPL} \cite{fourer1990ampl} is split in two components: its commercial
\emph{translator} writes the structure of the model to a \texttt{.nl} file
\cite{gay2005writing}, and the open source AMPL Solver Library (ASL) evaluates constraints
and their derivatives from this file inside the solver process \cite{gay1997hooking}.
\texttt{Pyomo} \cite{bynum2021pyomo} also interfaces to solvers through \texttt{.nl} files,
among other formats, though it generally writes them more slowly than the \texttt{AMPL}
translator.
On the other hand, \texttt{JuMP} \cite{dunning2017jump} has no single standard form to target.
The model is built constraint by constraint, and each constraint stores its coefficients directly,
so extraction reduces to copying them into the format of the chosen solver
\cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}.
We review both these modeling languages in \S\ref{sec:declared-structure}.

\paragraph{Our approach.}
We make use of the fact that the sparsity patterns of $A$ and $P$ are fixed across a problem
family, since the parameter $\theta$ changes the values of the problem data but not their
structure.
We can therefore split matrix stuffing in a symbolic and numeric phase.
The symbolic phase runs once on the first solve, where it computes the sparsity patterns,
allocates memory for all intermediate matrices, and caches other computations that remain valid
across the problem family.
These patterns are known analytically from the atoms, so nothing needs to be detected and no
graph coloring is needed.
The numeric phase then fills in new values at every solve.
This separation between structure and values is inspired by the interfaces of nonlinear
programming solvers such as \texttt{IPOPT} \cite{wachter2006ipopt}.

The symbolic phase is much cheaper to form and cache than the parameter tensor, and the
numeric phase is often similar in cost to applying the matrix-vector product.
The problem data is also assembled entirely in memory rather than through a separate model
file.
Finally, we exploit structure within the sparsity patterns themselves, which often contain dense
blocks of data.
When possible, we store these blocks as dense matrices and compute with dense linear algebra
kernels, rather than always relying on generic sparse formats and routines (we review dense and
sparse matrix computations in \S\ref{sec:intro-matrix}).

\section{Canonicalization}\label{sec:intro-canon}

The problem family \eqref{e:parametric-family} is written in \emph{functional} form: an objective and
constraint functions of the variable and the parameters.
In a modeling language, the optimization variables $x_1,\ldots,x_k$ are collected into a single
vector $x \in \reals^n$, and likewise the parameters $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_l$
into $\theta \in \reals^p$ \cite{boyd2004convex}.
Users are not required to write constraints with a zero right-hand side as in
\eqref{e:parametric-family}: modeling languages accept inequalities with a convex left-hand
side and a concave right-hand side, and equalities with both sides affine; moving every term
to one side recovers the form \eqref{e:parametric-family}.
Internally, each function is represented as an expression tree.

\paragraph{Atoms.}
Modeling languages for convex optimization are built on top of a library of functions which can be
used to describe problems. These functions are known as \emph{atoms} in \texttt{CVXPY} since they
are the fundamental building blocks of the modeling language \cite{grant2004disciplined}.
Alongside its numerical definition, each atom declares useful attributes such as 
its \emph{curvature}, \emph{monotonicity} with respect to each argument, and the
\emph{sign} of its output. A selection of common atoms is shown in \ref{tab:atoms}.

\paragraph{Expression trees.}
An expression is defined recursively as an atom evaluated at \emph{subexpressions}, each of which is
itself another expression. Such an expression is naturally stored as an \emph{expression tree},
with atoms at its internal nodes and variables, parameters, and constants at its leaves.
In Python, this tree can be formed automatically from user code through operator overloading.
Most operations on an expression, from evaluation to convexity verification to
differentiation, traverse the tree and combine the intermediate results at each atom.
\ref{fig:expr-tree} shows the tree of the affine expression $Ax - b$.

\paragraph{Disciplined convex programming.}
When $f_0$ and all $f_i$ are convex and all $g_j$ are affine,
\eqref{e:parametric-family} defines a convex optimization problem.
To certify convexity, DCP-based modeling languages use the DCP composition rules to classify
the curvature of each function \cite{grant2004disciplined}.
Once all functions are classified, a problem is \emph{DCP-compliant} if the objective and
each constraint have curvatures compatible with a convex problem: a convex objective,
convex $\leq$ concave inequalities, and affine equalities.

Otherwise, if a curvature is incompatible (\emph{e.g.}\ $f_0$ is concave) or cannot be
determined, the modeling language raises an error and terminates.
Because these rules are only sufficient for convexity, a problem that is genuinely convex
but not expressed in a recognized form may still be rejected.

\paragraph{Composition rules.}
The composition $\phi(f_1(x), f_2(x), \dots, f_k(x))$ of an atom $\phi$ with subexpressions $f_i$
is convex when $\phi$ is convex and, for each argument $i \in \{1, \dots, k\}$, one of the
following holds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\phi$ is non-decreasing in argument $i$ and $f_i$ is convex, or
    \item $\phi$ is non-increasing in argument $i$ and $f_i$ is concave, or
    \item $f_i$ is affine.
\end{itemize}
The same results hold for concave functions by swapping convex and concave.
Affine expressions require $\phi$ and all of its arguments to be affine \cite{grant2004disciplined}.

\subsection{Conic reformulation}\label{sec:bg-canonicalization}

\paragraph{Functional conic form.}
Once a problem is verified to be DCP-compliant, canonicalization
rewrites its functional form \eqref{e:parametric-family} into an equivalent \emph{functional conic form}
\BEQ\label{eq:conic-form}
\begin{array}{ll}
\mbox{minimize}   & f_0(x; \theta) \\
\mbox{subject to} & h_i(x; \theta) \in \mathcal K_i, \quad i = 1,\ldots,m,
\end{array}
\EEQ
where $f_0$ is affine or quadratic, each $h_i$ is affine, and each $\mathcal{K}_i$ is one of
the standard convex cones: the zero, nonnegative, second-order, power, exponential, and positive
semidefinite cones.

\paragraph{Conic reformulation.}
Every nonlinear atom is rewritten into conic constraints in three steps.
The first step rewrites the problem into \emph{Smith form}, where each
nonlinear atom is replaced by a new variable coupled with an equality constraint; every atom
then acts only on affine arguments \cite{smith1996}.
For a convex atom $\phi$, the Smith form gives an auxiliary variable $t$ with the equality
constraint $\phi(x) = t$. The second step relaxes this equality to the inequality $\phi(x) \le t$.
The DCP rules allow a convex atom only where its value is minimized or upper-bounded, so the solver
always prefers $t$ as small as possible; the relaxation is therefore \emph{lossless}.

The pair $(x, t)$ then lies in the \emph{epigraph} $\{(x, t) : \phi(x) \le t\}$ of $\phi$, so we
often refer to $t$ as an \emph{epigraph variable}.
A concave atom relaxes to $\phi(x) \ge t$ instead, giving a \emph{hypograph variable}.
The third and final step expresses each epigraph (hypograph) inequality as the membership of
an affine function of $(x, t)$ in a convex cone \cite{grant2008graph}.

\paragraph{Example of reformulations.}
A first example is the $\ell_2$ norm atom $\|x\|_2$, whose epigraph $\|x\|_2 \le t$ is exactly the
second-order cone constraint $(t, x) \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{soc}}$.
A second example is the nondifferentiable absolute value atom $|x|$, whose epigraph $|x| \le t$ can
be written smoothly as the pair of affine inequalities $-t \le x \le t$.
A final example is the sum of the $k$ largest entries of $x$, which expands into a small linear
program (LP): the epigraph $\mathrm{sum\_largest}(x, k) \le t$ holds if and only if there exist a
scalar $q$ and a vector $u$ such that
\BEQ\label{eq:sum-largest}
q\mathbf{1} + u \ge x, \qquad u \ge 0, \qquad kq + \mathbf{1}^\top u \le t.
\EEQ
Here $q$ acts as a threshold and each $u_i$ measures how far the entry $x_i$ rises above it, so that
the sum of the $k$ largest entries is bounded by $kq + \mathbf{1}^\top u$ \cite{boyd2004convex}.

\subsection{\texttt{CVXPY}}\label{sec:canonicalization}

\texttt{CVXPY} is a DCP-based modeling language embedded in Python \cite{diamond2016cvxpy},
which began in 2013 as an implementation of the modeling principles of \texttt{CVX} \cite{cvx}.
Three fundamental design decisions differed from \texttt{CVX} at the beginning.
The first is modularity: a few core data structures are shared by separate components for
verification, canonicalization, matrix stuffing, and solver interfaces.
The second is the expression tree as the central data structure, which is formed
automatically from user code through Python's operator overloading.
The third is support for parameters: since canonicalization never looks at the values of
constants, much of the compilation work can be reused when a problem is solved repeatedly.

These ideas matured with version 1.0, which redesigned canonicalization as
a general reduction-based rewriting system \cite{agrawal2018rewriting}.
The new design enabled the addition of other disciplined
grammars \cite{agrawal2019dgp, agrawal2020dqcp, cederberg2025disciplined}, and the early
parameter support grew into DPP, the restricted grammar
under which canonicalized programs can be updated efficiently \cite{agrawal2019dpp}.

The open source project has since grown through contributions from many other enthusiastic
developers; a recent paper surveys the new canonicalization and modeling features
introduced between versions 1.1 and 1.9 \cite{zhang2026cvxpy}.
The exposition of canonicalization in this chapter follows this survey, with more emphasis on
the non-DPP path.

\paragraph{Reductions.}
The rewriting from the functional form \eqref{e:parametric-family} to the functional conic form
\eqref{eq:conic-form} is carried out as a sequence of \emph{reductions}. Each reduction provides an
\texttt{apply} step that converts a problem into an equivalent one, and a \texttt{retrieve}
step that recovers a solution of the original problem from a solution of the rewritten one
\cite{agrawal2018rewriting}.

A first example is \texttt{FlipObjective}, which turns a maximization problem
into a minimization one by
flipping the sign of the objective; its \texttt{retrieve} step simply flips the sign back.

A second example, \texttt{CvxAttr2Constr}, handles variable attributes, which are properties
declared on a variable that restrict its domain. These include nonnegativity (\texttt{nonneg}),
positive semidefiniteness (\texttt{PSD}), and explicit variable bounds, amongst others.
When the target solver supports an attribute natively, \texttt{CVXPY} leaves it for the solver to
enforce;
otherwise the reduction creates a new variable that carries no attributes and adds
explicit constraints that enforce the attribute. Its \texttt{retrieve} step then maps the
reformulated solution back onto the original attributed variable.

\paragraph{The \texttt{Dcp2Cone} reduction.}
The main reduction, \texttt{Dcp2Cone}, applies the epigraph reformulation of
\S\ref{sec:bg-canonicalization} atom by atom: the problem is first rewritten into Smith form,
each resulting equality is relaxed to an epigraph (or hypograph) inequality, and each
inequality is finally expressed as a cone membership \cite{smith1996, grant2008graph}.
In practice the three steps are performed at once: each nonlinear atom is replaced directly by its
epigraph variable and cone constraints, and the intermediate Smith form and its relaxation
never appear explicitly.

\paragraph{Example of canonicalization.}
To show a concrete example of the frontend of canonicalization,
we consider a simple fitting with regularization problem
\BEQ
    \begin{array}{ll}
        \text{minimize} & \|Fx-g\|_2 + \mu \|x\|_2\\
        \text{subject to} & x \succeq 0\\
    \end{array}
      \label{eq:example}
\EEQ
with variable $x \in \reals^n$, fixed problem data $F\in \reals^{m\times n}$, and parameters
$g\in \reals^m$ and $\mu \geq 0$; the constraint $x \succeq 0$ requires every entry of $x$ to
be nonnegative.
The problem formulation in \texttt{CVXPY} code is
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import cvxpy as cp
import numpy as np
F = np.random.randn(m, n)
x = cp.Variable(n)
g = cp.Parameter(m)
mu = cp.Parameter(nonneg=True)
objective = cp.Minimize(cp.norm(F @ x - g) + mu * cp.norm(x))
prob = cp.Problem(objective, [x >= 0])
\end{lstlisting}
where we also use the numerical computation library \texttt{NumPy} \cite{numpy} for
the data arrays. The expression tree representation of the problem
is shown in \ref{fig:dcp-expression-tree}.

\paragraph{Conic form expression tree.}
After applying the reductions, the problem has been \emph{canonicalized} to
\BEQ\label{eq:graph}
\begin{array}{ll}
  \mbox{minimize}  & t_1 + \mu t_2 \\
  \mbox{subject to}  &
x \in \reals_+^n \\
& (t_1, Fx -g) \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{soc},m+1} \\
& (t_2, x) \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{soc},n+1} \\
\end{array}
\EEQ
where $t_1$ and $t_2$ are new epigraph variables, and the cone $\mathcal K$ from
\ref{e:quad-conic-form} is the product $\reals_+^n \times \mathcal K_{\mathrm{soc},m+1}
\times \mathcal K_{\mathrm{soc},n+1}$.
Problem \eqref{eq:graph} is now in the functional conic form of \S\ref{sec:bg-canonicalization}:
every constraint
is a cone membership, and the objective and constraint expressions are affine.
Comparing the DCP trees of \ref{fig:dcp-expression-tree} with the conic-form trees of
\ref{fig:conic-form-tree} shows the effect of canonicalization: the nonlinear norm atoms have been
replaced by epigraph variables, leaving affine expression trees in the variables.
The three rows of \eqref{eq:graph} remain three separate cone constraints, each carrying the
affine expressions that must lie in its cone; \ref{fig:conic-form-tree} accordingly shows one
expression tree per constraint, rather than a single combined tree.

\section{Computing derivatives}\label{sec:bg-derivatives}

There are many methods for computing the derivatives of a function, each with its own
trade-offs \cite{baydin2017ADsurvey}.
This section surveys the elementary ones; \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives} develops the
machinery for sparse derivatives and positions our approach among the systems that implement
it.

\paragraph{The Jacobian.}
For a vector-valued map $F : \reals^n \to \reals^m$ of an input $x \in \reals^n$, the first-order
derivative is the \emph{Jacobian} $J \in \reals^{m \times n}$ of partial derivatives of the outputs
with respect to the inputs,
\BEQ\label{eq:jacobian}
  J_{ij} = \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, m, \quad j = 1, \dots, n.
\EEQ
In this thesis, the map of interest stacks the affine constraint expressions of a canonicalized
problem into a single vector function of the variables; its Jacobian is the constraint matrix
$A$ of \eqref{e:quad-conic-form}.

\paragraph{The Hessian.}
For a scalar function $f : \reals^n \to \reals$, the second-order derivative is the \emph{Hessian}
$H \in \reals^{n \times n}$ of second partial derivatives,
\BEQ\label{eq:hessian}
  H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \, \partial x_j}, \qquad i, j = 1, \dots, n.
\EEQ
The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient of $f$, and is symmetric whenever $f$ is twice
continuously differentiable. It is therefore obtained by differentiating the gradient, with no method
beyond those used for the Jacobian.
Here the scalar function of interest is the canonicalized objective, and its Hessian is the
matrix $P$ of \eqref{e:quad-conic-form}.

\paragraph{Numerical differentiation.}
Other than computing derivatives by hand, the simplest method is to use \emph{numerical
differentiation}, which approximates the derivative of a function at a point by evaluating the
function at nearby points using a \emph{finite difference} formula such as
\BEQ
f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h},
\EEQ
where $h$ is a small step size (the central variant $f'(x) \approx (f(x+h) - f(x-h)) / 2h$
improves accuracy at the cost of one more evaluation).
The approach is simple but has two downsides.
First, it requires a linear number of function evaluations with respect to the number of
input variables, which is expensive for high-dimensional functions.
Second, it can easily lead to \emph{truncation errors} (when $h$ is too large) or
\emph{rounding errors} (when $h$ is too small) in the computed derivative if the step size is
not carefully chosen \cite{chakravarti1961truncation}.
Despite these downsides, numerical differentiation is still widely used in practice,
especially for verifying the correctness of derivative computations (also known as a
\emph{derivative checker}) \cite{wolfe1982checking}.

\paragraph{Symbolic differentiation.}
Mathematical expressions can be manipulated automatically using simple derivative rules of
calculus, such as the product and sum rule. This \emph{symbolic} approach to differentiation
is used in computer algebra systems such as \texttt{Mathematica} \cite{mathematica} and
\texttt{Maple}
\cite{maple} as well as the \texttt{SymPy} library in Python \cite{sympy2017}.
Unlike its numerical counterpart, symbolic differentiation tools compute derivatives as
closed-form expressions, which can be simplified and evaluated at any arbitrary point.
These closed-form expressions are particularly useful for deriving analytic solutions of
optimization problems, and can be further used in mathematical proofs and analyses.

However, a naive implementation of symbolic differentiation can quickly form exponentially
large symbolic expressions, which is impractical and inefficient for many problems
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
Interestingly, when intermediate expressions are stored as \emph{common subexpressions}, and
the expression is represented with a \emph{directed acyclic graph} (DAG) instead of a tree,
symbolic differentiation performs the same set of operations as \emph{reverse mode
differentiation} \cite{Laue2019OnTE}.

\paragraph{Algorithmic differentiation.}
\emph{Algorithmic differentiation} treats the function as a \emph{computational graph} of
elementary operations, each with a known local derivative rule.
The chain rule is then applied to combine these local derivatives throughout the graph, giving the
derivative of the whole function.
AD is also commonly referred to as \emph{automatic differentiation}; we prefer the term
\emph{algorithmic} to emphasize that AD differentiates the algorithm that computes a function,
rather than a numerical approximation or a symbolic formula \cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
In this broad sense, AD can refer to any method that applies the chain rule over a computational
graph; \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives} surveys the systems that implement it and the choices
that distinguish them.

\paragraph{Modes of differentiation.}
The chain rule can be applied in different orders, all giving the same result. The two best-known are
\emph{forward mode} (or \emph{tangent} mode), which propagates derivatives from the inputs
toward the outputs, and \emph{reverse mode} (or \emph{adjoint} mode), which propagates them from the
outputs back toward the inputs \cite{baydin2017ADsurvey}.
The two modes have asymmetric costs: in forward mode the work scales with the number of inputs, and
in reverse mode with the number of outputs.
Reverse mode is therefore preferred when there are far fewer outputs than inputs,
such as computing the gradient of a scalar function.
Other mixed-ordering modes exist, commonly grouped under the umbrella term \emph{cross-country}
elimination \cite{griewank2008evaluating}. However, finding the best ordering on a general graph is difficult,
so these modes are rarely used in practice \cite{naumann2008optimal}.

\section{Matrix computations}\label{sec:intro-matrix}

Matrix computations appear at every stage of this thesis.
The problem data $P$ and $A$ are sparse matrices, and matrix stuffing assembles them from
smaller intermediate matrices.
This section reviews the two families of matrix representations, dense and sparse, each from
the standpoint of both storage and algorithms.

\subsection{Dense matrices}\label{sec:bg-blas}

\paragraph{Dense storage.}
A dense matrix is stored as a single contiguous array of its entries, ordered row by row
(row-major) or column by column (column-major).
The position of an entry is computed directly from its indices, so no index arrays are
needed, and rows or columns are traversed sequentially in memory.

\paragraph{The BLAS.}
Dense linear algebra is served by a standardized interface of vector and matrix kernels, the
\emph{Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms} (BLAS). The interface is organized in three levels by
the ratio of computation to data: level 1 covers vector operations such as dot products and
scaled additions \cite{lawson1979blas}, level 2 covers matrix--vector products, and level 3
covers matrix--matrix products \cite{dongarra1990level3}. LAPACK, the library behind dense
factorizations and linear system solving in most scientific software, is built on top of these
kernels \cite{anderson1999lapack}. Because the interface is standard, decades of optimization
effort concentrate behind it: implementations such as \texttt{OpenBLAS}, Intel \texttt{MKL}, and
Apple \texttt{Accelerate}
are tuned to each processor family, and every program calling the interface benefits.
In practice, an optimized implementation is available on nearly every machine, whether
shipped with the operating system or bundled by numerical libraries.

\paragraph{Why dense kernels are fast.}
The performance of optimized BLAS implementations comes from working with, rather than against,
the memory system. A modern processor multiplies numbers far faster than it loads them from
main memory, so fast code must reuse data already sitting in its caches and must read memory
contiguously. Dense storage makes both possible: optimized kernels traverse arrays in memory
order, process several entries per instruction with vector (SIMD) units, and tile
matrix--matrix products into blocks that fit in cache \cite{goto2008anatomy}. Level-3 kernels
benefit the most: they perform $O(n^3)$ operations on $O(n^2)$ data, so each entry loaded from
memory is reused many times.

These speeds, however, rely on every entry being stored and processed, zeros included.

\subsection{Sparse matrices}\label{sec:bg-sparse}

In optimization problems, the matrices $P$ and $A$ contain mostly zero entries; such
matrices are called \emph{sparse}. Storing only the nonzero entries makes memory and work
scale with their number rather than with the product of the dimensions.
The simplest sparse matrix data structure is the \emph{coordinate} or \emph{triplet}
format. It consists of two integer index arrays \texttt{rows} and \texttt{cols} and a floating point
value array \texttt{vals} of length equal to the number of \emph{nonzero entries} in the matrix.

For example, the matrix
\BEQ\label{eq:sparse-matrix}
  S = \begin{bmatrix}
  0 & 1 & 0 & 0\\
  2 & 0 & 3 & 4\\
  0 & 5 & 6 & 0\\
  \end{bmatrix}
\EEQ
can be represented in coordinate format as
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
int    rows[] = {0,  1,  1,  1,  2,  2};
int    cols[] = {1,  0,  2,  3,  1,  2};
double vals[] = {1,  2,  3,  4,  5,  6};
\end{lstlisting}
where we use zero-based indexing and array declaration notation from the C programming
language \cite{Kernighan1988}.
The coordinate format is the easiest to understand and form, but it is not effective for most
operations on sparse matrices; the \emph{compressed sparse row} or CSR format is commonly
used instead.

\paragraph{Compressed sparse row format.}
The CSR format differs from the coordinate format in that it replaces the \texttt{rows} array with
a row pointer array \texttt{idxptr} of length equal to the number of rows plus one.
The row pointer array indicates the starting index of each row in the \texttt{cols} arrays.
For example, the matrix in \eqref{eq:sparse-matrix}
can be represented in CSR format as
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
int  idxptr[] = {0,  1,          4,      6};
int    cols[] = {1,  0,  2,  3,  1,  2};
double vals[] = {1,  2,  3,  4,  5,  6};
\end{lstlisting}
where we deliberately format \texttt{idxptr} to align with the start of each
row in the \texttt{cols} arrays.
The benefits of the CSR format are more than a reduced size of the \texttt{idxptr} array
compared to the \texttt{rows} array.
In the CSR format, accessing a row takes constant time and is very \emph{cache-friendly}
since the nonzero entries are stored contiguously in memory.
For this reason, the CSR format is the default for \emph{iterative methods}
to solve sparse linear systems \cite{saad2003iterative}.

\paragraph{Compressed sparse column format.}
The \emph{compressed sparse column} or CSC format applies the same compression to the
columns instead: \texttt{idxptr} now points into a \texttt{rows} array, and the nonzero
entries are stored column by column.
The CSC arrays of a matrix read exactly as the CSR arrays of its transpose, so transposition
is a conversion between the two formats, computable without sorting in one counting pass over
the nonzeros \cite{gustavson1978two}.
Accessing a column of a CSR matrix instead requires a scan of every row, so algorithms
that traverse columns prefer CSC; direct methods for sparse linear systems are the
standard example \cite{davis2006direct}.

\paragraph{Choice of format.}
The access pattern of the algorithm decides: a computation that traverses rows wants CSR, one
that traverses columns wants CSC. Since conversion costs a single counting pass, keeping the
same matrix in both formats is common. The engine developed in this thesis (\S\ref{sec:Theme1})
does exactly this, storing Jacobians in CSR and pairing the two formats in its matrix products.

\paragraph{Matrix-vector products.}
Both compressed formats multiply a dense vector in one pass over the nonzeros, but the loop
must follow the storage: CSR \emph{gathers}, computing each output entry as a sparse dot
product along its row, while CSC \emph{scatters}, accumulating each column's contribution into
the output vector.

\paragraph{Matrix-matrix products.}
The product of two sparse matrices is harder than the operations above because the sparsity
pattern of the output is not known in advance. Implementations therefore split the work in two
phases: a \emph{symbolic} phase determines the pattern and allocates the output, and a
\emph{numeric} phase fills its values \cite{gustavson1978two, bank1993smmp}. The split rewards
reuse, and the engine of \S\ref{sec:Theme1} is built around it: the symbolic phase runs once per
problem family and only the numeric phase repeats.

\paragraph{Other formats.}
The coordinate, CSR and CSC formats are the most widely used sparse matrix formats,
but many others exist for different specialized use cases.
The scientific Python sparse library \texttt{scipy.sparse} \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth}
allows users to form other types of sparse matrices such as the list of lists (LIL) matrix,
the dictionary of keys (DOK) matrix, and the block-sparse row (BSR) matrix.

The standard references develop these data structures for solving sparse linear systems, a
task this thesis never performs, but they apply unchanged \cite{davis2006direct,
Duff1986Direct, gill1984sparse}; Gustavson's paper \cite{gustavson1978two} is the origin of
the two-phase product and the sort-free transposition used by the engine.
