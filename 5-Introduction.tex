\Chapter{INTRODUCTION}\label{sec:Introduction}

Domain specific modeling languages for convex optimization such as CVXPY \cite{diamond2016cvxpy} and
YALMIP \cite{lofberg2004yalmip} allow users to formulate optimization problems in a natural
mathematical syntax. Through a process called \emph{canonicalization}, these high-level
problem descriptions are converted to a \emph{standard conic form} used by many
general-purpose convex solvers
\BEQ \label{e:standard-form}
\begin{array}{ll}
\mbox{minimize} & c^T x + d\\
\mbox{subject to} & b-Ax \in K
\end{array}
\EEQ
where $x \in \reals^n$ is the decision variables,
and the \emph{problem data} are the matrix $A \in \reals^{m \times n}$, the vectors $b \in \reals^m$
and $c \in \reals^n$, the scalar $d \in \reals$ and the non-empty closed convex cone
$K \subseteq \reals^m$.
Almost all of optimization practice can be divided into two groups, \emph{modeling} and
\emph{solving}. The modeler's role is to formulate problems based on real-world business
or engineering applications.
The solver's role is to find an optimal solution given the data of a problem instance.
Modeling languages serve as the communication bridge between these two groups.
When improvements in solvers are made, modeling languages must adapt to allow users
to benefit from them. Conversely, it is through natural formulations that modelers can
express their requirements and help motivate solver improvements.

While the time taken to solve an optimization problem is often dominated by the solver,
the time taken to \emph{canonicalize} (or \emph{compile}) a problem can also be significant,
especially for large problems or ones that are solved many times.
In the case of CVXPY, this compilation cost lies largely in \emph{matrix stuffing},
the final step of canonicalization where symbolic expression trees are converted to
the explicit numerical problem data $A$, $b$, $c$ and $d$.
Other modeling languages take different approaches to assemble this data; we review these
\emph{coefficient extraction} approaches in \S\ref{sec:coefficient-extraction}.

\paragraph{Parameters.}
A modeler can designate part of a problem's data as \emph{parameters}---values that vary between
solves while the problem's structure remains fixed. A parameterized model is therefore solved many
times, once for each setting of its parameters, so the cost of canonicalization is paid on every
solve rather than only once. Re-forming this data each time, when only the parameter values have
changed, is the cost this thesis sets out to eliminate.

\paragraph{Expression trees.}
Modeling languages for convex optimization are built on top of a library of functions which can be
used to describe problems. These functions are known as \emph{atoms} in CVXPY since they are the
fundamental building blocks of the modeling language \cite{grant2004disciplined}.
An expression is recursively defined as an atom evaluated at a \emph{subexpression}, which can
itself be a variable, a parameter, or another expression. Every formulation is therefore an
\emph{expression tree}, or more generally a \emph{computational graph}, of atoms.
After canonicalization this graph is purely \emph{affine} in the variables and parameters, and
matrix stuffing is the act of evaluating it into the problem data.

\paragraph{Related work.}
Every modeling language must turn a symbolic model into the numerical problem data a solver
consumes, and they differ both in how they extract it and in how they handle data that changes
between solves. JuMP scalarizes each constraint eagerly as the model is built; it supports parameters through a
dedicated set, but updating them efficiently relies on a separate package
(\texttt{ParametricOptInterface.jl}) or on native solver support, rather than a cached
parameter-to-data map. CasADi differentiates its expression
graph into a reusable function whose parameters are ordinary numeric inputs, re-evaluated on each
solve. CVXPY exposes parameters explicitly and caches a reusable map from them to the problem data
(\S\ref{sec:coefficient-extraction}).

Because the canonicalized model is affine, forming its problem data is at heart a derivative
computation: the constraint matrix is a Jacobian, and the matrix of a quadratic objective is a
Hessian. Coefficient extraction is thus part of the broader problem of computing sparse derivatives.
Such derivatives are classically computed one scalar at a time, using graph coloring to recover
their sparsity; only recently have they been computed directly on vectorized graphs, in statistics
\cite{kwok2025vectorised} and control \cite{zou2025vectorized} (\S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}).
This direct, vectorized view has not yet been brought to convex canonicalization, which is the gap
this thesis addresses.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A structured differentiation package.} We build \texttt{SparseDiffengine}, which
  computes sparse Jacobians and Hessians of vectorized expressions directly, with no graph coloring,
  separating their fixed sparsity pattern from their changing values.
  \item \textbf{A CVXPY backend.} We implement a canonicalization backend for CVXPY using the
  \texttt{SparseDiffengine}, finding the sparsity pattern once and refilling only its values on each
  solve.
  \item \textbf{A portable C implementation.} \texttt{SparseDiffengine} is written in
  dependency-light C with Python bindings, making the engine usable beyond CVXPY and well suited to
  embedded and real-time settings.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Thesis outline.}
The rest of this thesis begins with a review of the literature on modeling languages,
canonicalization, and computing derivatives in \S\ref{sec:RevLitt}.
In \S\ref{sec:Theme1} we present \texttt{SparseDiffengine}, a package for structured sparse
differentiation, and the affine atoms it uses to canonicalize a problem into its sparse Jacobian and
Hessian.
In \S\ref{sec:Theme2} we present benchmark results comparing our method
to existing approaches.
Finally, in \S\ref{sec:Conclusion} we summarize our contributions and
discuss future improvements to the canonicalization backend.
