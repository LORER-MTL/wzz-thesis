\section*{Message au jury}

Bonjour chers membres du jury,

Je voulais tout d'abord vous remercier pour vos commentaires sur mon mémoire. Comme vous m'avez
demandé de clarifier et d'approfondir plusieurs points des résultats, j'ai pris le temps de les
rendre plus complets, et j'ai aussi découvert quelques erreurs dans les affirmations que j'avais
faites sur les contributions.

Premièrement, il y a maintenant quatre problèmes qui sont plus lents. J'explique la cause de
chacun au chapitre~4 (\S\ref{T-sec:bench-oneshot}), avec la part de chaque phase du temps
(tableau~\ref{T-tab:phases}), et je donne le détail à la section~\ref{sec:results}. Pour deux d'entre eux, l'écart
disparaît presque ou s'inverse avec des changements dans le «~frontend~» (par frontend, je fais
référence à la manipulation des arbres d'expression avant le calcul des dérivées) :
UnconstrainedQP passe à $16$~ms contre $13$~ms pour \texttt{CVXPY}, avec le même changement pour
tous les backends, et OptimalAdvertising devient plus rapide ($0{,}33$~s contre $0{,}53$~s). Par exemple, pouvoir détecter que
\texttt{D @ ones} (où \texttt{D} est une matrice quelconque) équivaut à \texttt{D.sum(axis=1)},
la somme sur les colonnes, est un grand avantage. Un troisième,
ConvexPlasticity, devient $2{,}5$ fois plus rapide avec un test de densité, mais reste plus lent
que \texttt{CVXPY}. Ces changements ne règlent pas tout : une partie du ralentissement vient de
\texttt{SparseDiffEngine} lui-même (voir Q5).

Deuxièmement, j'ai refait toutes les mesures sur un ordinateur plus récent (MacBook M2 de 2022,
avec Apple \texttt{Accelerate}), plutôt que sur l'ordinateur Intel de 2018 de la version
soumise, pour que les résultats soient reproductibles sur du matériel actuel, comme celui de la
plupart des utilisateurs de \texttt{CVXPY}. Pour la première canonicalisation, \texttt{diffengine} reste le
plus rapide sur $21$ des $25$ problèmes, mais l'accélération moyenne passe de $2{,}8$ à
$2{,}3$ fois, en partie à cause d'UnconstrainedQP (troisième point). Pour les re-résolutions paramétriques, en revanche, \texttt{diffengine}
n'est plus rapide que sur trois des sept problèmes, contre cinq dans la version soumise ; sur
les quatre autres, la plus grande partie d'une re-résolution est du post-traitement, après
\texttt{SparseDiffEngine}. J'ai donc modifié ma conclusion et la discussion des résultats pour
dire seulement qu'il y a des bénéfices, mais que la séparation ne remplace pas encore le tenseur
pour les re-résolutions. Réduire ce post-traitement est maintenant le premier des travaux
futurs.

Troisièmement, le problème UnconstrainedQP n'était pas réellement 100 fois plus rapide. C'était
dû à un changement de frontend que j'avais appliqué sur la branche du backend
\texttt{diffengine}, mais pas sur celles des autres. En l'appliquant à tous, cet avantage
disparaît. Ceci a été rectifié : aucun backend n'utilise maintenant ce changement.

À part ces trois points, qui sont les changements majeurs, j'ai aussi fait les études
d'ablation et exécuté plus d'instances de benchmark, avec des mesures de signification
statistique, ce qui répond à tous vos commentaires.

\section{Summary of corrections}

\paragraph{Main findings.} The new measurements answer the committee's comments as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item The dense blocks help only on problems with dense data. They slow down the three problems
  that build a dense block from data that is mostly zeros, and change little on the others.
\item Over the suite, the speedup remains almost the same without the dense blocks.
\item Without the symbolic and numeric split, a re-solve repeats the conversion and the symbolic
  pass and is $2.9$ times slower.
\item The speedup holds across sizes, except on three of the slower problems. Where
  \texttt{diffengine} runs out of memory and \texttt{CVXPY} does not, the dense blocks are
  usually the cause.
\item The dense blocks pay off only when about half or more of the entries are nonzero.
\item Each of the four slower problems has an identified cause, and three of them improve with a
  change made before the engine.
\end{itemize}

\begin{thesisquote}{J2, (i): \tsec{sec:ms-parameters}, \texttt{CVXcanon} and \texttt{CVXGEN}}
the C++ backend \texttt{cvxcore} keeps one sparse matrix per parameter slice and loops over the
slices at every operator. It began as \texttt{CVXcanon}, a C++ library written to replace the
Python matrix stuffing of early versions of \texttt{CVXPY}, and was renamed \texttt{cvxcore} in
2018. [\ldots] \texttt{CVXPYgen} follows the earlier code generator \texttt{CVXGEN}, which
generates a custom C solver for a family of small quadratic programs.
\end{thesisquote}
\begin{thesisquote}{J2, (i): \tsec{sec:ampl}, \texttt{Pyomo}}
\texttt{Pyomo}, which also writes~\texttt{.nl} files, avoids a new file per parameter change
for solvers with an in-memory interface: its persistent solver interfaces keep the model inside
the solver and update the changed parameters and constraints in place.
\end{thesisquote}
\begin{thesisquote}{J2, (i): \tsec{sec:jump}, \texttt{Convex.jl}}
\texttt{Convex.jl}, the \ac{DCP} language of the Julia ecosystem, also targets MOI. Since
version~0.16, it represents every affine expression as a sparse matrix and an offset over the
solver variables, accumulated as the problem is converted to MOI. Like the backends of
\texttt{CVXPY}, it stores the coefficients in a general-purpose sparse format.
\end{thesisquote}
\begin{thesisquote}{J2, (i): \tsec{sec:theme1-matrix-representations}, block-sparse antecedents}
Keeping dense blocks inside a sparse matrix is not a new idea. Finite-element codes assemble
dense element matrices into a sparse global matrix, and nonlinear least-squares libraries such as
\texttt{Ceres} and \texttt{g2o} store the block-sparse Jacobians of bundle adjustment as dense
blocks. In those codes, the position of each block follows from the structure of the problem,
one element or one residual at a time. In our representations, the symbolic pass derives it from
the expression tree.
\end{thesisquote}
\begin{thesisquote}{J2, (i): \tsec{sec:problem-statement}, the symbolic and numeric split}
This separation between structure and values goes back to sparse direct solvers, which analyse
the sparsity pattern of a matrix once and repeat the numeric factorization for new values, and
to Gustavson's sparse product.
\end{thesisquote}
\begin{thesisquote}{J1a, J1c, (iv): Chapter~\ref{T-sec:Theme2}, Statistics}
Each measurement is made in $30$ runs, each in a fresh process, and on a parametric problem
each run uses new parameter values. The tables report the mean and the standard deviation of the
runs, and the ratios are ratios of medians. On each problem, the runs of \texttt{diffengine} and
of the other backend are compared with a two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ test, with the $p$-values
adjusted over the problems by the Holm--Bonferroni method. Over the suite, we test whether the
geometric mean of the ratios differs from $1$ with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on their
logarithms. [\ldots] Every ratio
in the tables of this chapter is significant unless its caption says otherwise.
\end{thesisquote}
\begin{thesisquote}{(vii): Chapter~\ref{T-sec:Theme2}, Setup}
Before any timing, we check that \texttt{diffengine} returns the same problem data as
\texttt{CPP}, to within $10^{-9}$ of the largest entry, at the first canonicalization and after
a parameter update. [Footnote:] Except
for one NaN entry that comes from the data of OptimalAdvertising itself.
\end{thesisquote}
\begin{thesisquote}{Point 6: \tsec{sec:theme1-kernel-dispatch}, complexity}
A permuted dense matrix with an $m_0 \times n_0$ block stores $m_0 n_0$ values and $m_0 + n_0$
indices, against $m_0 n_0$ values and $m_0 n_0 + m + 1$ indices for the same block in \ac{CSR}
form, where $m$ is the number of rows of the whole matrix. For~(3.2), [\ldots] the symbolic pass
costs $O(m_A + m_B)$ and the numeric pass performs $n_B\, s\, n_A$ multiply-adds in one
\texttt{dgemm}, whereas Gustavson's algorithm performs the same multiply-adds after a symbolic
pass of $O(n_B\, s\, n_A)$.
\end{thesisquote}

\section{Replies to the defence questions}\label{sec:replies}

\question{1}{Why materialize dense blocks rather than leave them as operators, as in the
matrix-free approach of Diamond and Boyd~\cite{diamond2016matrixfree}? When is each approach
preferable?}
Because the solvers that \texttt{CVXPY} calls need the matrices. Its default solvers are
\texttt{Clarabel}, an interior-point method, and \texttt{OSQP} and \texttt{SCS}, which are
first-order methods; commercial solvers such as \texttt{MOSEK} and \texttt{Gurobi} are
interior-point or simplex methods. All of them factorize a matrix built from $\mmat{A}$ and
$\mmat{P}$: an interior-point method factorizes a KKT system at every iteration, and
\texttt{OSQP} and \texttt{SCS}, in their default direct mode, factorize their KKT matrix and
reuse the factorization across iterations, refactorizing only when a step-size parameter changes. So every one of them needs $\mmat{A}$ and $\mmat{P}$ explicitly,
whatever its algorithm. Matrix-free modelling pays off for very large problems built from fast
transforms, solved by first-order methods that only need products with $\mmat{A}$ and
$\mmat{A}^\top$, such as the indirect mode of \texttt{SCS}. A permuted dense block lies between
the two: it is a materialized matrix, but it keeps the structure of the operator, namely its
row and column selectors and its dense block.

\begin{thesisquote}{\tref{sec:computing-derivatives}}
In convex optimization, Diamond and Boyd take the matrix-free view. Instead of forming
$\mmat{A}$, they represent the problem as a graph of linear operators, available only through
their forward and adjoint products, which suits first-order solvers. The solvers that
\texttt{CVXPY} calls by default instead factorize a matrix built from $\mmat{A}$ and $\mmat{P}$.
The matrix-free approach pays off on very large problems built from fast transforms, such as
convolutions and Fourier transforms, and it needs solvers that accept operators instead of
matrices. The permuted dense matrices of \tsec{sec:theme1-matrix-representations} sit between the two: they form the matrix that
the solver factorizes, but keep the structure of the operator, its row and column selectors and
its dense block.
\end{thesisquote}

\question{2}{If the permuted dense representations are disabled, what fraction of the speedup
remains?}
Most of it. A switch of our \texttt{CVXPY} branch stores every constant matrix in the sparse
format. With it, \texttt{diffengine} is $2.15$ times faster than the best \texttt{CVXPY} backend
over the suite, against $2.29$ times with the dense blocks, and the difference is not
significant ($p = 0.07$). Per problem, the dense blocks make \texttt{diffengine} $1.2$ to $3$
times faster on twelve problems, and $1.4$ to $46$ times slower on CVaR, ConvexPlasticity and
UnconstrainedQP, which build a dense block from data that is mostly zeros
(Table~\ref{tab:sup-nodense}). Without them, \texttt{diffengine} also uses $0.83$ times the peak
memory in geometric mean (Table~\ref{tab:sup-memory}).
The thesis states these results in \tsec{sec:bench-ablation}.

The split between the symbolic and numeric passes can be turned off on re-solves only; without
it, a re-solve is $2.9$ times slower, significantly on all seven problems
(Table~\ref{tab:res-ablation}).

\question{3}{How does re-solving behave on a non-DPP parametric problem, for instance with a
product of two parameters or a parameter in a non-affine position?}
On the two families written with a product of two parameters, which \ac{DPP} forbids,
\texttt{CVXPY} has to canonicalize the problem again at every solve, while \texttt{diffengine}
keeps its symbolic pass and reruns only the numeric pass. It re-solves as fast as on the
\ac{DPP} variant, $13$ to $14\times$ faster than \texttt{CVXPY} on \texttt{lasso} and
\texttt{factor\_cov}, significantly on both (Table~\ref{tab:res-nondpp}). ConvexPlasticity, the suite's only natural non-DPP parametric problem, breaks \ac{DPP} for the
second reason: its parameters lie inside the argument of a quadratic form, which \texttt{CVXPY}
requires to be parameter-free for a solver that accepts a quadratic objective. It is the
exception: it re-solves in $207$~ms
against $42$~ms for \texttt{CVXPY}, because $88\,\%$ of the re-solve assembles a dense $3{,}000
\times 3{,}000$ block of $\mmat{P}$ built from a diagonal. Without the dense blocks, it re-solves
in $2.0$~ms, $21\times$ faster than \texttt{CVXPY}. The thesis no longer claims that the split
replaces the tensor (Q4).

\question{4}{Why not re-evaluate only the subtrees that depend on the updated parameters?}
The engine now does this: after a parameter update, the numeric pass re-evaluates only the
subtrees that contain a parameter, and keeps the cached values of the others. All re-solve
times in the thesis use it.

The prediction of the examiner report, that this would make \texttt{diffengine} at least as fast as the tensor in every

\question{5}{When does the global layout become a handicap relative to \texttt{CVXPY}'s local
layout, in particular for memory?}
When the problem has many variables and many nodes. In the global layout, each node keeps,
besides its Jacobian, integer work arrays of length $n$, the total number of variables, whatever
variables its own subtree contains. Their memory is therefore $O(\text{nodes} \times n)$
integers. In \texttt{CVXPY}'s local layout, each node stores blocks only for the variables it
depends on, so the same overhead is proportional to the sum, over the nodes, of the number of
variables each one depends on. The two coincide when every node depends on every variable, and
the local layout wins when most nodes depend on a few variables out of many.

OptimalAdvertising shows it. Each of its $250$ minimum terms depends on one row of a
$250 \times 1{,}000$ variable, but has a Jacobian over all $250{,}000$ variables. Without the dense
blocks, its peak memory is $945$~MB, against $173$ to $197$~MB for the \texttt{CVXPY} backends.
With them it reaches $6.1$~GB, because each dense block of the aggregate constraints
$\mmat{D}^\top \ones \le \mvec{t}$ and $\mmat{D} \ones \ge \mvec{c}$ also keeps one integer per
variable of the problem: the same cost, paid per block rather than per node. Rewriting these
products with a vector of ones as sums avoids the dense blocks: OptimalAdvertising then
canonicalizes in $0.33$~s instead of $0.91$~s, faster than the $0.53$~s of \texttt{CVXPY}, with
$1.4$~GB instead of $6.1$~GB.


\question{6}{What part of \texttt{SparseDiffEngine} pre-existed the thesis or was developed for
DNLP, and what part is specific to this work?}
\texttt{SparseDiffEngine} is joint work with Daniel Cederberg. It was first built as the
differentiation engine of the \texttt{DNLP} extension of
\texttt{CVXPY}~\cite{cederberg2025disciplined}, which differentiates nonlinear atoms at every
iterate of the solver.
The version sent to the committee should have said so clearly; it
now does. This thesis uses the engine for a different
purpose, canonicalization: it evaluates the affine atoms and the quadratic form at the origin to
obtain the problem data. What is specific to the thesis is the interface to \texttt{CVXPY},
namely the \texttt{diffengine} backend and its re-solve path for parametric problems, and the
benchmarks of Chapter~4.

\question{7}{What is the strategy for \ac{BLAS} and binary distribution of
\texttt{sparsediffpy}?}
\texttt{cvxcore} is a C++ extension that lives inside the \texttt{CVXPY} repository, so
\texttt{CVXPY} has to compile and ship it. \texttt{sparsediffpy} is a separate package that
\texttt{CVXPY} only depends on. Its compiled wheels for Linux, macOS and Windows are published
on \texttt{PyPI}; they link Apple's \texttt{Accelerate} on macOS and bundle \texttt{OpenBLAS}
elsewhere, so users install nothing beyond the package. The binary does not go away, but it is
built and released independently of \texttt{CVXPY}, like its other compiled dependencies, which
is what the removal of \texttt{cvxcore} aims at.

\end{table}

\paragraph{UnconstrainedQP.} The problem minimizes
$\lVert \mmat{F}^{\mathsf{H}} \mmat{E}(\mvec{z}) \mmat{F} \mvec{x} - \mvec{y} \rVert_2$, with
$\mmat{E}(\mvec{z}) = \mmat{I}_{14} \otimes \diag(\mvec{z})$, over $\mvec{z} \in \complex^{18}$,
where $\mmat{F}$ is the $252 \times 252$ discrete Fourier transform matrix. Evaluated from the
left, the product $(\mmat{F}^{\mathsf{H}} \mmat{E}) \mmat{F}$ has a dense Jacobian of
$63{,}504 \times 36$ entries, which the numeric pass fills one entry at a time. Evaluated from
$\mvec{x}$, as the reassociation of matrix products excluded from the thesis measurements does,
every intermediate is a vector, and the canonicalization takes $16$ milliseconds instead of
$22.9$ seconds, with a peak memory of $53$ megabytes instead of $1.75$ gigabytes.

\paragraph{OptimalAdvertising.} The problem maximizes
$\sum_{i=1}^{250} \min(r_i \mvec{p}_i^\top \mvec{d}_i, b_i)$ subject to $\mmat{D} \ge \zeros$,
$\mmat{D}^\top \ones \le \mvec{t}$ and $\mmat{D} \ones \ge \mvec{c}$, over
$\mmat{D} \in \reals^{250 \times 1{,}000}$ with rows $\mvec{d}_i^\top$. Each minimum term depends
on one row of $\mmat{D}$, but its Jacobian spans all $250{,}000$ variables, and the symbolic pass
visits every column of it, so its cost grows with the number of terms times the number of
variables. The two aggregate constraints are written as products with a vector of ones, which
become dense blocks. A change to \texttt{CVXPY} lowers them to sums, which reduces the
canonicalization from $0.91$ to $0.33$ seconds, below the $0.53$ seconds of the fastest
\texttt{CVXPY} backend, and the peak memory from $6.1$ to $1.4$ gigabytes.

\paragraph{SDPSegfault1132.} Three dense blocks of $49.5$ million entries cancel in a sum with
$1.01$ million nonzeros, and \texttt{diffengine} carries the cancelled entries through both
passes.

\paragraph{ConvexPlasticity.} The diagonal $h \mmat{I}$ of the quadratic objective is given as a
dense array and becomes a dense $3{,}000 \times 3{,}000$ block of $\mmat{P}$. A change to
\texttt{CVXPY} stores $\mmat{P}$ sparse below $5\,\%$ nonzeros, as is already done for a
constant factor of a matrix product, which reduces the canonicalization from $509$ to $203$
milliseconds and the peak memory from $1.33$ to $0.59$ gigabytes. The problem remains $2.9$
times slower than on the fastest \texttt{CVXPY} backend, and most of the remaining time is in
the symbolic pass.

On each slower problem, one or two phases take almost all of the time, and three of the four
improve with a change made before the data reaches the engine.
