\Chapter{RESULTS}\label{sec:Theme2}

This chapter compares \texttt{diffengine}, the canonicalization backend we built in
Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme1} using \texttt{SparseDiffEngine}, against \texttt{CVXPY}'s three
existing backends. The three are the
default \texttt{cvxcore}, which we refer to as \texttt{CPP}, and the two Python
backends \texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO}, all described in \S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}.
Two questions motivate these comparisons.
First, we want to evaluate the relative benefit of our proposed symbolic and numeric split
against the parameter-to-data tensor. Both approaches store results of the first
canonicalization so that subsequent parameter updates are cheap.
If the split matches the tensor's re-solve
times at a lower upfront cost, building the tensor becomes harder to justify.
Second, the \texttt{CVXPY} developers have deprecated \texttt{cvxcore} and plan
to remove it from the library~\cite{zhang2026cvxpy}. It nevertheless remains the default
backend, in part because neither Python backend has shown a decisive speedup on the first
canonicalization. These comparisons allow us to assess whether
\texttt{diffengine} could be a suitable replacement.

We use the $25$ problems of \texttt{CVXPY}'s public benchmark suite
\cite{cvxpybenchmarks}. The suite includes problems from portfolio selection, regression
and classification, image inpainting, and many more applications in convex optimization.
Appendix~\ref{app:bench} describes each problem and gives its dimensions.
On parametric problems, canonicalization follows either
the \ac{DPP} path or the \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path, both described in
\S\ref{sec:ms-parameters}. \S\ref{sec:bench-oneshot} compares the first canonicalization time
of all $25$ problems on the \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path. \S\ref{sec:bench-ablation} turns the
dense blocks off, \S\ref{sec:bench-sensitivity} varies the size and the density of the
problems, and \S\ref{sec:bench-slower} examines the four problems on which
\texttt{diffengine} is slower. \S\ref{sec:bench-param} compares the parameter-to-data tensor
against the symbolic and numeric split on the seven \ac{DPP} parametric problems of the suite,
and on problems that are not \ac{DPP}.

\paragraph{Setup.}
All measurements run single-threaded on an Apple M2 with \SI{24}{\giga\byte} of memory,
under macOS~26.5.1, with Apple's Accelerate framework for the \ac{BLAS} calls of the engine.
\texttt{CVXPY}'s backends run on the released
version 1.9.2. The \texttt{diffengine} backend is in a separate branch,
available as a pull request:
\begin{center}
  \url{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3448}
\end{center}
No backend uses the reassociation of matrix products of \texttt{CVXPY} pull request
\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}{\#3524},
which applies to all of them (\S\ref{sec:bench-slower}).
Likewise, on a re-solve, \texttt{diffengine} reorders the rows of $\mmat{A}$ into the cone
order of the solver as version 1.9.2 does, by a product with a signed permutation matrix, and
not as pull request \href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3525}{\#3525} does. That pull
request replaces the product by a row selection, which the tensor backends apply once to the
tensor, but which costs \texttt{diffengine} a conversion of $\mmat{A}$ to CSR and back at every
re-solve. On a first canonicalization, which reorders the rows once, \texttt{diffengine} uses
the row selection.
Each measurement times one call to \texttt{get\_problem\_data} with
\texttt{Clarabel}~\cite{clarabel} as the target solver, under a time limit of 30
minutes and a memory limit of \SI{20}{\giga\byte}. The method canonicalizes the
problem and returns the resulting problem data without calling the solver. We exclude the
solver because the choice of backend affects only canonicalization. The target solver only
fixes the form of the output. We choose \texttt{Clarabel} because it is the default conic
solver of \texttt{CVXPY} when no commercial solver is installed, and because it accepts a
quadratic objective and every cone of the suite, so every problem is canonicalized to the same
form~\eqref{e:quad-conic-form} without reductions specific to a solver.
Before any timing, we check that the problem data of
\texttt{diffengine} matches that of \texttt{CPP} to a relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$, at the
first canonicalization and after a parameter update. The one exception is OptimalAdvertising,
whose generator divides zero by zero and gives one coefficient equal to NaN; \texttt{diffengine},
which obtains $\mvec{b}$ by evaluating the constraints at the origin, carries it into one entry
of $\mvec{b}$ as well as into $\mmat{A}$.
The benchmark scripts and all recorded timings are available at
\begin{center}
  \url{https://github.com/SparseDifferentiation/canonicalization-benchmarks}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Each measurement is made in $30$ runs, each in a fresh process, and on a parametric problem
each run uses new parameter values. The only exceptions are three measurements of the size
sweep of \S\ref{sec:bench-sensitivity}, whose first run takes more than $30$~seconds and which
are made in $10$ runs. The tables report the mean and the standard deviation of
the runs, and the ratios are ratios of medians. On each problem, the runs of
\texttt{diffengine} and those of the other backend are compared with a two-sided
Mann--Whitney $U$ test~\cite{mann1947}, with $p$-values adjusted for the number of problems in
the comparison by the Holm--Bonferroni method~\cite{holm1979}. The Mann--Whitney test compares
the ranks of the runs and assumes no particular distribution of the times, which are skewed
to the right by interruptions of the operating system. Over the suite, we test whether the
geometric mean of the ratios differs from $1$ with a Wilcoxon signed-rank
test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} on their logarithms, and give a bootstrap $95\,\%$ confidence
interval for it~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}. A significant difference on a problem means that
the gap between the two backends is too large to be explained by the variation from one run to
the next. Because we test $25$ problems at once, some of them would appear significant by chance
alone if each were tested at $5\,\%$; the Holm--Bonferroni adjustment keeps the probability of
even one such false result below $5\,\%$ over the whole comparison, so each significant ratio
can be read on its own. In the figures, a filled marker denotes a
significant difference (Holm-adjusted $p < 0.05$) and an open marker one that is not.

\label{sec:bench-oneshot}

Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}a reports the Dolan--Mor\'e performance profile
\cite{dolan2002} of the canonicalization times. A performance profile compares the backends
over the whole suite at once. We define $t_{p,s}$ as the median time of backend $s$ on
problem $p$. The profile of backend $s$ is the fraction of the $N$ problems on which it
is within a factor $\tau$ of the fastest backend,
\BEQ\label{eq:perf-profile}
f_s(\tau) = \frac{1}{N}\,\bigl|\{\, p : t_{p,s} \le \tau \, \textstyle\min_b t_{p,b}
\,\}\bigr|.
\EEQ
The minimum $\min_b t_{p,b}$ is the time of the fastest backend on problem~$p$.
The value at $\tau = 1$ is the fraction of
problems on which the backend is the fastest, and the curve reaches $1$ at the factor the
backend needs to cover every problem. \texttt{diffengine} is within a factor of $1.68$ of
the best backend on $23$ of the $25$ problems, within $7.3$ on ConvexPlasticity and within
$11.7$ on UnconstrainedQP. No
\texttt{CVXPY} backend is fastest on more than two problems, and each needs
$\tau \approx 7$ to cover $92$--$96\,\%$ of the problems.

Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}b reports the same profile for peak memory,
with $t_{p,s}$ replaced by the growth of the process's peak resident memory during
canonicalization, and Table~\ref{tab:app-memory} gives it for every problem.
\texttt{diffengine} has the lowest peak memory on
$12$ of the $25$ problems and \texttt{SCIPY} on $8$. In geometric mean, \texttt{diffengine}
uses $1.04$ times the memory of \texttt{CPP}, $0.98$ times that of \texttt{COO}, and $1.45$
times that of \texttt{SCIPY}.

\section{Ablation of the dense blocks}
\label{sec:bench-ablation}

The branch of \texttt{CVXPY} used in this chapter has a switch that stores every constant
matrix in the sparse format, so that no dense block is built from constant data. The other
differences between \texttt{diffengine} and \texttt{CVXPY}'s backends, namely the
implementation in C, one Jacobian per node and the global layout, are part of every atom and
cannot be turned off one at a time. The symbolic and numeric split plays no part in a first
canonicalization, because each pass runs once; \S\ref{sec:bench-param} measures it on the
re-solves.

Table~\ref{tab:app-nodense} and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}a report the first canonicalization
without the dense blocks. Over the suite, \texttt{diffengine} is $1.06$ times slower without
them in geometric mean, with a confidence interval of $0.71$ to $1.45$, which is not
significant (Wilcoxon, $p = 0.07$): \texttt{diffengine} is $2.15$ times faster than the best
\texttt{CVXPY} backend without them and $2.29$ times with them. Per problem, the effect is
significant on $24$ of the $25$ problems and large in both directions. On six problems with
dense data, the dense blocks take \texttt{diffengine} from about $3$ to about $7$ times faster
than the best \texttt{CVXPY} backend, and on ConeMatrixStuffing and SmallMatrixStuffing they
make it about $3$ times faster. Without them, \texttt{diffengine} is significantly faster on
$8$ problems, among them UnconstrainedQP by a factor of $46$, ConvexPlasticity by a factor of
$5.2$ and CVaR by a factor of $1.4$. On these problems a dense block stores every entry,
including the zeros of a constant that is sparse in fact, such as the diagonal of
ConvexPlasticity.

The same effect shows in memory (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}b). Without the dense blocks,
\texttt{diffengine} uses $0.83$ times the peak memory in geometric mean
(Table~\ref{tab:app-memory}). The peak memory of ConvexPlasticity falls from $1\,329$ to
$25.5$ megabytes, that of OptimalAdvertising from $6\,066$ to $945$, and that of CVaR from
$10\,654$ to $2\,115$.

\section{Sensitivity to size and density}
\label{sec:bench-sensitivity}

The problems of the suite have fixed sizes. To see how the comparison depends on the
characteristics of the problem, we vary them on the suite problems themselves. Each problem
fixes its sizes as literals in its source, and a factor $s$ multiplies these literals before
the problem is built, which multiplies the number of nonzeros of its data by about $s$ on most
problems. All $25$ problems and the parameter-free \texttt{lasso} family of
\S\ref{sec:bench-nondpp}, with $n = 1000$ at $s = 1$, are measured at
$s \in \{1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4\}$, except QuantumHilbertMatrix, whose only size is its number of
qubits, at $s \in \{1/2, 1, 2\}$. For the density, the data matrix of the eight problems of
Figure~\ref{fig:density} is multiplied entrywise by a random $0/1$ mask with a fraction
$\rho \in \{0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1\}$ of ones, drawn from its own generator so that
the other data is unchanged. The masked data stays a dense array, as it would be in a user's
code. Each point is measured as in \S\ref{sec:bench-oneshot}, in $30$ runs, and the
$p$-values are Holm-adjusted over the problems of each size and of each density.

Figure~\ref{fig:size} reports the ratio of \texttt{diffengine} to the best \texttt{CVXPY}
backend at every size, and the difference is significant in all $119$ cells measured. On $18$ of
the $22$ problems where \texttt{diffengine} is faster at $s = 1$, counting \texttt{lasso}, the
ratio stays within $20\,\%$ of its value at $s = 1$ at every size, over a factor of $16$ in size.
The largest changes are on FactorCovarianceModel, from $0.15$ at $s = 1$ to $0.22$ at $s = 4$,
SmallMatrixStuffing, $0.42$ at $s = 1/4$, and ParametrizedQP, $0.17$ at $s = 1/4$. Murray and
QuantumHilbertMatrix, the two problems closest to a tie at $s = 1$, are slightly slower at some
sizes, by a factor of at most $1.17$. Among the four slower problems, the gap widens with the
size on UnconstrainedQP, from $5.7$ at $s = 1/4$ to $22.6$ at $s = 4$, and on ConvexPlasticity,

\section{The four slower problems}
\label{sec:bench-slower}

On the four problems on which \texttt{diffengine} is slower, the cause lies in how their data
reaches the engine: a chain of products evaluated away from its constant (UnconstrainedQP),
the global layout of \S\ref{sec:Limitations}, which makes the symbolic pass visit every
variable once per term (OptimalAdvertising), dense blocks that cancel in a sum
(SDPSegfault1132), and a diagonal given as a dense array (ConvexPlasticity).
Table~\ref{tab:app-phases} gives the share of each phase of the first canonicalization of

fix was applied alone to the version of \texttt{CVXPY} used in this chapter and measured as in
\S\ref{sec:bench-oneshot}, in $30$ runs, together with $30$ new runs without it in the same
session. The times without the fix match those of Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile}.

\paragraph{UnconstrainedQP.} The problem is
\BEQ
\mbox{minimize} \quad \bigl\lVert \mmat{F}^{\mathsf{H}} \mmat{E}(\mvec{z}) \mmat{F} \mvec{x}
- \mvec{y} \bigr\rVert_2, \qquad \mmat{E}(\mvec{z}) = \mmat{I}_{14} \otimes \diag(\mvec{z}),
\EEQ
over $\mvec{z} \in \complex^{18}$, where $\mmat{F}$ is the $252 \times 252$ discrete Fourier
transform matrix, $\mmat{F}^{\mathsf{H}}$ its conjugate transpose, and $\mvec{x}$ and $\mvec{y}$ are constant vectors. Evaluated from the left,
the product $(\mmat{F}^{\mathsf{H}} \mmat{E}) \mmat{F}$ has a dense Jacobian of
$63\,504 \times 36$ entries, which the numeric pass fills one entry at a time. Evaluated from
$\mvec{x}$, as pull request
\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}{\#3524} does, every intermediate is a vector,
and the canonicalization takes $16$ milliseconds instead of $22.9$ seconds, with a peak memory
of $53$ megabytes instead of $1.75$ gigabytes.

\paragraph{OptimalAdvertising.} The problem is
\BEQ
\mbox{maximize} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{250} \min\bigl(r_i \mvec{p}_i^\top \mvec{d}_i,\; b_i\bigr)
\quad \mbox{subject to} \quad \mmat{D} \ge \zeros, \quad \mmat{D}^\top \ones \le \mvec{t}, \quad
\mmat{D} \ones \ge \mvec{c},
\EEQ
over $\mmat{D} \in \reals^{250 \times 1000}$ with rows $\mvec{d}_i^\top$. Each minimum term
depends on one row of $\mmat{D}$, but its Jacobian spans all $250\,000$ variables, and the
symbolic pass visits every column of it. Its cost grows with the number of terms times the
number of variables, which is why the ratio grows with the size of the problem
(\S\ref{sec:bench-sensitivity}). The two aggregate constraints are written as products with a
vector of ones, which become dense blocks. Pull request
\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3570}{\#3570} lowers them to sums, which
reduces the canonicalization from $0.91$ to $0.33$ seconds, below the $0.53$ seconds of the
fastest \texttt{CVXPY} backend, and the peak memory from $6.1$ to $1.4$ gigabytes.

\paragraph{SDPSegfault1132.} With $\mmat{K} = \mmat{V} \mmat{G} \mmat{V}^\top$, the problem is
\BEQ
\mbox{maximize} \quad \Tr \mmat{G} - \bigl\lVert \ones \diag(\mmat{K})^\top + \diag(\mmat{K})
\ones^\top - 2 \mmat{K} - \mmat{D} \bigr\rVert_F
\EEQ
over $\mmat{G} \in \symm^{99}_+$, with constant $\mmat{V} \in \reals^{100 \times 99}$ and
$\mmat{D} \in \reals^{100 \times 100}$. Three dense blocks of $49.5$ million entries cancel
in a sum with $1.01$ million nonzeros, and \texttt{diffengine} carries the cancelled entries
through both passes.

\paragraph{ConvexPlasticity.} The problem is
\BEQ
\begin{array}{ll}
\mbox{minimize} & \tfrac{1}{2} \mvec{e}^\top \mmat{S} \mvec{e} + \tfrac{1}{2}
(\mvec{p} - \mvec{p}_0)^\top (h \mmat{I}) (\mvec{p} - \mvec{p}_0) \\
\mbox{subject to} & \sqrt{3/2}\, \lVert \mmat{Q} \gvec{\sigma}_j \rVert_2 \le \sigma_0 + h p_j,
\quad j = 1, \ldots, 3000,
\end{array}
\EEQ
over the stresses $\gvec{\sigma}_j \in \reals^4$ and $\mvec{p} \ge \zeros$, where
$\mvec{e}$ stacks the differences $\gvec{\sigma}_j - \gvec{\sigma}_{\mathrm{el},j}$,
$\mmat{S}$ is block diagonal and $\mmat{Q}$ extracts the deviatoric part. The diagonal $h \mmat{I}$ is given as a
dense array and becomes a dense $3000 \times 3000$ block of $\mmat{P}$. Pull request
\href{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3571}{\#3571}
stores $\mmat{P}$ sparse below $5\,\%$ nonzeros, as is already done for a constant factor of a
matrix product, which reduces the canonicalization from $509$ to $203$ milliseconds and the peak
memory from $1.33$ to $0.59$ gigabytes. The problem remains $2.9$ times slower than on the
fastest \texttt{CVXPY} backend, and most of the remaining time is in the symbolic pass.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:bench-summary}

The benchmark experiments in this chapter lead us to make the following three observations:
\begin{itemize}
\item Caching canonicalization does not necessarily require the \ac{DPP} ruleset. In
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, the symbolic and numeric split re-solves within a factor of
four of the cached tensor on every \ac{DPP} problem. In
\S\ref{sec:bench-nondpp}, it re-solves problems that are not \ac{DPP}, on which
\texttt{CVXPY} has to canonicalize again, $13$ to $14$ times faster.
\item The tensor approach carries a large upfront cost that is not always justified. In
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, the split's first canonicalization is faster than building
the tensor on every problem. The tensor re-solves faster on four of the seven problems, but
it makes up for its slower first canonicalization only after $18$ to $124$ re-solves.
\item \texttt{cvxcore} can be replaced. \texttt{diffengine} canonicalizes faster than
\texttt{cvxcore} on $23$ of the $25$ problems, as reported in
Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile} and Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}. On the parametric problems of
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, \texttt{COO} builds the parameter-to-data tensor faster than
\texttt{cvxcore} on six of the seven.
\end{itemize}

Traditionally, \texttt{CVXPY} has relied on \ac{DPP} to guarantee a meaningful
speedup between the first and subsequent canonicalizations of a parametric problem.
If a problem does not conform to the \ac{DPP} grammar, users have to either attempt
one of many possible reformulations or accept that every subsequent canonicalization
will cost the same as the first. In addition, even if the problem conforms to the grammar,
forming the tensor is sometimes prohibitive, especially for large-scale problems.
Our first two observations challenge this tradition and suggest that \ac{DPP} should be
used more carefully, for problems that are re-solved enough times or for applications
built on downstream packages that depend on the tensor, for example
\texttt{CVXPYgen}~\cite{schaller2022cvxpygen} and \texttt{CVXPYlayers}~\cite{agrawal2019dpp}.
With our approach, a parametric problem is re-solved without building the tensor and without
any rules about how its parameters appear, at re-solve times within a factor of four of the
tensor on the \ac{DPP} problems of the suite.

In addition, the second and third observations suggest that \texttt{diffengine} can
replace \texttt{cvxcore} as the default backend of \texttt{CVXPY}.
Its first canonicalization is faster on most benchmark problems, and its re-solves stay
within a small factor of the tensor without requiring one, so there is
little reason to use \texttt{cvxcore} in the future.
Making this change will require substantial work and coordination with the
maintainers of \texttt{CVXPY}. We discuss these
next steps and the roadmap of future work in \S\ref{sec:FutureResearch}.
Finally, \texttt{diffengine} has downsides. Its peak
memory is higher on several problems, and its re-solve requires the cached program to
stay in memory. The benchmark suite also covers only a subset of possible optimization
problems, and more experiments would be needed to validate these observations further.
We return to these limitations in \S\ref{sec:Limitations}.
