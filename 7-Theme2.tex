\Chapter{BENCHMARK RESULTS}\label{sec:Theme2}

This chapter presents benchmark results which compare the \texttt{diffengine} backend
(the \texttt{CVXPY} implementation based on \texttt{SparseDiffEngine}) against
\texttt{CVXPY}'s own canonicalization backends: the default \texttt{cvxcore} backend, the
matrix-stuffing baseline of \S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}, and the two pure-Python backends,
\texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO}, that \texttt{CVXPY} ships as alternatives to it. The
comparison is restricted to \texttt{CVXPY}'s own pipeline
because that is the claim under test: \texttt{diffengine} is a drop-in replacement for the
canonicalization backend, and the relevant question is whether it constructs the same
problem data faster.
All backends run on the $25$ problems of \texttt{CVXPY}'s public benchmark suite
(\url{https://github.com/cvxpy/benchmarks}). These convex optimization problems cover many
fields, ranging from portfolio models, robust regression, and semidefinite programs to simple
LPs and QPs that are close to standard form.

\section{Benchmark Setup}

Every benchmark measures the construction of the problem data $(A,b,c,P)$; no solver is
invoked. For both backends this corresponds to a call to \texttt{get\_problem\_data}
with the target solver fixed to \texttt{Clarabel}~\cite{clarabel}: the \texttt{CPP}
(\texttt{cvxcore})
baseline runs on the default DPP path, while \texttt{diffengine} is selected by passing
\texttt{ignore\_dpp=True}. The alternative \texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO} backends of
\S\ref{sec:bench-scipy-coo} are selected through the \texttt{canon\_backend} argument of the
same call.

% TODO(read-through): with a single baseline left, "Every comparison" reads odd; rephrase
% (e.g. "Each benchmark reports up to two timings").
Every comparison reports up to two timings: a \emph{single} solve, where the problem
data are constructed once from scratch, and, on the subset of parametric problems, a
\emph{re-solve}, where the problem data are reconstructed after reassigning random parameter
values on a cached problem.

All measurements run single-threaded (BLAS thread pools pinned to one thread) on a 2018 MacBook
Pro with a 6-core Intel Core i7-8850H at \SI{2.6}{\giga\hertz} and \SI{16}{\giga\byte} of RAM.
The software versions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{Python} 3.14,
  \item \texttt{CVXPY} 1.10 (development version),
  \item \texttt{sparsediffpy} 0.6.1,
  \item \texttt{NumPy} 2.4~\cite{numpy} and \texttt{SciPy} 1.17~\cite{2020SciPy-NMeth}.
\end{itemize}

Each cold compilation is measured once, in a fresh process per backend so that no cached
compilation chain is reused; the re-solve columns are means of three re-evaluations.
The \texttt{cvxcore} and \texttt{diffengine} timings were recorded in one session; the
\texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO} timings of \S\ref{sec:bench-scipy-coo} were recorded in a
second session on the same machine and software versions, in which re-measured
\texttt{cvxcore} times reproduced the first session within a few percent.
Before any timing, \texttt{diffengine} is verified to reproduce
the \texttt{cvxcore} problem data bit-identically ($\max|\Delta| = 0$) at full benchmark
size. The scripts which run the benchmarks and all recorded timings are available
at \url{https://github.com/SparseDifferentiation/Jacobian-accumulation} under the
\texttt{canonicalization/} directory.

\section{Single-solve compilation against the \texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO} backends}
\label{sec:bench-scipy-coo}

Beyond the default C++ \texttt{cvxcore}, \texttt{CVXPY} ships two pure-Python
canonicalization backends built on \texttt{scipy.sparse}~\cite{2020SciPy-NMeth}. The
\texttt{SCIPY} backend represents each slice of the parameter tensor as a two-dimensional
sparse matrix, while the more recent \texttt{COO} backend (introduced in 2025) keeps the
whole tensor in coordinate format (\S\ref{sec:bg-sparse}) so that its operations scale with
the number of stored nonzeros even when the parameters are large. Re-running the cold-compile
experiment of \S\ref{sec:bench-cpp-single} under these two backends answers whether the
conclusions above depend on the choice of \texttt{cvxcore} as the baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:backend-sweep}). The \texttt{CPP} and \texttt{diffengine} columns repeat
those of Table~\ref{tab:cold-compile}.

The sweep first shows that \texttt{cvxcore} is not uniformly the fastest of \texttt{CVXPY}'s
own backends. The pure-Python \texttt{SCIPY} backend beats \texttt{CPP} on $18$ of the $22$
completed problems, usually by a modest $1.0$--$1.3\times$ but by $5.9\times$ on
\textsc{SDPSegfault1132} and $6.7\times$ on \textsc{Cajas}. Its weakness is the
parameter-heavy problems, where building one tensor slice per parameter entry collapses:
\textsc{ParamSmallMatrixStuffing} takes \SI{28.0}{\second} against \texttt{CPP}'s
\SI{0.48}{\second}. The \texttt{COO} backend repairs exactly those rows
(\SI{0.42}{\second} and \SI{0.27}{\second}, on par with \texttt{CPP}), which is what its
coordinate-format tensor is designed for, at the price of trailing \texttt{SCIPY} elsewhere
(\textsc{SDPSegfault1132}, \textsc{ConeMatrixStuffing}). Neither Python backend completes
the full suite: both raise a \texttt{ValueError} on \textsc{ConvexPlasticity}, the sweep
lost \textsc{SimpleFullyParametrizedLP} to a crash of the benchmark process, and
\textsc{ParametrizedQP} exceeded the \SI{1200}{\second} per-problem timeout.

Measured against an oracle that picks the fastest of the three backends on each problem,
\texttt{diffengine} remains the fastest option on $18$ of the $22$ completed problems and is
within $10\,\%$ of the best on two more (\textsc{Murray}, \textsc{CVaR}), with a
geometric-mean ratio of $0.33$, that is, $3.1\times$ faster than the oracle. It is clearly
beaten only on \textsc{SDPSegfault1132} ($1.2\times$ slower than \texttt{SCIPY}) and
\textsc{OptimalAdvertising} ($2.5\times$ slower than \texttt{COO}); as in
\S\ref{sec:bench-cpp-single}, both are problems whose compilation is dominated by moving one
large block of constant data into the problem matrices rather than by computing
coefficients. The conclusions of \S\ref{sec:bench-cpp-single} therefore do not hinge on
\texttt{cvxcore} being the baseline.

\texttt{diffengine} is faster on the re-solve for six of the eight parametric problems, and on
the first solve for all but \textsc{ConvexPlasticity}. Its re-solves are fast because the
compiled program is cached, so only the parameter-dependent coefficient entries are
re-evaluated. The DPP baseline instead multiplies its cached parameter tensor by the new
parameter vector, which is hard to beat when that tensor is small and dense. This explains the
two rows where DPP remains around $2\times$ faster: a scalar parameter whose effect is spread
across many coefficients (\textsc{SVM}'s regularization weight, \textsc{FactorCovariance}'s
risk aversion) is the ideal case for the tensor product.
