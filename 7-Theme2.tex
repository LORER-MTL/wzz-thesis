\Chapter{BENCHMARK RESULTS}\label{sec:Theme2}

This chapter compares the \texttt{diffengine} backend of Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme1} against
\texttt{CVXPY}'s three canonicalization backends: the default \texttt{cvxcore}
backend of \S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}, denoted \texttt{CPP}, and the two pure-Python
backends \texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO}. The comparison is restricted to \texttt{CVXPY}'s
own backends because \texttt{diffengine} is a drop-in replacement for them, and the
relevant question is whether it constructs the same problem data faster. All measurements
use the $25$ problems of \texttt{CVXPY}'s public benchmark suite \cite{cvxpybenchmarks},
which range from LPs and QPs close to standard form to portfolio, regression, and
semidefinite models. Seventeen problems have constant data; the other eight contain
parameters.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:bench-setup}

Every benchmark measures the construction of the problem data $(P, c, A, b)$; no solver is
invoked. The timed region is a single call to \texttt{get\_problem\_data} with the target
solver fixed to \texttt{Clarabel}~\cite{clarabel}. The \texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO}
backends are selected through the \texttt{canon\_backend} argument of that call, and
\texttt{diffengine} by passing \texttt{ignore\_dpp=True}.

On the parametric problems the default path and \texttt{diffengine} build different
objects. The \ac{DPP} path builds the parameter-to-data tensor of
\S\ref{sec:ms-parameters}, while \texttt{diffengine} emits the concrete matrices for the
current parameter values. \texttt{CVXPY}'s backends are therefore measured twice on these
problems, once on the default DPP path and once with \texttt{ignore\_dpp=True}, on which
each parameter is substituted by its value and the backend builds the same matrices
\texttt{diffengine} does.

\texttt{CVXPY} also does not always use the backend it is asked for. When no
\texttt{canon\_backend} is given and the total parameter size reaches $1000$ entries, it
silently substitutes \texttt{COO} for \texttt{cvxcore}. The \texttt{CPP} columns are
therefore obtained by passing \texttt{canon\_backend="CPP"} explicitly.

All measurements run single-threaded on a MacBook Pro (15-inch, 2018) with a 6-core Intel
Core i7-8850H and \SI{16}{\giga\byte} of RAM. Each cold construction is measured in a
fresh process, and a measurement is abandoned after \SI{2400}{\second} or when the process
is killed by the operating system. Entries are medians of three replications unless a
table marks them otherwise. \texttt{CPP}, \texttt{SCIPY}, and \texttt{COO} require
unmodified \texttt{CVXPY}, so their measurements are taken in a second environment
running \texttt{CVXPY} 1.9.2 as released,
while \texttt{diffengine} runs on the fork that carries it. The upstream environment is
slightly faster on control problems, so every comparison that crosses the two works
against \texttt{diffengine}. The benchmark scripts and all recorded timings are available
in the \texttt{canonicalization/} directory of the \texttt{Jacobian-accumulation}
repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/SparseDifferentiation/Jacobian-accumulation}}.

Speed is summarized in two ways. In the tables, the ratio on each problem is
\texttt{diffengine}'s time over the fastest of \texttt{CVXPY}'s three backends on that
problem, so values below $1$ mean \texttt{diffengine} beats all three, and the final row
is the geometric mean of the per-problem ratios. The figures report performance profiles
\cite{dolan2002}. With $t_{p,s}$ the time of backend $s$ on problem $p$ and $n$ the
number of problems, the profile of backend $s$ is the fraction of problems on which it is
within a factor $\tau$ of the fastest backend,
\BEQ\label{eq:perf-profile}
f_s(\tau) = \frac{1}{n}\,\bigl|\{\, p : t_{p,s} \le \tau \, \textstyle\min_b t_{p,b}
\,\}\bigr|,
\EEQ
the minimum running over all four backends. The same profile is reported for memory, with
$t_{p,s}$ replaced by the growth of the process's peak resident memory over the timed
call.

\section{Cold canonicalization}
\label{sec:bench-cold}

Table~\ref{tab:cold-compile} reports the cold times on the $17$ problems without
parameters, where all four backends construct the same object. \texttt{cvxcore} is
frequently not the fastest of \texttt{CVXPY}'s backends; \texttt{SCIPY} beats it on $14$ of the $17$
problems. Against \texttt{cvxcore} alone the geometric-mean ratio would be $0.20$ rather
than $0.32$, a difference attributable to the choice of baseline rather than to
\texttt{diffengine}.

\texttt{diffengine} is the fastest of the four on $14$ of the $17$ problems, with a
geometric-mean ratio of $0.32$, that is, $3.1\times$ faster than an oracle that picks the
best \texttt{CVXPY} backend for each problem. The margins are largest where computing coefficients
dominates the construction; \textsc{UnconstrainedQP}, a small problem wrapped in a large
\texttt{kron} expression, is the extreme case at $121\times$. The three exceptions,
\textsc{CVaR}, \textsc{OptimalAdvertising}, and \textsc{SDPSegfault1132}, are the problems
whose construction is dominated by moving one large block of constant data into the
problem matrices. Where there are no coefficients to compute, structural knowledge of the
atoms buys nothing, and the comparison reduces to how efficiently each backend copies
memory.

Table~\ref{tab:param-cold} reports the cold times on the $8$ parametric problems, each
\texttt{CVXPY} backend measured on both paths. The gap between a backend's two columns is the cost
of the tensor. Building it costs \texttt{cvxcore} and \texttt{SCIPY} $18$--$58\times$
their direct construction time on the two matrix-stuffing problems with tens of thousands
of parameter entries, and neither completes it on the two problems with more than $10^6$
parameter entries. Only \texttt{COO}, whose coordinate-format tensor scales with the
number of stored nonzeros rather than with the parameter dimension
(\S\ref{sec:bg-sparse}), builds the tensor at no extra cost; this degradation is what the
substitution rule of \S\ref{sec:bench-setup} avoids.

\section{Parametric re-solve}
\label{sec:bench-resolve}

A parametric problem is re-solved as its parameter values change. After a parameter
update \texttt{CVXPY} re-applies the cached parameter-to-data tensor, while
\texttt{diffengine} re-runs the numeric pass on its cached program.
Table~\ref{tab:param-resolve} compares the two on the seven problems with a tensor to
re-apply; \textsc{ConvexPlasticity} is not \ac{DPP} as the solving chain evaluates it, so
\texttt{CVXPY} rebuilds it from scratch on every re-solve.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:bench-summary}

On cold canonicalization \texttt{diffengine} beats an oracle that picks the best \texttt{CVXPY}
backend for each problem by $3.1\times$ on the non-parametric half of the suite and by
$2.1\times$ on the parametric half. Its losses are confined to the problems whose
construction reduces to copying one large block of constant data and to the
materialization defect of \textsc{ConvexPlasticity}. Peak memory is at parity with
\texttt{cvxcore} and \texttt{COO} and above \texttt{SCIPY}, heaviest on those same
problems.

On re-solves the comparison is closer. The cached numeric pass beats re-applying the
parameter tensor by $1.4\times$ in geometric mean, and the tensor keeps a $2\times$
advantage where the parameters enter as small dense maps. The tensor must first be built,
however, and on the largest parametric problems \texttt{COO} is the only \texttt{CVXPY} backend
that manages to do so.
