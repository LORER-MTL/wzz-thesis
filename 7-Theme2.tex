\Chapter{RESULTS}\label{sec:Theme2}

This chapter compares \texttt{diffengine}, the canonicalization backend we built in
Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme1} using \texttt{SparseDiffEngine}, against \texttt{CVXPY}'s three
existing backends. The three are the
default \texttt{cvxcore}, which we refer to as \texttt{CPP}, and the two Python
backends \texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO}, all described in \S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}.
Two questions motivate these comparisons.
First, we want to evaluate the relative benefit of our proposed symbolic and numeric split
against the parameter-to-data tensor. Both approaches store results of the first
canonicalization so that subsequent parameter updates are cheap.
If the split matches the tensor's re-solve
times at a lower upfront cost, building the tensor becomes harder to justify.
Second, the \texttt{CVXPY} developers have deprecated \texttt{cvxcore} and plan
to remove it from the library~\cite{zhang2026cvxpy}. It nevertheless remains the default
backend, in part because neither Python backend has shown a decisive speedup on the first
canonicalization. These comparisons allow us to assess whether
\texttt{diffengine} could be a suitable replacement.

We use the $25$ problems of \texttt{CVXPY}'s public benchmark suite
\cite{cvxpybenchmarks}. The suite includes problems from portfolio selection, regression
and classification, image inpainting, and many more applications in convex optimization.
Appendix~\ref{app:instances} describes each problem and gives its dimensions.
On parametric problems, canonicalization follows either
the \ac{DPP} path or the \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path, both described in
\S\ref{sec:ms-parameters}. We conduct numerical experiments on both.
\S\ref{sec:bench-oneshot} compares the first canonicalization of all $25$ problems, and
\S\ref{sec:bench-param} compares the re-solves of the parametric problems.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:bench-setup}

All measurements run single-threaded on an Apple M2 with $24$~\si{\giga\byte} of memory,
under macOS~26.5. The engine is linked against Apple's Accelerate framework for its \ac{BLAS}
calls, and the number of \ac{BLAS} and OpenMP threads is set to one.
\texttt{CVXPY}'s backends run on the released version 1.9.2. The \texttt{diffengine}
backend runs on a branch of \texttt{CVXPY} at commit \texttt{5c07151}, with
\texttt{SparseDiffEngine} at commit \texttt{4f42e05}. The branch adds to the backend only phase timers and
the two switches of \S\ref{sec:bench-ablation}, which are off unless stated otherwise.
The backend itself is available as a pull request:
\begin{center}
  \url{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3448}
\end{center}
This pull request has since been merged into the main branch of \texttt{CVXPY}, on
September 9, 2026, as an opt-in backend for parameter-free problems. The parametric re-solve
path of \S\ref{sec:bench-param} is not yet integrated into \texttt{CVXPY}.
The author also contributed to the \texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO} backends that
\texttt{diffengine} is compared against.

The branch also includes a rewrite of the expression tree from \texttt{CVXPY} pull
request~3524,\footnote{\url{https://github.com/cvxpy/cvxpy/pull/3524}} which reassociates a
chain of matrix products that ends in a constant, for example $(\mmat{A}\mmat{B})\mvec{c}$
into $\mmat{A}(\mmat{B}\mvec{c})$. The rewrite is not part of version 1.9.2 and would speed
up \texttt{CVXPY}'s backends too, so we also apply it before them on the five problems where
it applies, and credit each \texttt{CVXPY} backend with the faster of its two times.

Each measurement times one call to \texttt{get\_problem\_data} with
\texttt{Clarabel}~\cite{clarabel} as the target solver, under a time limit of 30
minutes and a memory limit of $20$~\si{\giga\byte}. The method canonicalizes the
problem and returns the resulting problem data without calling the solver. We exclude the
solver because the choice of backend affects only canonicalization.
Every measurement is repeated in a fresh process, so that no cache or allocation of one
replicate carries over to the next. A measurement is repeated $20$ times, or $10$ times when
its first replicate takes more than $30$ seconds, which no measurement of this chapter did.
On a parametric problem, each replicate
draws new parameter values from its own random seed, so the replicates are also different
instances of the same problem family. The tables report the mean and the standard deviation
of the replicates. Peak memory is measured once per problem and backend, in a separate
process, as the growth of the process's peak resident memory during the call.

On each problem, the replicates of \texttt{diffengine} and those of the other backend are
compared with a two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ test~\cite{mann1947}, and the $p$-values are adjusted for
the $25$ problems with the Holm--Bonferroni method~\cite{holm1979}. A difference is called
significant when its adjusted $p$-value is below $0.05$. Over the suite, we summarize a
comparison by the geometric mean of the per-problem ratios of median times.

Before any timing, we check that every backend produces the same problem data. The
matrices $\mmat{P}$ and $\mmat{A}$ and the vectors $\mvec{c}$ and $\mvec{b}$ of
\texttt{diffengine} are compared with those of \texttt{CPP} to a relative tolerance of
$10^{-9}$, at the first canonicalization and after a parameter update. Every problem of this
chapter passes. The benchmark scripts and all recorded timings are available at
\begin{center}
  \url{https://github.com/SparseDifferentiation/canonicalization-benchmarks}
\end{center}

\section{First canonicalization}
\label{sec:bench-oneshot}

Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile} summarizes the results. \texttt{diffengine} is the
fastest of the four backends on $20$ of the $25$ problems, and the difference is
significant on all $20$. The geometric mean of its ratio to the best \texttt{CVXPY}
backend is $0.40$, and on $15$ problems it is more than twice as fast as the
best \texttt{CVXPY} backend. It is significantly slower on the other five: ConvexPlasticity,
QuantumHilbertMatrix, OptimalAdvertising, SDPSegfault1132 and UnconstrainedQP, which we
return to at the end of this section.

UnconstrainedQP shows how much the rewrite of \S\ref{sec:bench-setup} matters. Its matrix
$\mmat{A}$ has only $18.6$ thousand nonzeros, but its objective multiplies the variable by a
chain of complex constant matrices, among them a Kronecker product with a $252 \times 252$
identity. Evaluated in the order of the expression, every intermediate product of this chain
is wide. Without the rewrite, the three \texttt{CVXPY} backends take $1.95$ to $2.43$ seconds
to build the problem data, and \texttt{diffengine} took $22.9$ seconds on the parent commit
of its branch. With it, \texttt{CPP} takes $0.013$ seconds and \texttt{diffengine} $0.016$.
The rewrite also takes \texttt{CPP} from $1.06$ to $0.27$ seconds on QuantumHilbertMatrix,
which is enough to make \texttt{diffengine} slower there. Appendix~\ref{app:results} gives
the times of the three backends with and without it.

Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}a reports the Dolan--Mor\'e performance profile
\cite{dolan2002} of the canonicalization times. A performance profile compares the backends
over the whole suite at once. We define $t_{p,s}$ as the median time of backend $s$ on
problem $p$. The profile of backend $s$ is the fraction of the $N$ problems on which it
is within a factor $\tau$ of the fastest backend,
\BEQ\label{eq:perf-profile}
f_s(\tau) = \frac{1}{N}\,\bigl|\{\, p : t_{p,s} \le \tau \, \textstyle\min_b t_{p,b}
\,\}\bigr|.
\EEQ
The minimum $\min_b t_{p,b}$ is the time of the fastest backend on problem~$p$.
The value at $\tau = 1$ is the fraction of
problems on which the backend is the fastest, and the curve reaches $1$ at the factor the
backend needs to cover every problem. \texttt{diffengine} is within a factor of $1.85$ of
the best backend on $24$ of the $25$ problems, and within $6.9$ on ConvexPlasticity. No
\texttt{CVXPY} backend is fastest on more than two problems, and each needs
$\tau \approx 7$ to cover $92$--$96\,\%$ of the problems.

Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}b reports the same profile for peak memory,
with $t_{p,s}$ replaced by the growth of the process's peak resident memory during
canonicalization. \texttt{diffengine} has the lowest peak memory on
$13$ of the $25$ problems and \texttt{SCIPY} on $8$. In geometric mean, \texttt{diffengine}
uses $0.87$ times the memory of \texttt{CPP}, $0.83$ times that of \texttt{COO}, and $1.22$
times that of \texttt{SCIPY}. The exceptions are large. On OptimalAdvertising,
\texttt{diffengine} grows by $6.1$~\si{\giga\byte} where \texttt{CPP} grows by
$0.17$~\si{\giga\byte}, and on ConvexPlasticity by $1.3$~\si{\giga\byte} against
$82$~\si{\mega\byte}. Appendix~\ref{app:results} gives the peak memory of every backend on
every problem.

\paragraph{The five slower problems.} To explain the five problems on which
\texttt{diffengine} is not the fastest, we time the phases of its canonicalization
separately. On four of the five, the symbolic pass takes the largest share, between $43$ and $90\,\%$ of the
time, against a median of $20\,\%$ over the suite. UnconstrainedQP is the smallest problem
of the suite, and the whole canonicalization takes $16$ milliseconds, against $13$ for
\texttt{CPP} with the rewrite. On SDPSegfault1132 and ConvexPlasticity, dense constant data
is handled poorly. On the first, three dense blocks of $49.5$ million entries cancel in a sum
that has only $1.01$ million nonzeros, and \texttt{diffengine} carries the cancelled entries
through its symbolic and numeric passes. On the second, a diagonal matrix of size $3000$ is
given as a dense array and becomes a dense $3000 \times 3000$ block of the Hessian.

Two further experiments on the first canonicalization are reported in the appendix. Without
the dense blocks, \texttt{diffengine} is $1.27$ times slower in geometric mean
(\S\ref{sec:bench-ablation}). As nine suite problems and the \texttt{lasso} family are
rescaled over a factor of $16$ in size, its ratio to the best \texttt{CVXPY} backend stays
between $0.13$ and $0.22$ on seven of the ten, and its advantage shrinks as the density of
the data falls, until it is slower on three of five problems at a density of $5\,\%$
(\S\ref{sec:bench-sensitivity}).

\section{Parametric canonicalization}
\label{sec:bench-param}

Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor} reports first canonicalization and re-solve times on the
seven \ac{DPP} parametric problems. We refer to a re-solve in this context as rebuilding the
problem data after a parameter update, so the reported times again exclude the solver.
Each replicate performs one first canonicalization followed by five re-solves with new
parameter values, and the re-solve time of the replicate is the median of the five.
On the first
canonicalization, each \texttt{CVXPY} backend runs the \ac{DPP} path and builds a
parameter-to-data tensor. \texttt{diffengine} instead runs its symbolic pass and one
numeric pass. This is the same work as on the \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path, because
\texttt{diffengine} builds no tensor, so its first canonicalization column repeats the
corresponding times of Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile}. It has the fastest first
canonicalization on every problem, and among
the \texttt{CVXPY} backends only \texttt{COO} is able to build the tensor on all seven. On a
parameter update, the three \texttt{CVXPY} backends re-solve by applying the cached
tensor, and the tensor column reports the fastest of the three. \texttt{diffengine} instead
runs another numeric pass.

The tensor re-solves faster on five of the seven problems, by a factor of $1.12$ to $3.98$,
and \texttt{diffengine} on the two smallest, ParamSmallMatrixStuffing and
ParamConeMatrixStuffing. On the five, little of the re-solve is spent in the numeric pass
itself. The numeric pass takes $3$ to $20\,\%$ of the re-solve, and the assembly and the
formatting, which convert the output of the engine to compressed sparse column
matrices in the cone order of the solver and run again after every numeric pass, take
$66$ to $94\,\%$. On SVMWithL1Regularization and FactorCovarianceModel, the two problems
with the largest ratios, the parameters are a few scalars, the numeric pass takes $3$ and
$4\,\%$ of the re-solve, and nearly all of the time goes to rebuilding $\mmat{A}$ in the
compressed sparse column format and in the cone order.
Without the split, that is, with the conversion and the symbolic pass repeated at every
parameter update, the re-solves are $2.56$ times slower in geometric mean
(\S\ref{sec:bench-ablation}).

\subsection{Problems that are not DPP}
\label{sec:bench-nondpp}

The comparison above is restricted to \ac{DPP} problems, the only ones
on which \texttt{CVXPY} can build a tensor. The numeric pass has no such restriction. It
needs a fixed expression tree and fixed sparsity patterns, and never asks whether the
problem data is affine in the parameters. To measure what this means for re-solves, we
write two problem families in three variants each. Appendix~\ref{app:extra-problems} defines
them. In the \ac{DPP} variant, the parameters enter the problem as \ac{DPP} allows. In the
non-\ac{DPP} variant, one parameter is replaced by the product of two parameters, which
violates the \ac{DPP} rules without changing the problem data. In the parameter-free
variant, the parameters are replaced by constants, so every solve is the canonicalization of
a new problem. At the default parameter values the three variants have the same problem
data, so their times are directly comparable.

Table~\ref{tab:nondpp} reports the re-solve times. On the non-\ac{DPP} variant,
\texttt{CVXPY} has no tensor and canonicalizes the problem again at every solve, at the cost
of a first canonicalization. \texttt{diffengine} re-solves the \ac{DPP} and non-\ac{DPP}
variants with the same numeric pass, in $26.4$ milliseconds on both variants of the
\texttt{lasso} family. Its re-solve on the non-\ac{DPP} variant is $12$ times faster than
\texttt{CVXPY}'s, and $1.35$ times faster than its own canonicalization from scratch, the
rebuild column. The \texttt{factor\_cov} family gives the same picture, with a re-solve
$13$ times faster than \texttt{CVXPY}'s. On the \ac{DPP} variants, the tensor remains
faster, by a factor of $4.1$ on \texttt{lasso} and $2.9$ on \texttt{factor\_cov}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:bench-summary}

The benchmark experiments in this chapter lead us to make the following three observations:
\begin{itemize}
\item Caching canonicalization does not require the \ac{DPP} ruleset. In
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, the symbolic and numeric split re-solves within a factor of
four of the cached tensor on every \ac{DPP} problem. In
Table~\ref{tab:nondpp}, it re-solves problems that are not \ac{DPP}, on which
\texttt{CVXPY} has to canonicalize again, $12$ to $13$ times faster.
\item The tensor approach carries a large upfront cost that is not always justified. In
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, the split's first canonicalization is faster than building
the tensor on every problem. The tensor re-solves faster on five of the seven problems, but
it makes up for its slower first canonicalization only after $16$ to $77$ re-solves.
\item \texttt{cvxcore} can be replaced. \texttt{diffengine} canonicalizes faster than
\texttt{cvxcore} on $22$ of the $25$ problems, as reported in
Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile} and Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}. On the parametric problems of
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, \texttt{COO} builds the parameter-to-data tensor faster than
\texttt{cvxcore} on six of the seven.
\end{itemize}

Traditionally, \texttt{CVXPY} has relied on \ac{DPP} to guarantee a meaningful
speedup between the first and subsequent canonicalizations of a parametric problem.
If a problem does not conform to the \ac{DPP} grammar, users have to either attempt
one of many possible reformulations or accept that every subsequent canonicalization
will cost the same as the first. In addition, even if the problem conforms to the grammar,
forming the tensor is sometimes prohibitive, especially for large-scale problems.
Our first two observations challenge this tradition and suggest that \ac{DPP} should be
used more carefully, for problems that are re-solved enough times or for applications
built on downstream packages that depend on the tensor, for example
\texttt{CVXPYgen}~\cite{schaller2022cvxpygen} and \texttt{CVXPYlayers}~\cite{agrawal2019dpp}.
With our approach, a similar speedup can be obtained through the symbolic and numeric split
without enforcing any rules about how parameters appear in the problem formulation.

In addition, the second and third observations suggest that \texttt{diffengine} can
replace \texttt{cvxcore} as the default backend of \texttt{CVXPY}.
Its first canonicalization is faster on most benchmark problems, and its re-solves stay
within a small factor of the tensor without requiring one, so there is
little reason to use \texttt{cvxcore} in the future.
Making this change will require substantial work and coordination with the
maintainers of \texttt{CVXPY}. We discuss these
next steps and the roadmap of future work in \S\ref{sec:FutureResearch}.
Finally, \texttt{diffengine} has downsides. Its peak
memory is much higher on a few problems, it exceeds the memory limit on some of the larger
problems of \S\ref{sec:bench-sensitivity}, and its re-solve requires the cached program to
stay in memory. Its dense representations also slow it down when dense blocks cancel in a
sum, as on SDPSegfault1132, or when a sparse constant is given as a dense array, as on
ConvexPlasticity. The benchmark suite covers only a subset of possible optimization
problems, and the families of \S\ref{sec:bench-nondpp} and \S\ref{sec:bench-sensitivity} add
only a few more. We return to these limitations in \S\ref{sec:Limitations}.
