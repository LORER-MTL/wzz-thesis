\Chapter{RESULTS}\label{sec:Theme2}

This chapter compares \texttt{diffengine}, the canonicalization backend we built in
Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme1} using \texttt{SparseDiffEngine}, against \texttt{CVXPY}'s three
existing backends. The three are the
default \texttt{cvxcore}, which we refer to as \texttt{CPP}, and the two Python
backends \texttt{SCIPY} and \texttt{COO}, all described in \S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}.
Two questions motivate these comparisons.
First, we want to evaluate the relative benefit of our proposed symbolic and numeric split
against the parameter-to-data tensor of \S\ref{sec:ms-parameters}. Both approaches store
results of the first
canonicalization so that subsequent parameter updates are cheap.
If the split matches the tensor's re-solve
times at a lower upfront cost, building the tensor becomes harder to justify.
Second, the \texttt{CVXPY} developers have deprecated \texttt{cvxcore} and plan
to remove it from the library~\cite{zhang2026cvxpy}. It nevertheless remains the default
backend, in part because neither Python backend has shown a decisive speedup on the first
canonicalization. These comparisons allow us to assess whether
\texttt{diffengine} could be a suitable replacement.

We use the $25$ problems of \texttt{CVXPY}'s public benchmark suite
\cite{cvxpybenchmarks}. The suite includes problems from portfolio selection, regression
and classification, image inpainting, and many more applications in convex optimization.
Appendix~\ref{app:bench} describes each problem and gives its dimensions.
On parametric problems, canonicalization follows either
the \ac{DPP} path or the \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path, both described in
\S\ref{sec:ms-parameters}. \S\ref{sec:bench-oneshot} compares the first canonicalization time
of all $25$ problems on the \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path, with and without the dense blocks, and at
other sizes and densities. \S\ref{sec:bench-param} compares the parameter-to-data tensor
against the symbolic and numeric split on the seven \ac{DPP} parametric problems of the suite,
and on problems that are not \ac{DPP}.

\paragraph{Setup.}
All measurements run single-threaded on an Apple M2 with \SI{24}{\giga\byte} of memory,
under macOS~26.5.1, with Apple's Accelerate framework for the \ac{BLAS} calls of the engine.
\texttt{CVXPY}'s backends run on the released
version 1.9.2. The \texttt{diffengine} backend is in a separate branch of \texttt{CVXPY},
not yet part of a release.
No backend uses a later change to \texttt{CVXPY} that reassociates chains of matrix products
ending in a constant, since it applies to all of them.
Each measurement times one call to \texttt{get\_problem\_data} with
\texttt{Clarabel}~\cite{clarabel} as the target solver, under a time limit of 30
minutes and a memory limit of \SI{20}{\giga\byte}. The method canonicalizes the
problem and returns the resulting problem data without calling the solver. We exclude the
solver because the choice of backend affects only canonicalization.
Before any timing, we check that the problem data of
\texttt{diffengine} matches that of \texttt{CPP} to a relative tolerance of $10^{-9}$, at the
first canonicalization and after a parameter update, except for one NaN coefficient in the
data of OptimalAdvertising.
The benchmark scripts and all recorded timings are available at
\begin{center}
  \url{https://github.com/SparseDifferentiation/canonicalization-benchmarks}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Each measurement is made in $30$ runs, each in a fresh process, and on a parametric problem
each run uses new parameter values. The tables report the mean and the standard deviation of
the runs, and the ratios are ratios of medians. On each problem, the runs of
\texttt{diffengine} and of the other backend are compared with a two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$
test~\cite{mann1947}, with the $p$-values adjusted over the problems by the Holm--Bonferroni
method~\cite{holm1979}. A significant difference is thus one too large to be explained by the
variation between runs. Over the suite, we test whether the geometric mean of the ratios
differs from $1$ with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} on their logarithms, and
give a bootstrap $95\,\%$ confidence interval for it~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}.

\section{First canonicalization}
\label{sec:bench-oneshot}

Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile} summarizes the results. \texttt{diffengine} is the
fastest of the four backends on $21$ of the $25$ problems, and the difference is
significant on all $21$. The geometric mean of its ratio to the best \texttt{CVXPY}
backend is $0.44$, with a bootstrap confidence interval of $0.29$ to $0.70$, and it differs
significantly from $1$ (Wilcoxon, $p = 0.003$). On $15$ problems \texttt{diffengine} is more
than twice as fast as the best \texttt{CVXPY} backend. It is significantly slower on the other
four, where the cause lies in how the data reaches the engine. On UnconstrainedQP, a chain of
matrix products is evaluated away from its constant, and the numeric pass, $98\,\%$ of the
time, fills a dense Jacobian. On OptimalAdvertising, the global layout of
\S\ref{sec:Limitations} makes the symbolic pass, $89\,\%$ of the time, visit every variable once
per minimum term. On SDPSegfault1132, dense blocks that cancel in a sum are carried through the
symbolic and numeric passes, $49$ and $47\,\%$ of the time. On ConvexPlasticity, a diagonal
given as a dense array becomes a dense block of $\mmat{P}$, which the symbolic pass and the
assembly take $47$ and $38\,\%$ of the time to process. Over the $25$ problems, no phase takes
more than $19\,\%$ of the time at the median.

Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}a reports the Dolan--Mor\'e performance profile
\cite{dolan2002} of the canonicalization times, which compares the backends over the whole
suite at once. With $t_{p,s}$ the median time of backend $s$ on problem $p$, the profile of
backend $s$ is the fraction of the $N$ problems on which it is within a factor $\tau$ of the
fastest backend,
\BEQ\label{eq:perf-profile}
f_s(\tau) = \frac{1}{N}\,\bigl|\{\, p : t_{p,s} \le \tau \, \textstyle\min_b t_{p,b}
\,\}\bigr|.
\EEQ
The value at $\tau = 1$ is the fraction of
problems on which the backend is the fastest, and the curve reaches $1$ at the factor the
backend needs to cover every problem. \texttt{diffengine} is within a factor of $1.68$ of
the best backend on $23$ of the $25$ problems, within $7.3$ on ConvexPlasticity and within
$11.7$ on UnconstrainedQP. No
\texttt{CVXPY} backend is fastest on more than two problems, and each needs
$\tau \approx 7$ to cover $92$--$96\,\%$ of the problems.

Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}b reports the same profile for peak memory,
with $t_{p,s}$ replaced by the growth of the process's peak resident memory during
canonicalization.
\texttt{diffengine} has the lowest peak memory on
$12$ of the $25$ problems and \texttt{SCIPY} on $8$. In geometric mean, \texttt{diffengine}
uses $1.04$ times the memory of \texttt{CPP}, $0.98$ times that of \texttt{COO}, and $1.45$
times that of \texttt{SCIPY}.

Most of the speedup of \texttt{diffengine} remains without the dense
blocks.\label{sec:bench-ablation} A switch of our \texttt{CVXPY} branch stores every constant
matrix in the sparse format, and the last column of Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile} gives the
ratio with this switch on. Over the suite, \texttt{diffengine} is then $2.15$ times faster than
the best \texttt{CVXPY} backend, against $2.29$ times with the dense blocks, a difference that
is not significant (Wilcoxon, $p = 0.07$). Per problem, the dense blocks gain up to a factor of
$3$ on problems with dense data, and lose up to a factor of $46$ on CVaR, ConvexPlasticity and
UnconstrainedQP, which build a dense block from data that is sparse in fact. Without them,
\texttt{diffengine} also uses $0.83$ times the peak memory in geometric mean.

The suite has fixed sizes, so we also scale\label{sec:bench-sensitivity} every problem, and the
\texttt{lasso} of \S\ref{sec:bench-nondpp}, by a factor $s \in \{1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4\}$ of its
size literals, and mask the data matrix of eight problems to densities $\rho$ from $1$ down to
$0.01$; the three points whose first run exceeds $30$~seconds are made in $10$ runs. Over a
factor of $16$ in size, the ratio stays within $20\,\%$ of its value at $s = 1$ on $18$ of the
$22$ problems where \texttt{diffengine} is faster, and the gap widens on three of the four
slower problems, for the reasons above. As the density falls, the dense blocks lose their
advantage. With them, the ratio starts at $0.14$ to $0.18$ on dense data and crosses the ratio
without them between $\rho = 0.5$ and $\rho = 0.2$, while without them it rises only to $0.52$
to $0.79$ at $\rho = 0.01$ on six of the eight problems. The dense blocks therefore pay off only
on data that is dense in fact. Every difference in these sweeps is significant.

\section{Parametric canonicalization}
\label{sec:bench-param}

Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor} reports first canonicalization and re-solve times on the
seven \ac{DPP} parametric problems. A re-solve here means rebuilding the problem data after a
parameter update, so the times again exclude the solver. Each of the $30$ runs performs one
first canonicalization followed by five re-solves with new parameter values, and keeps the
median of the five.
On the first canonicalization, each \texttt{CVXPY} backend runs the \ac{DPP} path and builds a
parameter-to-data tensor, while \texttt{diffengine} runs its symbolic pass and one numeric
pass, the same work as in Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile}. \texttt{diffengine} has the
fastest first canonicalization on every problem, and among the \texttt{CVXPY} backends only
\texttt{COO} builds the tensor on all seven. On a parameter update, the \texttt{CVXPY}
backends apply the cached tensor, and the tensor column reports the fastest of the three;
\texttt{diffengine} runs another numeric pass. Both re-solve times cover the whole call to
\texttt{get\_problem\_data}, including the construction of the solver's matrices.

The tensor re-solves faster on four of the seven problems, by a factor of $1.10$ to $3.60$,
and \texttt{diffengine} on the other three, ParamSmallMatrixStuffing, ParamConeMatrixStuffing
and SimpleFullyParametrizedLP. On the four, the numeric pass takes only $4$ to $24\,\%$ of the
re-solve. The rest is post-processing after the engine, which assembles the matrices and
converts them to the solver's format, and which the tensor avoids by producing that format
directly.

\paragraph{Problems that are not DPP.}\label{sec:bench-nondpp}
The tensor of \texttt{CVXPY} exists only for \ac{DPP} problems, while the numeric pass needs
only a fixed expression tree and fixed sparsity patterns. We test this on the \texttt{lasso},
with $n = 1000$ variables, and on FactorCovarianceModel, each written once with a scalar
parameter and once with that parameter as the product of two, which \ac{DPP} forbids. On the
non-\ac{DPP} variants, \texttt{CVXPY} canonicalizes again at every solve, while
\texttt{diffengine} reruns only its numeric pass and re-solves $13$ to $14$ times faster.
ConvexPlasticity, the only parametric problem of the suite that is not \ac{DPP}, is the
exception: \texttt{diffengine} re-solves it in $207$ milliseconds against $42$ for
\texttt{CVXPY}, because $88\,\%$ of the re-solve assembles its dense block of $\mmat{P}$.
Without the dense blocks, it re-solves in $2.0$ milliseconds, $21$ times faster than
\texttt{CVXPY}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:bench-summary}

The benchmark experiments in this chapter lead us to make the following three observations:
\begin{itemize}
\item Caching canonicalization does not necessarily require the \ac{DPP} ruleset. In
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, the symbolic and numeric split re-solves within a factor of
four of the cached tensor on every \ac{DPP} problem. In
\S\ref{sec:bench-nondpp}, it re-solves problems that are not \ac{DPP}, on which
\texttt{CVXPY} has to canonicalize again, $13$ to $14$ times faster.
\item The tensor approach carries a large upfront cost that is not always justified. In
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, the split's first canonicalization is faster than building
the tensor on every problem. The tensor re-solves faster on four of the seven problems, but
it makes up for its slower first canonicalization only after $18$ to $124$ re-solves.
\item \texttt{cvxcore} can be replaced. \texttt{diffengine} canonicalizes faster than
\texttt{cvxcore} on $23$ of the $25$ problems, as reported in
Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-compile} and Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}. On the parametric problems of
Table~\ref{tab:param-tensor}, \texttt{COO} builds the parameter-to-data tensor faster than
\texttt{cvxcore} on six of the seven.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{CVXPY} has relied on \ac{DPP} to make re-solving a parametric problem cheap. A problem
outside the \ac{DPP} grammar must be reformulated, or every re-solve costs as much as the first
canonicalization, and even within the grammar the tensor can be prohibitive to build on large
problems. Our first two observations suggest that \ac{DPP} is best kept for problems that are
re-solved many times and for packages that depend on the tensor, such as
\texttt{CVXPYgen}~\cite{schaller2022cvxpygen} and \texttt{CVXPYlayers}~\cite{agrawal2019dpp}.
With our approach, a parametric problem is re-solved without the tensor and without rules on
how its parameters appear, within a factor of four of the tensor on the \ac{DPP} problems of
the suite.

The second and third observations suggest that \texttt{diffengine} can replace
\texttt{cvxcore} as the default backend of \texttt{CVXPY}, a change that will require work
with its maintainers (\S\ref{sec:FutureResearch}). \texttt{diffengine} also has downsides: its
peak memory is higher on several problems, its re-solves need the cached program to stay in
memory, and the suite covers only a subset of optimization problems. We return to these
limitations in \S\ref{sec:Limitations}.
