\Chapter{Handling parameters}\label{sec:Theme2}

\paragraph{Parameters in optimization models}
Many applications require solving a sequence of related optimization problems that differ only in their data.
In Model Predictive Control (MPC), a controller solves an optimization problem at each timestep with the current system state as input.
In parameter sweeps, we explore the behavior of a system across a range of parameter values.
The key insight is that these problems share the same structure---only the numerical coefficients change.
We want to solve many such problems efficiently without reconstructing the problem from scratch each time.

\paragraph{A first example: scalar objective coefficient}
Consider the simplest case where parameters affect the objective function.
The following CVXPY code defines a linear objective with a parametric coefficient vector:
\begin{verbatim}
c = cp.Parameter(3)
x = cp.Variable(3)
objective = c @ x  # c^T x
\end{verbatim}
When the parameter vector $\theta = [c_1, c_2, c_3]^T$ is set, the objective coefficient vector $c(\theta)$ is computed.
In this case, the mapping from $\theta$ to $c$ is simply the identity:
\[
c(\theta) =
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{blockarray}{ccc}
\theta_1 & \theta_2 & \theta_3 \\
\begin{block}{[ccc]}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 \\
\end{block}
\end{blockarray}
\begin{bmatrix}
\theta_1 \\ \theta_2 \\ \theta_3
\end{bmatrix}
\]
We can write this as $c(\theta) = M\theta$ where $M$ is the mapping matrix.
Here $M = I$, but as we will see, more complex expressions produce sparse, non-identity mapping matrices.

\paragraph{A second example: mixed constants and parameters}
Consider an objective where only some coefficients are parametric:
\begin{verbatim}
c = cp.Parameter(2)
x = cp.Variable(4)
objective = c @ x[:2] + np.array([5, 3]) @ x[2:]
\end{verbatim}
This is equivalent to $[c_1, c_2, 5, 3]^T x$, where two coefficients are parametric and two are constant.
The mapping is now:
\[
c(\theta) =
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1 \\ c_2 \\ 5 \\ 3
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{blockarray}{ccc}
1 & \theta_1 & \theta_2 \\
\begin{block}{[ccc]}
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 \\
5 & 0 & 0 \\
3 & 0 & 0 \\
\end{block}
\end{blockarray}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\ \theta_1 \\ \theta_2
\end{bmatrix}
\]
The first column represents the constant part (multiplied by the implicit constant 1), and the remaining columns map $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ to their positions.
Note that this matrix is sparse---most entries are zero.
This sparsity is typical: each coefficient depends on at most one parameter, so each row has few nonzeros.

\paragraph{Generalizing to constraint matrices: the 3D tensor}
So far we have considered vectors $c(\theta)$.
Constraints involve matrices $A(\theta)$, where each entry $A_{ij}$ may depend on parameters.
Consider a parametric constraint matrix:
\begin{verbatim}
P = cp.Parameter((2, 2))
x = cp.Variable(2)
constraint = P @ x <= 0
\end{verbatim}
The parameter $P$ contributes four scalar parameters $\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3, \theta_4$ (in column-major order: $P_{11}, P_{21}, P_{12}, P_{22}$).
The constraint matrix $A(\theta)$ equals $P$:
\[
A(\theta) =
\begin{bmatrix}
A_{11} & A_{12} \\
A_{21} & A_{22}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\theta_1 & \theta_3 \\
\theta_2 & \theta_4
\end{bmatrix}
\]
To represent how each matrix entry depends on the parameters, we use a 3D tensor.
Flattening the matrix entries $(i,j)$ into rows and the parameter indices into columns:
\[
\begin{blockarray}{cccccc}
& 1 & \theta_1 & \theta_2 & \theta_3 & \theta_4 \\
\begin{block}{c[ccccc]}
A_{11} & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
A_{12} & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
A_{21} & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
A_{22} & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
\end{block}
\end{blockarray}
\]
Each row shows how one matrix entry is computed from the parameters.
The first column (labeled $1$) is for constants; here it is all zeros since $P$ has no constant part.

\paragraph{A mixed example with constants}
When parameters are combined with constants, both contribute to the tensor:
\begin{verbatim}
P = cp.Parameter((2, 2))
Q = np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4]])
x = cp.Variable(2)
constraint = (P + Q) @ x <= 0
\end{verbatim}
Now $A(\theta) = P + Q$, so each entry is the sum of a parameter and a constant:
\[
A(\theta) =
\begin{bmatrix}
\theta_1 + 1 & \theta_3 + 2 \\
\theta_2 + 3 & \theta_4 + 4
\end{bmatrix}
\]
The tensor representation includes the constants in the first column:
\[
\begin{blockarray}{cccccc}
& 1 & \theta_1 & \theta_2 & \theta_3 & \theta_4 \\
\begin{block}{c[ccccc]}
A_{11} & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
A_{12} & 2 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
A_{21} & 3 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
A_{22} & 4 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
\end{block}
\end{blockarray}
\]
This representation makes evaluation straightforward: to compute $A(\theta)$, we multiply each row by the extended parameter vector $[1, \theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3, \theta_4]^T$.

\paragraph{The tensor contraction operation}
Given the tensor representation, evaluating $A(\theta)$ is a tensor contraction: we sum over the parameter dimension.
Conceptually, think of the tensor as a stack of matrices---one ``slice'' for the constants and one for each parameter:
\begin{itemize}
\item Slice 0 (constants): $A_0 = Q$
\item Slice 1 ($\theta_1$): has a 1 at position $(1,1)$, zeros elsewhere
\item Slice 2 ($\theta_2$): has a 1 at position $(2,1)$, zeros elsewhere
\item Slice 3 ($\theta_3$): has a 1 at position $(1,2)$, zeros elsewhere
\item Slice 4 ($\theta_4$): has a 1 at position $(2,2)$, zeros elsewhere
\end{itemize}
The evaluation is:
\[
A(\theta) = A_0 \cdot 1 + A_1 \cdot \theta_1 + A_2 \cdot \theta_2 + A_3 \cdot \theta_3 + A_4 \cdot \theta_4
\]
This is simply a weighted sum of the slices.

\paragraph{Sparse representation}
The tensor is extremely sparse.
In the example above, each slice $A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4$ has exactly one nonzero entry.
More generally, each parameter affects only specific entries of the coefficient matrices.
We store the tensor in a sparse format:
\begin{itemize}
\item Shape: $(m, n, p+1)$ where $A(\theta) \in \reals^{m \times n}$ and $\theta \in \reals^p$
\item Storage: list of (row, column, slice, value) tuples for nonzero entries
\end{itemize}
Evaluation produces $A(\theta)$ in COO (Coordinate) format, which we then convert to CSR for efficient arithmetic.

\paragraph{Summary}
The tensor approach represents parametric coefficient matrices as a sparse 3D tensor:
\begin{itemize}
\item Each matrix entry's dependence on parameters is encoded as a row of the flattened tensor
\item The first slice holds constants; subsequent slices hold parameter coefficients
\item Evaluation is a sparse tensor-vector multiplication
\item Sparsity is high because each coefficient typically depends on few parameters
\end{itemize}
This representation enables efficient re-evaluation when parameters change, without rebuilding the optimization problem structure.
