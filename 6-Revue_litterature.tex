\Chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}\label{sec:RevLitt}

This chapter reviews the implementation of \texttt{CVXPY}'s matrix stuffing
(\S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}) and of other modeling language approaches, from sparse AD
(\S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}) to \texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP}
(\S\ref{sec:declared-structure}). These modeling languages have a similar purpose, and the goal of the
chapter is to make the distinctions clear. The backends of \texttt{CVXPY} compute the same
problem data from the same input as \texttt{SparseDiffEngine}, and they serve as the baselines
for the benchmarks of Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme2}. \texttt{CasADi} carries vectorized expressions, but
must discover the structure of their derivatives at runtime, over many sweeps of the graph.
\texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP} let the user declare vectorized expressions, and scalarize
them for processing.

\section{Matrix stuffing in \texttt{CVXPY}}\label{sec:matrix-stuffing}

\texttt{CVXPY} takes the perspective of matrix calculus \cite{magnus2019matrix} for computing the
coefficient matrices of linear expressions, and this is where most of the computational burden of
canonicalization lies.
The section gives a brief summary of matrix calculus and a few examples, presents the conventions
of \texttt{CVXPY}'s backend, and closes with implementation details that become important when
contrasting it with our methodology (\S\ref{sec:Theme1}).

\paragraph{Matrix calculus.}
Matrix calculus extends differentiation to functions whose arguments and values are vectors and
matrices. Systematic rules for such derivatives date back to the 1940s \cite{dwyer1948symbolic}.
The simplest convention is to vectorize \cite{magnus2019matrix}: every expression is identified
with
\BEQ\label{eq:vec}
\vect(X) = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & \cdots & x_{m1} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{mn} \end{bmatrix}^T,
\EEQ
the vector that stacks the columns of $X \in \reals^{m \times n}$, and the derivative of one
vectorized expression with respect to another is an ordinary Jacobian matrix. Under this
convention matrix operations become linear operators on the vectorized arguments, and their
Jacobians carry structure: for example
\BEQ\label{eq:vec-kron}
\vect(FX) = (I \otimes F)\,\vect(X),
\EEQ
so the matrix product $FX$ corresponds to the Kronecker-structured, block-diagonal Jacobian
$I \otimes F$. The convention is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:vec-variable}.

\paragraph{Composing linear operators.}
After canonicalization every expression is affine, so each node of a conic-form tree can be
written in the vectorized form
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-form}
f(x) = A\,x + b
     = \begin{bmatrix} A & b \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where $x$ is the stacked vector of all the problem variables, $A$ is the Jacobian of the node's
expression $f$ with respect to $x$, constant since $f$ is affine, and $b$ is a constant vector.
Each variable occupies a contiguous block of columns of $A$, starting at its \emph{column
offset} within $x$, and the columns of the variables that do not appear under the node are zero.
Matrix stuffing computes the augmented matrix $[A\ b]$ of every node in one bottom-up pass. A
variable leaf contributes an identity block at its own column offset, and a constant leaf
contributes its value to the last column. An interior atom $g$ with arguments $f_1, \dots, f_k$
is a linear map $g = \sum_i L_i f_i$, and since each $L_i$ is a constant matrix under the vec
convention, the chain rule reduces to one matrix product per child:
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule}
\begin{bmatrix} A & b \end{bmatrix}
  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} L_i \begin{bmatrix} A_i & b_i \end{bmatrix}.
\EEQ
Computing the problem data thus amounts to composing linear operators over the tree, with no
differentiation at the nodes. The node matrices of the figures in this section are the matrices
$[A\ b]$, and the final result of the walk is the problem data.

\paragraph{Views and copies.}
The linear operators divide into \emph{views}, which involve no atom data, and \emph{copies},
which do. A view reuses the stored coefficients of its argument: negation flips the sign of
each entry, indexing keeps a subset of the rows, and reshaping leaves the vectorization
unchanged, so the cost of a view is at most proportional to the number of stored nonzeros. A
copy occurs at the bivariate atoms that carry data. In a matrix product, an elementwise
multiplication, or a division, one operand is constant under the DCP rules, and the operator
multiplies a Kronecker or diagonal matrix formed from this constant operand with the augmented
matrix of the other, storing the product as a new coefficient matrix with a new sparsity
pattern. Table~\ref{tab:views-copies} classifies a subset of the operators, where
$\mathcal{I}$ denotes an index set and $\diag(v)$ the diagonal matrix with the entries of $v$
on its diagonal.

\paragraph{Local versus global derivatives.}
The augmented matrices of \eqref{eq:linop-form} can be laid out in two ways
(Figure~\ref{fig:local-vs-global}). \texttt{CVXPY} stores them \emph{locally}: each node keeps
one coefficient block per variable appearing beneath it, keyed by the variable's identifier, and
every operator is applied to each block in turn. A block's columns index only the entries of its
own variable, so the column offsets are not needed during the walk. The constant data is stored
under the key $-1$ and becomes the last column of the assembled matrix. Only after the walk
completes are the blocks shifted to their variables' column offsets and concatenated into the
full matrix $[A\ b]$.

In the alternative, \emph{global} layout, each node stores one matrix whose columns span the
whole stacked vector $x$, and the column offsets are applied from the start rather than at the
end. The local layout keeps the blocks small and skips the variables absent from a subtree, but
every operator runs once per stored block. The engine of \S\ref{sec:Theme1} uses the global
layout.

\paragraph{Parameters and tensors.}
In a problem family the structure is fixed and only the numeric parameter values change
between solves. \ac{DPP} restricts how parameters may enter such families: only affinely, so
that the problem data is affine in the parameters \cite{agrawal2019dpp}. \texttt{CVXPY}
accounts for this with a third tensor axis.
Every coefficient block stores one slice per
parameter entry plus one slice for the constant part, and the operators of the walk apply to
each slice. Flattening the slices of the root yields the cached \emph{parameter map}
\BEQ\label{eq:param-maps}
\begin{bmatrix} c \\ d \end{bmatrix} = Q\,\tilde\theta,
\qquad
\tilde\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \theta \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-dpp} for the objective of problem \eqref{eq:example}; the constraint
data $[A\ b]$ is recovered the same way from its own map. After a parameter change, a re-solve
costs a single sparse matrix--vector product $Q\tilde\theta$.

\paragraph{The \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path.}
Problems that fall outside the DPP grammar, or that opt out of it by solving with
\texttt{ignore\_dpp=True}, take a simpler path. The reduction \texttt{EvalParams} replaces each
parameter leaf by a constant holding its current value, and the resulting parameter-free tree
goes through the walk as if the problem had no parameters
(Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-evalparams}). The result is the same objective data $c$ as in
Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-dpp}, now obtained by re-walking the tree instead of applying the
cached product $Q\tilde\theta$. The path is fully general, since arbitrary functions of
parameters reduce to constants, but every change of $\theta$ repeats the whole symbolic walk
even though the sparsity pattern of the problem data never changes.

\paragraph{The three backends.}
\texttt{CVXPY} implements the walk in three canonicalization backends that differ in how they
represent the parameter axis. The C++ backend \texttt{cvxcore} keeps one sparse matrix per
parameter slice and loops over the slices at every operator. The \texttt{SciPy} backend stacks
the slices vertically into a single two-dimensional sparse matrix. The \texttt{COO} backend does
not form the tensor; it stores each nonzero with an explicit parameter index alongside its row
and column.

\paragraph{Code generation.}
When canonicalization remains too slow, \texttt{CVXPYgen} can compile the problem ahead of
time: it emits problem-specific C code that embeds the cached parameter map
\eqref{eq:param-maps} of a DPP problem \cite{schaller2022cvxpygen}, continuing an earlier line
of work on code generation for embedded second-order cone programs \cite{chu2013codegen}. The
benchmarks of \S\ref{sec:Theme2} do not compare against it: code generation is an offline step
that fixes the problem structure at compile time, a different deployment model from a runtime
backend that must accept arbitrary problems.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
Two implementation details, shared by all the backends, limit the current approaches
in \texttt{CVXPY}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{External sparse libraries.} The backends delegate their matrix operations to a
general-purpose sparse library, Eigen \cite{guennebaud2010eigen} in \texttt{cvxcore} and
\texttt{scipy.sparse} \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth} in the Python backends. Although the COO backend
stores no \texttt{scipy.sparse} matrices, it still converts to them for sparse operations such
as matrix products and Kronecker products. The storage formats and kernels are fixed by the
library, leaving no room to exploit structure known at canonicalization time. In
\texttt{cvxcore} the views of Table~\ref{tab:views-copies} also materialize explicit sparse
operators: a reshape builds a sparse identity matrix that is then multiplied.
\item \emph{Everything is sparse.} All coefficient data is treated as sparse: dense atom data
is converted to a sparse matrix on entry, and no operator carries a dense or block special
case, so dense blocks pay sparse-format overhead and no BLAS kernel (\S\ref{sec:bg-blas}) is
used.
\end{itemize}
Other design choices matter when comparing with \texttt{SparseDiffEngine}: each node stores a
dictionary of
local Jacobian blocks rather than a single global matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:local-vs-global}),
and the quadratic forms of the objective are removed by substitution and recovered in a
separate matrix stuffing pass that assembles the objective matrix $P$. In \S\ref{sec:Theme1}
we revisit these limitations and design choices, which motivate our implementation.

\section{Sparse derivatives from detected structure}
\label{sec:computing-derivatives}

The introduction (\S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives}) organized differentiation software by four choices:
the granularity of the expression graph, the mode of differentiation, the assembly of the result,
and its storage.
This section examines the assembly choice in detail, with emphasis on oracle assembly, the
standard of the literature \cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
Under oracle assembly, sparsity detection and graph coloring act as compression: they reduce the
number of oracle sweeps needed to recover the sparse matrix.
Table~\ref{tab:ad-landscape} gathers recent systems, and the section closes with the system
nearest this thesis, \texttt{CasADi}.

\paragraph{Scalar traces.}
Early AD tools such as ADOL-C \cite{griewank1996adolc} and CppAD \cite{bell2012cppad} record the
scalar graph as a runtime trace of every elementary operation
\cite{griewank2008evaluating, Naumann2011}.
Such a trace can be enormous: a single matrix--vector product $Ax$ unrolls into one scalar
multiplication for every entry of $A$, together with the additions that sum them, so it quickly
reaches millions of nodes for a realistic problem.

\paragraph{Oracle-based differentiation.}
The oracle of \S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives} returns the Jacobian-vector product (JVP) $Jv$ in forward
mode and the vector-Jacobian product (VJP) $J^\top w$ in reverse mode (for the Hessian, the
analogous Hessian-vector product $Hv$ comes from one forward sweep over one reverse sweep)
\cite{baydin2017ADsurvey}.
These matrix-vector products are obtained without ever forming $J$, each by one sweep of the
graph at about the cost of one function evaluation; borrowing terminology from numerical linear
algebra, this is also called the \emph{matrix-free} view of AD.
Figure~\ref{fig:forward-sweep} traces one forward sweep on a small graph.

\paragraph{Recovering the Jacobian.}
Recovering $J$ explicitly requires \emph{sweeping} the oracle over a sequence of seed vectors: the $n$
unit seeds recover it column by column in forward mode, and the $m$ unit seeds recover it row by row
in reverse mode (the Hessian is recovered the same way, though its symmetry permits fewer seeds)
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
Seeding the full identity recovers $J$ exactly, $JI = J$, so assembling a dense $J$ is simply
the oracle batched over the unit seeds.
When the Jacobian is sparse, most of each sweep is wasted on zeros, and the full identity is
prohibitively expensive; reducing the number of sweeps is the purpose of graph coloring.
Coloring operates on the oracle alone, independently of how the underlying graph is built,
so even fully vectorized frameworks such as JAX \cite{jax2018github}, whose local derivatives are
Jacobian blocks combined by the rules of matrix calculus \cite{magnus2019matrix}, rely on it when
they need an assembled sparse Jacobian.

\paragraph{Graph coloring.}
The number of sweeps drops when several seeds share one. Two columns of $J$ with no nonzero
in a common row are \emph{structurally orthogonal}, so one seed recovers both without their nonzeros
colliding. Finding the fewest such groups is a graph-coloring problem on the column intersection
graph, whose edges join columns that share a row
\cite{curtis1974estimation, coleman1983estimation, gebremedhin2005color}.
Figure~\ref{fig:jacobian-coloring} illustrates the compression: one sweep per color group replaces
one sweep per column.
The Hessian compresses
further: its symmetry admits dedicated star and acyclic colorings that use fewer colors than the
general Jacobian case \cite{Gebremedhin2009SparseHessians}.

\paragraph{Detect, compress, propagate.}
An oracle-based sparse Jacobian is obtained in three stages: \emph{detect} the sparsity pattern,
\emph{compress} it by coloring, and \emph{propagate} the compressed seeds of
Figure~\ref{fig:jacobian-coloring} through the oracle to recover the nonzero entries
\cite{hill2025sparser}. The pattern is computed once and reused, so its
cost is amortized across repeated evaluations of the same function. This scheme underlies sparse AD
in oracle-based tools such as \texttt{CasADi} (\S\ref{sec:casadi}).

\paragraph{Direct differentiation.}
Direct assembly (\S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives}) needs neither sweeps nor coloring: each node
carries its whole derivative object, and one pass over the graph combines them into $J$.
On a scalar graph the cost is prohibitive. The derivative work at a node is proportional to
the gradient it carries, not to the $O(1)$ operation it performs, and every intermediate
materializes a gradient of its own: the inner product $y = a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_k x_k$ unrolls
into $k-1$ running partial sums whose gradients grow from one nonzero to $k$, so direct
propagation costs $O(k^2)$ against $O(k)$ for one sweep. The affine expressions of
canonicalized problems are dominated by such sums.
Direct assembly on scalar graphs is therefore rare: \emph{vertex elimination} (also called
\emph{cross-country} accumulation) forms $J$ by eliminating the intermediate vertices of the
linearized graph, but the optimal elimination order is intractable
\cite{griewank2008evaluating, naumann2008optimal}, and the scalar symbolic graphs of
\texttt{CasADi} build derivative expressions node by node in the same spirit \cite{casadidocs}.
Vectorization removes the obstruction: a node is a whole matrix operation, its local
derivative is a structured block rather than a $0/1$ dependency, and the graph shrinks from
millions of scalar operations to a few structured nodes, so carrying a sparse matrix per node
is affordable. Recent works apply this idea in statistics \cite{kwok2025vectorised} and
optimal control \cite{zou2025vectorized}.
On a vectorized graph, direct propagation is cheaper when the local blocks are structured and
intermediate fill-in stays bounded; the oracle with coloring is cheaper when the intermediates
densify.
Which assembly is preferable follows the solver that consumes the result: the conic
solvers targeted in this thesis require the matrix explicitly as data, which the direct approach
produces.

\subsection{\texttt{CasADi}}
\label{sec:casadi}

\texttt{CasADi} \cite{andersson2019} is an integrated symbolic-AD framework for optimization
whose quadratic-programming interface extracts the explicit matrices $P$ and $A$ by AD on the
expression graph; it is the reference implementation of the detect--compress--propagate scheme
of this section in optimization software.

\paragraph{Expression types.}
Expressions come in two types \cite{casadidocs}. An \texttt{SX} graph is scalar, with exact
node-by-node sparsity, at the cost of a graph whose size grows with the problem data. An
\texttt{MX} graph is vectorized: nodes are whole matrix operations, so large models keep small
graphs. Either graph is compiled into a flat instruction
tape over a shared work vector, which enables common-subexpression elimination, constant
folding, and C code generation.

\paragraph{Jacobian assembly.}
Even on a vectorized graph, the Jacobian is reached through the oracle: a sweep over the tape
carries a batch of seed directions and returns their products $Jv$, not $J$ itself. Assembly
therefore follows the stages of this section: the sparsity is computed by a pattern propagation over
the tape, compressed by greedy distance-2 coloring, and the compressed seeds are pushed through
batched forward or reverse sweeps, with the mode chosen by a cost heuristic on the pattern's
shape. The Hessian is assembled as the Jacobian of the gradient, one forward sweep over the
reverse tape, with a star coloring. Pattern, coloring, and the compiled
derivative function are cached across evaluations. This is how
\texttt{CasADi}'s own \texttt{qpsol} interface recovers the problem data of a quadratic
program, by differentiating the model without invoking a solver:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import casadi as cs
z, th = cs.MX.sym("z", n), cs.MX.sym("th", p)
f, g = objective(z, th), slacks(z, th)  # lowered conic model
c = cs.substitute(cs.gradient(f, z), z, 0)
P = cs.jacobian(cs.gradient(f, z), z)   # Jacobian of the gradient
A = -cs.jacobian(g, z)
b = cs.substitute(g, z, 0)
data = cs.Function("data", [th], [P, c, A, b])
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{The dense-block worst case.}
A dense $m \times n$ block in the problem data makes every pair of its columns collide in some
row: the column intersection graph restricted to the block is a clique, no coloring can use
fewer than $\min(m,n)$ colors, and the sweep count grows to the full dimension of the block,
even when the assembled matrix is mostly sparse. Lowered conic
problems routinely embed dense blocks, so this worst case governs coefficient extraction in
the setting of this thesis.

\paragraph{References.}
The book by Griewank and Walther \cite{griewank2008evaluating} covers the theory and practice of
scalar AD, including the graph-elimination view of Jacobian accumulation; the book by Naumann
\cite{Naumann2011} focuses on implementation by operator overloading and source transformation.
The survey by Baydin et al.\ \cite{baydin2017ADsurvey} covers AD in machine learning.
For the combinatorial side, the survey of Gebremedhin et al.\ \cite{gebremedhin2005color} is the
standard entry point, and the illustrated guide of Hill and Dalle \cite{hill2025illustrated} is a
modern tutorial on the detection--coloring--compression scheme.
The \texttt{CasADi} paper \cite{andersson2019} covers its expression graphs, AD implementation,
and solver interfaces.

\section{Coefficient extraction from declared structure}
\label{sec:declared-structure}

The systems of the previous section detect the structure of the problem at runtime and recover
its coefficients through coloring. \texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP} need no detection: the
structure is declared up front and the coefficients are stored as the user types them, so
extraction reduces to reading off what is already known. This section examines both systems.

\subsection{\texttt{AMPL}}
\label{sec:ampl}

\texttt{AMPL} \cite{fourer1990ampl} is the oldest system in this review, and its architecture
still defines how most solvers receive nonlinear problems. The AMPL translator runs in its own
process: it parses the model and the data, instantiates every constraint, and writes the
resulting instance to a \texttt{.nl} file \cite{gay2005writing}, which the solver, a separate
executable linking the AMPL Solver Library (ASL), reads back \cite{gay1997hooking}.

\paragraph{The \texttt{.nl} format.}
The \texttt{.nl} file is an instance format, not a model format: sets and indexing are resolved
by the translator, and what remains is one flat record per objective and constraint. A header
states the dimensions of the problem and the nonzero counts of its Jacobian and gradients; the
body is a sequence of segments keyed by a leading letter. \texttt{C} and \texttt{O} segments
hold the nonlinear parts of constraints and objectives as expression graphs in Polish prefix
notation; \texttt{J} and \texttt{G} segments hold their linear parts as explicit lists of
(variable, coefficient) pairs; the remaining segments hold bounds and nonzero counts.

\paragraph{Example.}
The following quadratic program shows the syntax of \texttt{.nl} files.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Ampl]
var x; var y;
minimize obj: x^2 + x*y + 3*x;
subject to c1: x + 2*y >= 1;
\end{lstlisting}
The translator writes the following \texttt{.nl} file (ASCII encoding; annotations and
elisions ours):
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
g3 0 1 0     # text format; 9 more header lines follow
 2 1 1 0 0   # 2 vars, 1 constraint, 1 objective, 0 ranges, 0 eqns
...
r            # constraint bounds: c1 >= 1
2 1
...
O0 0         # minimize objective 0; nonlinear part x^2 + x*y
o0           #   add
o5           #     pow
v0           #       variable 0 (x)
n2           #       constant 2
...          #     multiply subtree of x*y
J0 2         # linear Jacobian row of c1: two entries
0 1          #   coefficient 1 on variable 0
1 2          #   coefficient 2 on variable 1
G0 2         # linear part of the objective gradient: 3x
0 3
1 0
\end{lstlisting}
The quadratic terms $x^2 + xy$ appear only as the expression graph of the \texttt{O0} segment;
every linear coefficient is spelled out in the \texttt{J} and \texttt{G} segments. For a linear
program, the file \emph{is} the problem data.

\paragraph{Solver-side evaluation.}
Inside the solver, ASL reads the linear coefficients and bounds directly from the file, so for
a linear or quadratic-over-linear instance this read is the whole extraction, at a cost linear
in the number of nonzeros. Only the rebuilt nonlinear graphs are differentiated, by
reverse-mode AD \cite{gay1991automatic}, with sparse Hessians assembled from partial
separability \cite{gay1996more}.

\paragraph{Advantages of the file format.}
The format has three benefits: any system that writes \texttt{.nl} reaches every solver that
links ASL; the format has stayed backward compatible since 1990; and an instance is an
ordinary file that can be archived, shipped to reproduce a bug, and rerun across machines.
The cost is the decoupling itself: every solve serializes the instance to disk and rebuilds
its expression graphs in the solver process.

\subsection{\texttt{JuMP}}
\label{sec:jump}

\texttt{JuMP} \cite{dunning2017jump, lubin2023jump10recent} is an algebraic modeling language
embedded in Julia; its abstraction layer between models and solvers is
\ac{MOI} \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}.
Where \texttt{AMPL} decouples modeler and solver through a
file, \texttt{JuMP} decouples them through a typed in-memory interface. For the linear,
quadratic, and conic problems considered here, coefficients are stored as the user types them,
and no differentiation occurs anywhere on the problem-data path.

\paragraph{Macros and term lists.}
The macros of \texttt{JuMP} rewrite each expression as it is entered: \texttt{@objective} and
\texttt{@constraint} build affine and quadratic expression objects term by term, merging
duplicates eagerly, so the model never holds an expression tree, only coefficient lists. These
lists are typed by MOI. A \texttt{VectorAffineFunction} is a list of (row, variable,
coefficient) terms plus a constant vector; a \texttt{ScalarQuadraticFunction} adds a list of
(variable, variable, coefficient) triples. A constraint pairs one such function with a set, for
instance a nonnegative orthant or a second-order cone: the functional conic form of
\S\ref{sec:bg-canonicalization}.
The constraint of the \texttt{AMPL} example above, entered as
\lstinline|@constraint(model, x + 2y >= 1)|, is stored as the pair
\begin{lstlisting}
ScalarAffineFunction([(1.0, x), (2.0, y)], 0.0)   in   GreaterThan(1.0)
\end{lstlisting}
a term list, a constant, and a set; the expression itself is gone once the macro returns.

\paragraph{Extraction.}
Extraction is serialization: when the model is passed to a solver, MOI copies each constraint
into the solver's own representation (\texttt{copy\_to}); solvers that consume matrix data use
the \texttt{MatrixOfConstraints} utility, which concatenates the stored term lists into one
sparse matrix in the solver's format. The cost of extraction is this copy: a pure
data-structure transformation, linear in the number of terms, with no analysis of any graph.
The assembled matrix is the same problem data that \texttt{CVXPY}'s matrix stuffing computes
(\S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}); \texttt{JuMP} arrives at it by copying stored coefficients
rather than by differentiating conic-form trees.

\paragraph{Bridges.}
When a solver does not support a constraint type natively, MOI reformulates it through its
\emph{bridge} system: a bridge is a structural rewrite from one function-set pair to another,
for instance from a rotated to a plain second-order cone, chosen by a shortest-path search over
the graph of available bridges \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}. Bridging manipulates stored
coefficients and adds auxiliary variables and constraints. This mirrors \texttt{CVXPY}'s
canonicalization (\S\ref{sec:canonicalization}) with one difference: \texttt{JuMP} rewrites
problems whose data is already in matrix form, while \texttt{CVXPY} must first produce that
data from a DCP atom tree, which is the work this thesis accelerates.

\texttt{JuMP} also has a nonlinear path, in which expression graphs are retained and
derivatives come from sparse AD in the style of \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}; it serves
general nonlinear solvers and is out of scope for this thesis.

\paragraph{References.}
The MathOptInterface paper \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface} is the best reference on the
design of \texttt{JuMP}'s solver abstraction layer and its bridge system, and the \ac{MOI}
and \texttt{JuMP} documentation \cite{moidocs, jumpdocs} cover the implementation details.
David Gay's technical reports \cite{gay1997hooking, gay2005writing} remain the primary
description of the \texttt{.nl} format and the \texttt{AMPL} solver interface.
