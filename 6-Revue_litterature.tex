\section{Matrix stuffing in \texttt{CVXPY}}
\label{sec:matrix-stuffing}

\texttt{CVXPY} takes the perspective of matrix calculus \cite{magnus2019matrix} for computing the
coefficient matrices of linear expressions; this is where most of the computational burden of
canonicalization lies. Matrix stuffing is named for what it does: the coefficients of every
canonicalized expression are stuffed into their designated slots in the sparse standard-form data
handed to the solver. The name suggests bookkeeping, but the coefficients it assembles are the
Jacobians and Hessians of the canonicalized expressions: matrix stuffing is a sparse derivative
computation, a view developed in \S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives} and \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}.

The section gives a brief summary of matrix calculus and a few examples, presents the conventions
of \texttt{CVXPY}'s backend, and closes with implementation details that become important when
contrasting
it with the engine of \S\ref{sec:Theme1}.

\paragraph{Matrix calculus.}
Matrix calculus extends differentiation to functions whose arguments and values are vectors and
matrices. Systematic rules for such derivatives go back to Dwyer and Macphail
\cite{dwyer1948symbolic}, and there are many ways to define the derivative of a matrix-valued
expression with respect to a matrix variable \cite{magnus2019matrix}. The simplest is to
vectorize: every expression is identified with
\BEQ\label{eq:vec}
\vect(X) = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & \cdots & x_{m1} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{mn} \end{bmatrix}^T,
\EEQ
the vector that stacks the columns of $X \in \reals^{m \times n}$, and the derivative of one
vectorized expression with respect to another is an ordinary Jacobian matrix. Under this
convention matrix operations become linear operators on the vectorized arguments, and their
Jacobians carry structure: for example
\BEQ\label{eq:vec-kron}
\vect(FX) = (I \otimes F)\,\vect(X),
\EEQ
so the matrix product $FX$ corresponds to the Kronecker-structured, block-diagonal Jacobian
$I \otimes F$. The convention is illustrated in \ref{fig:vec-variable}: as an expression, a
matrix variable is the identity map of its own entries, so its Jacobian is an identity block
whose columns are indexed by the entries of $\vect(X)$, and the Jacobian of the product $FX$ is
block-diagonal with one copy of $F$ per column of $X$.

\paragraph{Composing linear operators.}
After canonicalization every expression is affine, so each node of a conic-form tree can be
written in the vectorized form
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-form}
f(x) = A\,x + b
     = \begin{bmatrix} A & b \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where $x$ is the stacked vector of all variables, $A$ is the Jacobian of the node, constant
since $f$ is affine, and $b$ is a constant offset. Matrix stuffing computes the augmented matrix
$[A\ b]$ of every node in one bottom-up pass. A variable leaf contributes an identity block in
its own column slot, a constant leaf contributes its value to the offset column, and an interior
atom $g$ with arguments $f_1, \dots, f_k$ is a linear map $\sum_i L_i f_i$ whose constant
Jacobians $L_i$ apply directly to the augmented matrices of its children,
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule}
\begin{bmatrix} A & b \end{bmatrix}
  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} L_i \begin{bmatrix} A_i & b_i \end{bmatrix}.
\EEQ
Computing the problem data thus amounts to composing many linear operators over the tree: the
node matrices of the figures in this section are exactly these augmented blocks, and at the root
of the constraint tree the augmented matrix is the problem data $[A\ b]$ itself.

\paragraph{Views and copies.}
The simple operators act as \emph{views}, modifying the augmented matrix of their argument in
place: negation flips the sign of every stored entry, indexing keeps the selected rows, and
reshaping costs nothing at all, since it leaves the vectorization of the expression unchanged.
The operators that create \emph{copies} are mostly the bivariate atoms that carry data: one
operand of a matrix product, an elementwise multiplication or a division is constant under the
DCP rules, and the atom forms a matrix from that operand, multiplies it with the augmented
matrix of the other, and stores the product as new data. An expository subset is classified in
\ref{tab:views-copies}.

\paragraph{Local versus global derivatives.}
The augmented matrices of \eqref{eq:linop-form} can be laid out in two ways
(\ref{fig:local-vs-global}). \texttt{CVXPY} differentiates \emph{locally}, one variable at a time:
each
node stores one coefficient block per variable appearing beneath it, keyed by the variable's
identifier, and every operator is applied to each block in turn. The constant data is stored
under the key $-1$, a Python idiom for the last column, the offset $b$. The columns of a block
index only the entries of its own variable; only after the walk completes are the blocks shifted
to their variables' column offsets and concatenated into the full matrix $[A\ b]$.

The alternative is a \emph{global} derivative: a single Jacobian per node whose columns span the
whole stacked vector $x$ from the outset. The local layout keeps the blocks small and skips
variables absent from a subtree, but every operator runs once per stored block; the engine of
\S\ref{sec:Theme1} takes the global route.

\paragraph{Parameters and tensors.}
DPP restricts parameters to enter expressions affinely, so the problem data is affine in the
parameters \cite{agrawal2019dpp}, and \texttt{CVXPY} accounts for this with a third tensor axis:
every
coefficient block stores one slice per
parameter entry plus one slice for the constant part, and the operators of the walk apply to
each slice. Flattening the slices of the root yields the cached \emph{parameter map}
\BEQ\label{eq:param-maps}
\begin{bmatrix} c \\ d \end{bmatrix} = Q\,\tilde\theta,
\qquad
\tilde\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \theta \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
shown in \ref{fig:param-map-dpp} for the objective of problem \eqref{eq:example}; the constraint
data $[A\ b]$ is recovered the same way from its own map. After a parameter change, a re-solve
costs a single sparse matrix--vector product $Q\tilde\theta$.

\paragraph{The \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path.}
Problems that fall outside the DPP grammar, or that opt out of it by solving with
\texttt{ignore\_dpp=True}, take a simpler path. The reduction \texttt{EvalParams} replaces each
parameter leaf by a constant holding its current value, and the parameter-free tree that results
is stuffed as if the problem had no parameters (\ref{fig:param-map-evalparams}). The path is
fully general, since arbitrary functions of parameters reduce to constants, but every change of
$\theta$ repeats the whole symbolic walk even though the sparsity pattern of the problem data
never changes. This is the setting the engine of \S\ref{sec:Theme1} improves.

\paragraph{Code generation.}
A third option is to compile the problem ahead of time. \texttt{CVXPYgen} emits problem-specific C
code
that embeds the cached parameter map \eqref{eq:param-maps} of a DPP problem
\cite{schaller2022cvxpygen}, continuing an earlier line of work on code generation for embedded

compare against it: code generation is an offline step that fixes the problem structure at
compile time, a different deployment model from a runtime backend that must accept arbitrary
problems.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
Three implementation details, shared by \texttt{CVXPY}'s two backends, the C++ \texttt{cvxcore} and
the
\texttt{SciPy} backend, stand out as limitations of the current approach; the engine of
\S\ref{sec:Theme1} does each differently.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{External sparse libraries.} Both backends delegate every matrix operation to a
general-purpose sparse library, Eigen \cite{guennebaud2010eigen} for \texttt{cvxcore} and
\texttt{scipy.sparse} \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth} for the \texttt{SciPy} backend. The storage formats and
kernels are fixed by the library, leaving no
room to exploit structure known at canonicalization time.
\item \emph{Everything is sparse.} All coefficient data is treated as sparse: dense atom data
is converted to a sparse matrix on entry, and no operator carries a dense or block special
case, so dense blocks pay sparse-format overhead and no BLAS kernel (\S\ref{sec:bg-blas}) is
ever used.
\item \emph{Quadratic objectives are separate.} Each quadratic form is swapped for a dummy
variable, and the surrounding affine expression is stuffed by the same walk. The matrices of
the quadratic forms are then scaled by the resulting coefficients and reassembled into the
global objective matrix $P$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Sparse derivatives by structure discovery}
\label{sec:computing-derivatives}

The introduction (\S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives}) presented the elementary methods for computing
derivatives: finite differences, symbolic differentiation, and AD with its forward and reverse
modes.
The software systems implementing AD differ in four basic choices: whether the
function is treated as a \emph{scalar}
or \emph{vectorized} expression, whether derivatives are propagated \emph{forward} or in
\emph{reverse}, whether the Jacobian or Hessian is assembled \emph{directly} or accessed through an
\emph{oracle} of matrix--vector products, and whether the resulting matrix is \emph{sparse} or
\emph{dense}.

The problem data for an optimization model is often large and sparse, so we place
particular emphasis on computing sparse derivatives in this section.
The standard approach in the literature is scalar and oracle-based differentiation
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}, where graph coloring is used to recover a sparse matrix in far fewer
oracle evaluations.
This thesis instead proposes to compute these sparse derivatives directly on a vectorized graph
whose sparsity pattern follows from the structure of the operations, so no coloring is needed.

The remainder of the section presents the standard scalar and oracle-based pipeline, then the
vectorized and direct alternatives, and closes by gathering a range of relevant works,
organizing them in a table according to their particular approach to differentiation.

\paragraph{Scalar differentiation.}
The earliest and most foundational form of AD operates on a \emph{scalar} computational graph, where
every node is a single elementary operation (an addition, a multiplication, or a univariate
function such as $\sin$ or $\exp$), and every edge carries the corresponding scalar partial
derivative \cite{griewank2008evaluating, Naumann2011}.
Since each elementary operation has a simple and known derivative, the chain rule reduces to
multiplying and summing scalars along the graph.
Early AD tools such as ADOL-C \cite{griewank1996adolc} and CppAD \cite{bell2012cppad} form the
scalar graph by recording a runtime trace of every elementary operation. Such a trace can be enormous:
a single matrix--vector product $Ax$ unrolls into one scalar
multiplication for every entry of $A$, together with the additions that sum them, so it quickly
reaches millions of nodes for a realistic problem.

\paragraph{Oracle-based differentiation.}
Instead of the Jacobian $J$ itself, AD returns its action on a vector: the Jacobian-vector product
(JVP) $Jv$ in forward mode, and the vector-Jacobian product (VJP) $J^\top w$ in reverse mode (for
the Hessian, the analogous Hessian-vector product $Hv$ comes from one forward pass over one
reverse pass) \cite{baydin2017ADsurvey}.
These matrix-vector products are obtained without ever forming $J$, each at about the cost of one
function evaluation. Borrowing terminology from numerical linear algebra, we call this the
\emph{matrix-free}, or \emph{oracle}, view of AD.
The oracle is sufficient for many applications; they never form the matrix and use only its action
on vectors, as in backpropagation for training neural networks or Krylov methods for linear systems.

\paragraph{Recovering the Jacobian.}
Recovering $J$ explicitly requires \emph{probing} the oracle with a sequence of seed vectors: the $n$
unit seeds recover it column by column in forward mode, and the $m$ unit seeds recover it row by row
in reverse mode (the Hessian is recovered the same way, though its symmetry permits fewer seeds)
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
When the Jacobian has many rows or columns this becomes prohibitively expensive, and reducing the
number of probes is exactly the purpose of the graph-coloring methods described next.
Crucially, coloring operates on the oracle alone, independently of how the underlying graph is built,
so even fully vectorized frameworks rely on it when they need an assembled sparse Jacobian.

\paragraph{Graph coloring.}
The cost of probing drops when several seeds share a single pass. Two columns of $J$ with no nonzero
in a common row are \emph{structurally orthogonal}, so one seed recovers both without their nonzeros
colliding. Finding the fewest such groups is a graph-coloring problem on the column intersection
graph, whose edges join columns that share a row
\cite{curtis1974estimation, coleman1983estimation, gebremedhin2005color}. The Hessian compresses
further: its symmetry admits dedicated star and acyclic colorings that use fewer colors than the
general Jacobian case \cite{Gebremedhin2009SparseHessians}.

\paragraph{Sparse derivatives.}
An oracle-based sparse Jacobian is obtained in three stages: \emph{detect} the sparsity pattern,
\emph{compress} it by coloring, and \emph{propagate} the compressed seeds through the oracle to
recover the nonzero entries \cite{hill2025sparser}. The pattern is computed once and reused, so its
cost is amortized across repeated evaluations of the same function. This pipeline underlies sparse AD
in oracle-based tools such as \texttt{CasADi} \cite{andersson2019}, which we examine at the end of
this section.

\paragraph{Vectorized differentiation.}
More recent frameworks coarsen the graph so that each node is a whole vector or matrix operation
rather than a single scalar. The local derivative of such a node is then a matrix, a block of the
Jacobian, and these blocks are combined by the rules of matrix calculus \cite{magnus2019matrix}.
The resulting graph has far fewer nodes than the scalar trace, which lets frameworks such as JAX
\cite{jax2018github} differentiate large programs efficiently. Vectorization alone, however, does not
change the assembly: these frameworks still expose only the oracle, and still rely on coloring when
they need an assembled sparse Jacobian.

\paragraph{Direct differentiation.}
Direct methods go a step further and abandon the oracle altogether. Rather than returning products,
they propagate the local Jacobian blocks themselves through the graph and combine them to form $J$
directly, so neither probing nor coloring is needed. Vectorization is the enabler: it gives each
block algebraic structure, whereas a scalar node carries only a $0/1$ dependency. Recent works apply
this idea in statistics \cite{kwok2025vectorised} and optimal control \cite{zou2025vectorized}.
Direct propagation wins when the local blocks are well structured and intermediate fill-in stays
bounded, while coloring through the oracle can still be cheaper when the intermediates densify.
Which assembly is preferable ultimately follows the solver that consumes the result: the conic
solvers targeted in this thesis require the matrix explicitly as data, which is exactly what the
direct approach produces.

\subsection{\texttt{SparseConnectivityTracer}}

\section{Coefficient extraction from declared structure}
\label{sec:declared-structure}

A useful question sorts every modeling system: are the coefficients of the problem data
\emph{stored} as the user typed them, \emph{differentiated} out of an expression graph, or
\emph{detected} at runtime and recovered through coloring?
The previous section covered the third answer; this section examines two mature systems that
avoid discovery altogether, \texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP}. Both declare the structure of the
problem up front, so extraction reduces to reading off what is already known.

\subsection{\texttt{AMPL}}
\label{sec:ampl}

\texttt{AMPL} \cite{fourer1990ampl} is accessed from Python through the \texttt{amplpy} API
\cite{amplpy}: the model is written to a compact binary \texttt{.nl} file \cite{gay2005writing}
whose expression graphs the AMPL Solver Library (ASL) rebuilds inside the solver process
\cite{gay1997hooking}.

\subsection{\texttt{JuMP}}
\label{sec:jump}

\texttt{JuMP} \cite{dunning2017jump, lubin2023jump10recent} is an algebraic modeling language
embedded in Julia and built on the \texttt{MathOptInterface} (MOI) abstraction layer
\cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}. For the linear, quadratic, and conic problems considered here,
coefficients are stored as the user types them, and no differentiation occurs anywhere on the
problem-data path.
