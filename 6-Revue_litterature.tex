\Chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}\label{sec:RevLitt}

This chapter reviews the implementation of \texttt{CVXPY}'s matrix stuffing
(\S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}) and of other modelling language approaches, from sparse AD such as
\texttt{CasADi} (\S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}) to coefficient extraction in \texttt{AMPL}
and \texttt{JuMP} (\S\ref{sec:declared-structure}). These modelling languages all produce the
same output as \texttt{CVXPY}, the solver problem data, but differ in the inputs they accept
and in how they compute the coefficients. For example, \texttt{CasADi} accepts vectorized expressions as input, but differentiates
them through matrix--vector products, so the structure of the derivatives is never materialized
and must be detected at runtime, over many sweeps of the expression graph.
\texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP} let the user declare vectorized expressions but scalarize them
when processing the data into coefficients.
On the other hand, the canonicalization backends of \texttt{CVXPY} keep the expressions
vectorized and need no detection step (for this reason, we only compare against these backends
in Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme2}).

\section{Matrix stuffing in \texttt{CVXPY}}\label{sec:matrix-stuffing}

Matrix stuffing converts the conic-form expression trees into the problem data. After
canonicalization every expression is affine, so the Jacobian of
each node is a constant matrix, and matrix calculus \cite{magnus2019matrix} gives the Jacobian
of each atom in closed form. Computing the problem data reduces to composing these linear
operators over the tree. This section reviews these derivative rules, along with two design
choices of the \texttt{CVXPY} implementation, the storage of the coefficients during the
pass and the caching of the problem data across parameter changes. Finally,
we describe \texttt{CVXPY}'s current backends and some of their limitations.

% \paragraph{Matrix calculus.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Matrix calculus extends differentiation to functions whose arguments and values are vectors and
matrices. Systematic rules for such derivatives date back to the 1940s \cite{dwyer1948symbolic}.
The simplest convention is to vectorize \cite{magnus2019matrix}: every expression is identified
with
\BEQ\label{eq:vec}
\vect(\mmat{X}) = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & \cdots & x_{m1} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{mn} \end{bmatrix}^\top,
\EEQ
the vector that stacks the columns of $\mmat{X} \in \reals^{m \times n}$, and the derivative of one
vectorized expression with respect to another is an ordinary Jacobian matrix. Under this
convention, matrix operations become linear operators on the vectorized arguments, and their
Jacobians are structured. For example, the matrix product $\mmat{F}\mmat{X}$ has the
Kronecker-structured, block-diagonal 
Jacobian $\mmat{I} \otimes \mmat{F}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:vec-variable}).

\subsection{Composing linear operators}\label{sec:ms-composing}
Every node of a conic-form tree, not only the root, computes an affine function of the problem
variables and can be written in the vectorized form
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-form}
f(\mvec{z}) = \mmat{A}_f\,\mvec{z} + \mvec{b}_f
     = \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
      \mvec{z} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where $\mvec{z}$ is the stacked vector of all the problem variables, $\mmat{A}_f$ is the
Jacobian of the node's expression $f$ with respect to $\mvec{z}$ and $\mvec{b}_f$ is a
constant vector. Stacking the augmented matrices of the constraint trees yields the problem
data $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$ of \eqref{e:quad-conic-form}.

Matrix stuffing computes the augmented matrix in one bottom-up pass over the
tree. A variable leaf contributes an identity block and a constant leaf contributes its value
to the last column (Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing}). An interior node with a single argument
applies a constant linear operator $\mmat{L}$ to the argument, which has the affine form
\eqref{eq:linop-form} with augmented matrix $[\mmat{A}_1\ \mvec{b}_1]$; applying $\mmat{L}$ gives
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule-unary}
\begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix}
  = \mmat{L} \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_1 & \mvec{b}_1 \end{bmatrix}.
\EEQ
The operator $\mmat{L}$ is the Jacobian of the node with respect to its argument, so
\eqref{eq:linop-chain-rule-unary} is exactly the chain rule of matrix calculus.
A node with $k$ arguments applies one operator per argument,
$f = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mmat{L}_i f_i$, which gives
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule}
\begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix}
  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mmat{L}_i \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_i & \mvec{b}_i \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
so the node costs one matrix product per argument.

% \paragraph{Views and copies.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
In \texttt{CVXPY}, the linear operators divide into views and copies. A view performs a
simple manipulation of the coefficients of its argument. Atoms with two arguments, such as
\texttt{matmul} and \texttt{multiply}, make copies instead. One argument is constant and
the other is variable-dependent. The operator is a constant matrix built from the constant
argument. Its product with the augmented matrix of the variable-dependent argument is
stored as a new coefficient matrix.
Table~\ref{tab:views-copies} classifies a subset of the operators.

% \paragraph{Local versus global derivatives.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The augmented matrices of \eqref{eq:linop-form} can be stored in two ways
(Figure~\ref{fig:local-vs-global}). In practice, \texttt{CVXPY} stores them locally. Each node keeps
one coefficient block per variable appearing beneath it, keyed by the variable's identifier, and
every operator is applied to each block in turn. A block's columns index only the entries of its
own variable, so the column offsets are not needed during the pass. The constant data is stored
under the key $-1$ and becomes the last column of the assembled matrix. Only after the pass
completes are the blocks shifted to their variables' column offsets and concatenated into the
full matrix $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$.

In the alternative, global layout, drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing}, each node stores
one matrix whose columns span the
whole stacked vector $\mvec{z}$, and the column offsets are applied from the start rather than
at the
end. The local layout keeps the blocks small and skips the variables absent from a subtree, but
every operator must be applied to every stored block.

\subsection{Parameter caching}\label{sec:ms-parameters}
% \paragraph{Parameters and tensors.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
In a problem family the structure is fixed and only the numeric parameter values change
between solves. \ac{DPP} requires the problem data to be affine in the parameters
\cite{agrawal2019dpp}. \texttt{CVXPY} handles this by adding a third axis to every coefficient
block, indexed by the parameter entries, and running the same pass on the resulting tensors.
The roots then yield the cached parameter maps
\BEQ\label{eq:param-maps}
\mvec{c} = \mmat{Q}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta},
\qquad
\vect([\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]) = \mmat{R}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta},
\qquad
\gvec{\tilde\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \gvec{\theta} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where the objective map $\mmat{Q}$ is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-dpp} for
problem \eqref{eq:example}. After a parameter change, a
re-solve costs one sparse matrix--vector product per map.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
$
\mmat{Q} =
\begin{blockarray}{cccc}
        & \mvec{g}^\top & \mu & 1 \\
\begin{block}{c[ccc]}
  t_1   & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & \plotzero & 1         \\
  t_2   & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & 1         & \plotzero \\
  \mvec{x}     & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & \plotzero & \plotzero \\
\end{block}
\end{blockarray}
\qquad
\mvec{c} = \mmat{Q}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta}
= \begin{bmatrix}1\\ \mu\\ \plotzero\end{bmatrix}
$
\caption{The DPP path on the objective data of problem \eqref{eq:example}: the parameter map
$\mmat{Q}$
is built and cached once, and each re-solve recovers $\mvec{c}$ with the single sparse
product
$\mmat{Q}\gvec{\tilde\theta}$.}
\label{fig:param-map-dpp}
\end{figure}

% \paragraph{The \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Problems that fall outside the DPP grammar, or that opt out of it with
\texttt{ignore\_dpp=True}, take a simpler path. The reduction \texttt{EvalParams} replaces each
parameter leaf by a constant holding its current value, and the resulting parameter-free tree
goes through the pass as if the problem had no parameters
(Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-evalparams}). The result is the same objective data $\mvec{c}$ as in
Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-dpp}, now obtained by repeating the pass instead of applying the
cached product $\mmat{Q}\gvec{\tilde\theta}$. The path is fully general, because arbitrary
functions of
parameters reduce to constants, but every change of $\gvec{\theta}$ repeats the whole
pass even though the sparsity pattern of the problem data never changes.

% \paragraph{The three backends.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
% (subsection heading "The canonicalization backends" merged into Parameter caching, 2026-08-03)
\texttt{CVXPY} implements the pass in three canonicalization backends that differ in how they
represent the parameter axis \cite{cvxpydocs}. The C++ backend \texttt{cvxcore} keeps one sparse matrix per
parameter slice and loops over the slices at every operator. The \texttt{SciPy} backend stacks
the slices vertically into a single strided two-dimensional sparse matrix.
The \texttt{COO} backend does not form the tensor explicitly, instead
it stores each nonzero with an explicit parameter index alongside rows and columns.

% \paragraph{Code generation.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
When canonicalization remains too slow, \texttt{CVXPYgen} can compile the problem ahead of
time. It emits problem-specific C code that embeds the cached parameter maps
\eqref{eq:param-maps} of a DPP problem \cite{schaller2022cvxpygen}, continuing an earlier line
of work on code generation for embedded second-order cone programs \cite{chu2013codegen}.

% \paragraph{Implementation details.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
All three backends share two limitations. Firstly, they delegate their matrix operations to a
general-purpose sparse library, Eigen \cite{guennebaud2010eigen} in \texttt{cvxcore} and
\texttt{scipy.sparse} \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth} in the Python backends. Although the
\texttt{COO} backend
stores no \texttt{scipy.sparse} matrices, it still converts to them for sparse operations such
as matrix products and Kronecker products. The storage formats and kernels are fixed by the
library, so structure known at canonicalization time cannot be exploited. In
\texttt{cvxcore} the views of Table~\ref{tab:views-copies} also materialize explicit sparse
operators. For example, a reshape builds a sparse identity matrix that is then multiplied.

Secondly, all coefficient data is treated as sparse. Dense atom data
is converted to a sparse matrix on entry, and no operator implements a dense or block special
case, so dense blocks incur sparse-format overhead and no BLAS kernel (\S\ref{sec:bg-blas}) is
used. Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme1} returns to these limitations.

\section{Sparse derivatives from detected structure}
\label{sec:computing-derivatives}

The introduction (\S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives}) organized differentiation software by four choices.
We focus here on the assembly choice, and in particular on matrix-free assembly, the standard
approach in the literature \cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
The input is an arbitrary differentiable program, the most general of this chapter, so the
structure of the derivatives is unknown until it is detected from the graph.
Under matrix-free assembly, sparsity detection and graph colouring reduce the
number of seeded sweeps needed to recover the sparse matrix.

\subsection{Matrix-free differentiation}\label{sec:matrix-free-diff}
% \paragraph{Scalar traces.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Early AD tools such as ADOL-C \cite{griewank1996adolc} and CppAD \cite{bell2012cppad} record the
scalar graph as a runtime trace of every elementary operation
\cite{griewank2008evaluating, Naumann2011}.
A single matrix--vector product $\mmat{A}\mvec{x}$ unrolls into one scalar
multiplication for every entry of $\mmat{A}$, together with the additions that sum them, so the
trace of a realistic problem reaches millions of nodes
(Figure~\ref{fig:scalar-vs-vectorized}).

% \paragraph{Matrix-free differentiation.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
A \emph{matrix-free} method returns only the action of the derivative: the Jacobian-vector
product (JVP) $\mmat{J}\mvec{v}$ in forward mode and the vector-Jacobian product (VJP)
$\mmat{J}^\top \mvec{w}$ in reverse mode (for the Hessian, the analogous Hessian-vector product
$\mmat{H}\mvec{v}$ comes from one forward sweep over one reverse sweep) \cite{baydin2017ADsurvey}.
These matrix-vector products are obtained without ever forming $\mmat{J}$, each by one sweep of
the graph at about the cost of one function evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:ad-modes}); the term
is borrowed from numerical linear algebra.

% \paragraph{Recovering the Jacobian.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Recovering $\mmat{J}$ explicitly requires \emph{sweeping} over a sequence of seed vectors. The $n$
unit seeds recover it column by column in forward mode, and the $m$ unit seeds recover it row by row
in reverse mode (the Hessian is recovered the same way, though its symmetry permits fewer seeds)
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
When the Jacobian is sparse, most of each sweep is wasted on zeros, and sweeping all the unit
seeds is prohibitively expensive; graph colouring is used to reduce the number of sweeps.

\subsection{Graph colouring}\label{sec:graph-colouring}
% \paragraph{Graph colouring.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The number of sweeps drops when several columns share one seed
(Figure~\ref{fig:jacobian-coloring}). Two columns of $\mmat{J}$ with no nonzero
in a common row are \emph{structurally orthogonal}, so one seed recovers both without their nonzeros
colliding. Finding the fewest such groups is a graph-colouring problem on the column intersection
graph, whose edges join columns that share a row
\cite{curtis1974estimation, coleman1983estimation, gebremedhin2005color}.
The Hessian compresses
further because its symmetry admits dedicated star and acyclic colourings, which use fewer
colours than the general Jacobian case \cite{Gebremedhin2009SparseHessians}.
Colouring operates on the matrix-free products alone, so even fully vectorized frameworks such
as JAX \cite{jax2018github} rely on it when they need an assembled sparse Jacobian.

% \paragraph{Detect, compress, propagate.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
A matrix-free sparse Jacobian is obtained in three stages: \emph{detect} the sparsity pattern,
\emph{compress} it by colouring, and \emph{propagate} the compressed seeds of
Figure~\ref{fig:jacobian-coloring} through seeded sweeps to recover the nonzero entries
\cite{hill2025sparser}. The pattern is computed once and reused, so its
cost is amortized across repeated evaluations of the same function. This scheme underlies sparse AD
in matrix-free tools such as \texttt{CasADi} (\S\ref{sec:casadi}).

% \paragraph{Direct differentiation.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The alternative is \emph{direct} assembly, which forms $\mmat{J}$ itself and needs neither
sweeps nor colouring. Each node stores its whole local derivative, and one pass over the graph
combines them into $\mmat{J}$.
On a scalar graph the stored derivatives grow with every intermediate and the cost is
prohibitive; there the method is known as \emph{vertex elimination}, and its optimal order is
intractable \cite{griewank2008evaluating, naumann2008optimal}.
On a vectorized graph the local derivatives are structured blocks and the graph has few
nodes, so storing a sparse matrix per node is affordable; recent works apply this in
statistics \cite{kwok2025vectorised} and optimal control \cite{zou2025vectorized}.
The conic solvers targeted in this thesis consume the matrix explicitly, which direct assembly
produces.

\subsection{\texttt{CasADi}}
\label{sec:casadi}

\texttt{CasADi} \cite{andersson2019} is a symbolic framework for nonlinear optimization built
around AD. The user writes vectorized expressions, and every matrix the solvers consume is
obtained by differentiating them. For example, the problem data of a quadratic program is
extracted as the
derivatives of its objective and constraints. The extraction follows the
detect--compress--propagate scheme of this section; among optimization software,
\texttt{CasADi} is its most complete implementation.

% \paragraph{Expression types.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Expressions come in two types \cite{casadidocs}. An \texttt{SX} graph is scalar, with exact
node-by-node sparsity, at the cost of a graph whose size grows with the problem data. An
\texttt{MX} graph is vectorized. Its nodes are whole matrix operations, so the graph stays
small even when the model is large. Either graph is compiled into a flat instruction
tape over a shared work vector, which enables common-subexpression elimination, constant
folding, and C code generation.

% \paragraph{Jacobian assembly.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Even on a vectorized graph, the Jacobian is computed matrix-free. A sweep over the tape
propagates a batch of seed directions and returns the products $\mmat{J}\mvec{v}$ rather than $\mmat{J}$ itself. The
sparsity is computed by a pattern propagation over
the tape, compressed by greedy distance-2 colouring, and the compressed seeds are pushed through
batched forward or reverse sweeps, with the mode chosen by a cost heuristic on the pattern's
shape. The Hessian is assembled as the Jacobian of the gradient, one forward sweep over the
reverse tape, with a star colouring. Pattern, colouring, and the compiled
derivative function are cached across evaluations. This is how
\texttt{CasADi}'s own \texttt{qpsol} interface recovers the problem data of a quadratic
program, by differentiating the model without invoking a solver:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import casadi as cs
z, th = cs.MX.sym("z", n), cs.MX.sym("th", p)
f, g = objective(z, th), slacks(z, th)  # lowered conic model
c = cs.substitute(cs.gradient(f, z), z, 0)
P = cs.jacobian(cs.gradient(f, z), z)   # Jacobian of the gradient
A = -cs.jacobian(g, z)
b = cs.substitute(g, z, 0)
data = cs.Function("data", [th], [P, c, A, b])
\end{lstlisting}

% \paragraph{The dense-block worst case.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
A dense $m \times n$ block in the problem data makes every pair of its columns collide in some
row, so the column intersection graph restricted to the block is a clique and no colouring can
use fewer than $\min(m,n)$ colours. The sweep count therefore grows to the full dimension of
the block, even when the assembled matrix is mostly sparse. Lowered conic
problems routinely contain dense blocks, so this worst case determines the cost of coefficient
extraction in the setting of this thesis. Vectorized input does not reduce the number of
sweeps, because the structure known to the modelling layer is only recovered at runtime.

% \paragraph{References.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The book by Griewank and Walther \cite{griewank2008evaluating} covers the theory and practice of
scalar AD, including the graph-elimination view of Jacobian accumulation; the book by Naumann
\cite{Naumann2011} focuses on implementation by operator overloading and source transformation.
The survey by Baydin et al.\ \cite{baydin2017ADsurvey} covers AD in machine learning.
For the combinatorial side, the survey of Gebremedhin et al.\ \cite{gebremedhin2005color} is the
standard entry point, and the illustrated guide of Hill and Dalle \cite{hill2025illustrated} is a
modern tutorial on the detection--colouring--compression scheme.
The \texttt{CasADi} paper \cite{andersson2019} covers its expression graphs, AD implementation,
and solver interfaces.

\section{Coefficient extraction from declared structure}
\label{sec:declared-structure}

The systems of the previous section detect the structure of the problem at runtime and recover
its coefficients through colouring. \texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP} need no detection, because
the structure is declared up front and the coefficients are stored as the user types them;
extraction reduces to reading the stored coefficients back
(Table~\ref{tab:modeling-systems}).

\subsection{\texttt{AMPL}}
\label{sec:ampl}

\texttt{AMPL} \cite{fourer1990ampl} is the oldest system in this review, and its architecture
still defines how most solvers receive nonlinear problems. The AMPL translator runs in its own
process. It parses the model and the data, instantiates every constraint, and writes the
resulting instance to a \texttt{.nl} file \cite{gay2005writing}, which the solver, a separate
executable linking the \ac{ASL}, reads back \cite{gay1997hooking}.

% \paragraph{The \texttt{.nl} format.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The \texttt{.nl} file is an instance format rather than a model format. Sets and indexing are
resolved by the translator, and what remains is one flat record per objective and constraint. A header
states the dimensions of the problem and the nonzero counts of its Jacobian and gradients; the
body is a sequence of segments keyed by a leading letter. \texttt{C} and \texttt{O} segments
hold the nonlinear parts of constraints and objectives as expression graphs in Polish prefix
notation; \texttt{J} and \texttt{G} segments hold their linear parts as explicit lists of
(variable, coefficient) pairs; the remaining segments hold bounds and nonzero counts.

% \paragraph{Example.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The following quadratic program shows the syntax of \texttt{.nl} files.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Ampl]
var x; var y;
minimize obj: x^2 + x*y + 3*x;
subject to c1: x + 2*y >= 1;
\end{lstlisting}
The translator writes the following \texttt{.nl} file (ASCII encoding; annotations and
elisions ours):
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
g3 0 1 0     # text format; 9 more header lines follow
 2 1 1 0 0   # 2 vars, 1 constraint, 1 objective, 0 ranges, 0 eqns
...
r            # constraint bounds: c1 >= 1
2 1
...
O0 0         # minimize objective 0; nonlinear part x^2 + x*y
o0           #   add
o5           #     pow
v0           #       variable 0 (x)
n2           #       constant 2
...          #     multiply subtree of x*y
J0 2         # linear Jacobian row of c1: two entries
0 1          #   coefficient 1 on variable 0
1 2          #   coefficient 2 on variable 1
G0 2         # linear part of the objective gradient: 3x
0 3
1 0
\end{lstlisting}
The quadratic terms $x^2 + xy$ appear only as the expression graph of the \texttt{O0} segment;
every linear coefficient is spelled out in the \texttt{J} and \texttt{G} segments. For a linear
program, the file therefore contains the complete problem data.

% \paragraph{Solver-side evaluation.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Inside the solver, ASL reads the linear coefficients and bounds directly from the file, so for
a linear or quadratic-over-linear instance extraction consists only of this read, at a cost
linear in the number of nonzeros. Only the rebuilt nonlinear graphs are differentiated, by
reverse-mode AD \cite{gay1991automatic}, with sparse Hessians assembled from partial
separability \cite{gay1996more}.

% \paragraph{Advantages of the file format.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The format has three benefits: any system that writes \texttt{.nl} reaches every solver that
links ASL; the format has stayed backward compatible since 1990; and an instance is an
ordinary file that can be archived, shipped to reproduce a bug, and rerun across machines.
The decoupling also has a cost. Every solve serializes the instance to disk and rebuilds
its expression graphs in the solver process.

\subsection{\texttt{JuMP}}
\label{sec:jump}

\texttt{JuMP} \cite{dunning2017jump, lubin2023jump10recent} is an algebraic modelling language
embedded in Julia; its abstraction layer between models and solvers is
\ac{MOI} \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}.
Where \texttt{AMPL} decouples modeller and solver through a
file, \texttt{JuMP} decouples them through a typed in-memory interface.

% \paragraph{Macros and term lists.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The macros of \texttt{JuMP} rewrite each expression as it is entered. \texttt{@objective} and
\texttt{@constraint} build affine and quadratic expression objects term by term, merging
duplicates eagerly, so the model stores coefficient lists instead of an expression tree. These
lists are typed by MOI. A \texttt{VectorAffineFunction} is a list of (row, variable,
coefficient) terms plus a constant vector; a \texttt{ScalarQuadraticFunction} adds a list of
(variable, variable, coefficient) triples. A constraint pairs one such function with a set, for
instance a nonnegative orthant or a second-order cone. This is the functional conic form of
\S\ref{sec:bg-canonicalization}.
The constraint of the \texttt{AMPL} example, entered as
\lstinline|@constraint(model, x + 2y >= 1)|, is stored as the pair
\begin{lstlisting}
ScalarAffineFunction([(1.0, x), (2.0, y)], 0.0)   in   GreaterThan(1.0)
\end{lstlisting}
a term list, a constant, and a set; the expression itself is not retained after the macro
returns.

% \paragraph{Extraction.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
When the model is passed to a solver, MOI copies each constraint
into the solver's own representation (\texttt{copy\_to}). Solvers that consume matrix data use
the \texttt{MatrixOfConstraints} utility, which concatenates the stored term lists into one
sparse matrix in the solver's format. The cost of extraction is the cost of this copy, which
is linear in the number of terms and requires no analysis of any graph.
The assembled matrix is the same problem data that \texttt{CVXPY}'s matrix stuffing computes
(\S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}); \texttt{JuMP} arrives at it by copying stored coefficients
rather than by differentiating conic-form trees.
% TODO(oscar): confirm copy_to + MatrixOfConstraints is the canonical path for conic solver
% wrappers (SCS.jl, Clarabel.jl), and whether any differentiation can occur on this path.

% \paragraph{Bridges.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
When a solver does not support a constraint type natively, MOI reformulates it through its
\emph{bridge} system. A bridge is a structural rewrite from one function-set pair to another,
for instance from a rotated to a plain second-order cone, chosen by a shortest-path search over
the graph of available bridges \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}. Bridging manipulates stored
coefficients and adds auxiliary variables and constraints. It is similar to
\texttt{CVXPY}'s canonicalization (\S\ref{sec:canonicalization}), except that
\texttt{JuMP} rewrites
problems whose data is already in matrix form, while \texttt{CVXPY} must first produce that
data from a DCP atom tree; producing this data is the work this thesis accelerates.

\texttt{JuMP} also has a nonlinear path, in which expression graphs are retained and
derivatives come from sparse AD in the style of \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}; it serves
general nonlinear solvers and is out of scope for this thesis.

% \paragraph{References.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The MathOptInterface paper \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface} is the best reference on the
design of \texttt{JuMP}'s solver abstraction layer and its bridge system, and the \ac{MOI}
and \texttt{JuMP} documentation \cite{moidocs, jumpdocs} cover the implementation details.
David Gay's technical reports \cite{gay1997hooking, gay2005writing} remain the primary
description of the \texttt{.nl} format and the \texttt{AMPL} solver interface.
