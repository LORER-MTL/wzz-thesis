\section{Matrix stuffing in \texttt{CVXPY}}\label{sec:matrix-stuffing}

\texttt{CVXPY} takes the perspective of matrix calculus \cite{magnus2019matrix} for computing the
coefficient matrices of linear expressions; this is where most of the computational burden of
canonicalization lies. Matrix stuffing is named for what it does: the coefficients of every
canonicalized expression are stuffed into their designated slots in the sparse standard-form data
handed to the solver. The name suggests bookkeeping, but the coefficients it assembles are the
Jacobians and Hessians of the canonicalized expressions: matrix stuffing is a sparse derivative
computation, a view developed in \S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives} and \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}.
% let's cut this naming stuff and the bookkeeping.
% let's also not say that matrix stuffing is a sparse derivative, they fundamentally take
% different approaches still. and we shouldn't draw the connection so early.
The section gives a brief summary of matrix calculus and a few examples, presents the conventions
of \texttt{CVXPY}'s backend, and closes with implementation details that become important when
contrasting it with our methodology \S\ref{sec:Theme1}.

\paragraph{Matrix calculus.}
Matrix calculus extends differentiation to functions whose arguments and values are vectors and
matrices. 
% on second read, I don't like this sentence anymore, I don't know about these people

% also let's try to avoid having too many semicolons once again.
Systematic rules for such derivatives go back to Dwyer and Macphail
\cite{dwyer1948symbolic}, and there are many ways to define the derivative of a matrix-valued
expression with respect to a matrix variable \cite{magnus2019matrix}. The simplest is to
vectorize: every expression is identified with
\BEQ\label{eq:vec}
\vect(X) = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & \cdots & x_{m1} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{mn} \end{bmatrix}^T,
\EEQ
the vector that stacks the columns of $X \in \reals^{m \times n}$, and the derivative of one
vectorized expression with respect to another is an ordinary Jacobian matrix. Under this
convention matrix operations become linear operators on the vectorized arguments, and their
Jacobians carry structure: for example
\BEQ\label{eq:vec-kron}
\vect(FX) = (I \otimes F)\,\vect(X),
\EEQ
so the matrix product $FX$ corresponds to the Kronecker-structured, block-diagonal Jacobian
% TODO(read-through): bare \ref for figures/tables here and throughout this chapter vs
% "Table~\ref" style in ch.4; pick one thesis-wide cross-reference convention.
$I \otimes F$. The convention is illustrated in \ref{fig:vec-variable}: as an expression, a
matrix variable is the identity map of its own entries, so its Jacobian is an identity block
whose columns are indexed by the entries of $\vect(X)$, and the Jacobian of the product $FX$ is
block-diagonal with one copy of $F$ per column of $X$.

\paragraph{Composing linear operators.}
After canonicalization every expression is affine, so each node of a conic-form tree can be
written in the vectorized form
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-form}
f(x) = A\,x + b
     = \begin{bmatrix} A & b \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where $x$ is the stacked vector of all variables, $A$ is the Jacobian of the node, constant
since $f$ is affine, and $b$ is a constant offset.
% for the sentence above, are we talking about the full Jacobian? or is A just the jacobian
% of this particular constraint with respect to all variables? 
Matrix stuffing computes the augmented matrix
$[A\ b]$ of every node in one bottom-up pass. A variable leaf contributes an identity block in
its own column slot, a constant leaf contributes its value to the offset column, and an interior
atom $g$ with arguments $f_1, \dots, f_k$ is a linear map $\sum_i L_i f_i$ whose constant
Jacobians $L_i$ apply directly to the augmented matrices of its children,
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule}
\begin{bmatrix} A & b \end{bmatrix}
  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} L_i \begin{bmatrix} A_i & b_i \end{bmatrix}.
\EEQ

node matrices of the figures in this section are exactly these augmented blocks, and at the root
of the constraint tree the augmented matrix is the problem data $[A\ b]$ itself.

\paragraph{Views and copies.}

The simple operators act as \emph{views}, modifying the augmented matrix of their argument in
place: negation flips the sign of every stored entry, indexing keeps the selected rows, and
reshaping costs nothing at all, since it leaves the vectorization of the expression unchanged.
The operators that create \emph{copies} are mostly the bivariate atoms that carry data: one
operand of a matrix product, an elementwise multiplication or a division is constant under the
DCP rules, and the atom forms a matrix from that operand, multiplies it with the augmented
matrix of the other, and stores the product as new data. An expository subset is classified in
\ref{tab:views-copies}.

DPP restricts parameters to enter expressions affinely, so the problem data is affine in the
parameters \cite{agrawal2019dpp}, and \texttt{CVXPY} accounts for this with a third tensor axis:
every
coefficient block stores one slice per
parameter entry plus one slice for the constant part, and the operators of the walk apply to
each slice. Flattening the slices of the root yields the cached \emph{parameter map}
\BEQ\label{eq:param-maps}
\begin{bmatrix} c \\ d \end{bmatrix} = Q\,\tilde\theta,
\qquad
\tilde\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \theta \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
shown in \ref{fig:param-map-dpp} for the objective of problem \eqref{eq:example}; the constraint
data $[A\ b]$ is recovered the same way from its own map. After a parameter change, a re-solve
costs a single sparse matrix--vector product $Q\tilde\theta$. % I think I already said that

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
% we should cleanup this paragraph after making the changes to the parts above.
Three implementation details, shared by \texttt{CVXPY}'s two backends, the C++ \texttt{cvxcore} and
the
\texttt{SciPy} backend, stand out as limitations of the current approach; the engine of
\S\ref{sec:Theme1} does each differently.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{External sparse libraries.} Both backends delegate every matrix operation to a
general-purpose sparse library, Eigen \cite{guennebaud2010eigen} for \texttt{cvxcore} and
\texttt{scipy.sparse} \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth} for the \texttt{SciPy} backend. The storage formats and
kernels are fixed by the library, leaving no
room to exploit structure known at canonicalization time.
\item \emph{Everything is sparse.} All coefficient data is treated as sparse: dense atom data
is converted to a sparse matrix on entry, and no operator carries a dense or block special
case, so dense blocks pay sparse-format overhead and no BLAS kernel (\S\ref{sec:bg-blas}) is
ever used.
\item \emph{Quadratic objectives are separate.} Each quadratic form is swapped for a dummy
variable, and the surrounding affine expression is stuffed by the same walk. The matrices of
the quadratic forms are then scaled by the resulting coefficients and reassembled into the
global objective matrix $P$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Sparse derivatives from detected structure}
\label{sec:computing-derivatives}

The introduction (\S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives}) organized differentiation software by four choices:
the granularity of the expression graph, the mode of differentiation, the assembly of the result,
and its storage.
This section examines the assembly choice in detail, with emphasis on the oracle route, the
standard of the literature \cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
On this route, sparsity detection and graph coloring act as compression: they reduce the number of
oracle probes needed to recover the sparse matrix.
Table~\ref{tab:ad-landscape} gathers recent systems, and the section closes with the two nearest
this thesis.

\paragraph{Scalar traces.}
Early AD tools such as ADOL-C \cite{griewank1996adolc} and CppAD \cite{bell2012cppad} record the
scalar graph as a runtime trace of every elementary operation
\cite{griewank2008evaluating, Naumann2011}.
Such a trace can be enormous: a single matrix--vector product $Ax$ unrolls into one scalar
multiplication for every entry of $A$, together with the additions that sum them, so it quickly
reaches millions of nodes for a realistic problem.

\paragraph{Oracle-based differentiation.}
The oracle of \S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives} returns the Jacobian-vector product (JVP) $Jv$ in forward
mode and the vector-Jacobian product (VJP) $J^\top w$ in reverse mode (for the Hessian, the
analogous Hessian-vector product $Hv$ comes from one forward pass over one reverse pass)
\cite{baydin2017ADsurvey}.
These matrix-vector products are obtained without ever forming $J$, each at about the cost of one
function evaluation; borrowing terminology from numerical linear algebra, this is also called the
\emph{matrix-free} view of AD.

\paragraph{Recovering the Jacobian.}
Recovering $J$ explicitly requires \emph{probing} the oracle with a sequence of seed vectors: the $n$
unit seeds recover it column by column in forward mode, and the $m$ unit seeds recover it row by row
in reverse mode (the Hessian is recovered the same way, though its symmetry permits fewer seeds)
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}.
Probing with the full identity recovers $J$ exactly, $JI = J$, so for a dense target direct
assembly is the oracle batched over unit seeds.
When the Jacobian is sparse, most of each probe is wasted on zeros, and the full identity is
prohibitively expensive; reducing the number of probes is the purpose of graph coloring.
Coloring operates on the oracle alone, independently of how the underlying graph is built,
so even fully vectorized frameworks such as JAX \cite{jax2018github}, whose local derivatives are
Jacobian blocks combined by the rules of matrix calculus \cite{magnus2019matrix}, rely on it when
they need an assembled sparse Jacobian.

\paragraph{Graph coloring.}
The cost of probing drops when several seeds share a single pass. Two columns of $J$ with no nonzero
in a common row are \emph{structurally orthogonal}, so one seed recovers both without their nonzeros
colliding. Finding the fewest such groups is a graph-coloring problem on the column intersection
graph, whose edges join columns that share a row
\cite{curtis1974estimation, coleman1983estimation, gebremedhin2005color}.
Figure~\ref{fig:jacobian-coloring} illustrates the compression: one probe per color group replaces

\paragraph{Detect, compress, propagate.}
An oracle-based sparse Jacobian is obtained in three stages: \emph{detect} the sparsity pattern,
\emph{compress} it by coloring, and \emph{propagate} the compressed seeds of
Figure~\ref{fig:jacobian-coloring} through the oracle to recover the nonzero entries
\cite{hill2025sparser}. The pattern is computed once and reused, so its
cost is amortized across repeated evaluations of the same function. This scheme underlies sparse AD
in oracle-based tools such as \texttt{CasADi} (\S\ref{sec:casadi}).

\paragraph{Direct differentiation.}
The direct route of \S\ref{sec:bg-derivatives} needs neither probing nor coloring.
Direct assembly on scalar graphs exists but is rare: \emph{vertex elimination} (also called
\emph{cross-country} accumulation) forms $J$ by eliminating the intermediate vertices of the
linearized graph, though finding the optimal elimination order is intractable
\cite{griewank2008evaluating, naumann2008optimal}, and the scalar symbolic graphs of
\texttt{CasADi} build derivative expressions node by node in the same spirit \cite{casadidocs}.
A scalar node carries only a $0/1$ dependency, so direct propagation degenerates to bookkeeping
the sparsity pattern; vectorization upgrades the propagated object to a structured block, and
direct assembly pays off on vectorized graphs.
Recent works apply
this idea in statistics \cite{kwok2025vectorised} and optimal control \cite{zou2025vectorized}.
Direct propagation wins when the local blocks are well structured and intermediate fill-in stays
bounded, while coloring through the oracle can be cheaper when the intermediates densify.
Which assembly is preferable follows the solver that consumes the result: the conic
solvers targeted in this thesis require the matrix explicitly as data, which the direct approach
produces.

\subsection{Sparse automatic differentiation in Julia}
\label{sec:julia-ad}

The Julia sparse-AD stack developed by Hill and Dalle \cite{hill2025sparser, hill2025sct} is the
state of the art of the detect--compress--propagate scheme described above, packaged as three
composable libraries: \texttt{SparseConnectivityTracer} detects the sparsity pattern,
\texttt{SparseMatrixColorings} compresses it, and \texttt{DifferentiationInterface} drives the
compressed sweeps through any Julia AD backend.

\paragraph{Sparsity detection.}
Detection works by operator overloading. The function is evaluated once on \emph{index-set
tracers}: input $x_j$ starts as the singleton $\{j\}$, and every operation returns the union of
the index sets of its arguments. The index sets of the outputs are the rows of the Jacobian
pattern; a second-order tracer propagates index pairs the same way and yields the Hessian
pattern. The tracers carry no values, so the pattern is \emph{global}: it holds at every input
and is computed once per function.

\paragraph{Compression and propagation.}
\texttt{SparseMatrixColorings} compresses the pattern with greedy distance-2 and star colorings,
and \texttt{DifferentiationInterface} propagates one compressed seed per color
through the chosen backend, then scatters the recovered values into the sparse matrix.
All three stages are bundled into a preparation object, computed once and reused across
evaluations:
\begin{lstlisting}
using DifferentiationInterface, SparseConnectivityTracer, SparseMatrixColorings
import ForwardDiff

backend = AutoSparse(AutoForwardDiff();
    sparsity_detector  = TracerSparsityDetector(),
    coloring_algorithm = GreedyColoringAlgorithm())
prep = prepare_jacobian(f, backend, x)  # detect + color, once
J = jacobian(f, prep, backend, x)       # compressed sweeps, repeated
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Relation to the diff engine.}
The stack treats the program as opaque, so its sparsity must be traced, compressed, and
recovered. The diff engine of this thesis needs none of the three stages: the Jacobian pattern
of every DCP atom is known analytically.

\subsection{\texttt{CasADi}}
\label{sec:casadi}

\texttt{CasADi} \cite{andersson2019} is an integrated symbolic-AD framework for optimization
whose quadratic-programming interface extracts the explicit matrices $P$ and $A$ by AD on the
expression graph; it is the reference implementation of the detect--compress--propagate scheme
of this section in optimization software.

\paragraph{Expression types.}
Expressions come in two types \cite{casadidocs}. An \texttt{SX} graph is scalar, with exact
node-by-node sparsity, at the cost of a graph whose size grows with the problem data. An
\texttt{MX} graph is vectorized: nodes are whole matrix operations, so large models keep small
graphs. Either graph is compiled into a flat instruction
tape over a shared work vector, which enables common-subexpression elimination, constant
folding, and C code generation.

\paragraph{Jacobian assembly.}
Even on a vectorized graph, the Jacobian is reached through the oracle: a sweep over the tape
carries a batch of seed directions and returns their products $Jv$, not $J$ itself. Assembly
therefore follows the stages of this section: the sparsity is computed by a pattern propagation over
the tape, compressed by greedy distance-2 coloring, and the compressed seeds are pushed through
batched forward or reverse sweeps, with the mode chosen by a cost heuristic on the pattern's
shape. The Hessian is assembled as the Jacobian of the gradient, one forward sweep over the
reverse tape, with a star coloring. Pattern, coloring, and the compiled
derivative function are cached across evaluations. This is how
\texttt{CasADi}'s own \texttt{qpsol} interface recovers the problem data of a quadratic
program, by differentiating the model without invoking a solver:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import casadi as cs
z, th = cs.MX.sym("z", n), cs.MX.sym("th", p)
f, g = objective(z, th), slacks(z, th)  # lowered conic model
c = cs.substitute(cs.gradient(f, z), z, 0)
P = cs.jacobian(cs.gradient(f, z), z)   # Jacobian of the gradient
A = -cs.jacobian(g, z)
b = cs.substitute(g, z, 0)
data = cs.Function("data", [th], [P, c, A, b])
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{The dense-block worst case.}
A dense $m \times n$ block in the problem data makes every pair of its columns collide in some
row: the column intersection graph restricted to the block is a clique, no coloring can use
fewer than $\min(m,n)$ colors, and the sweep count grows to the full dimension of the block,
even when the assembled matrix is mostly sparse. Lowered conic
problems routinely embed dense blocks, so this worst case governs coefficient extraction in
the setting of this thesis.

\paragraph{References.}
The book by Griewank and Walther \cite{griewank2008evaluating} covers the theory and practice of
scalar AD, including the graph-elimination view of Jacobian accumulation; the book by Naumann
\cite{Naumann2011} focuses on implementation by operator overloading and source transformation.
The survey by Baydin et al.\ \cite{baydin2017ADsurvey} covers AD in machine learning.
For the combinatorial side, the survey of Gebremedhin et al.\ \cite{gebremedhin2005color} is the
standard entry point, and the illustrated guide of Hill and Dalle \cite{hill2025illustrated} is a
modern tutorial on the detection--coloring--compression scheme.
The \texttt{CasADi} paper \cite{andersson2019} covers its expression graphs, AD implementation,
and solver interfaces.

\section{Coefficient extraction from declared structure}
\label{sec:declared-structure}

One question sorts every modeling system: are the coefficients of the problem data
\emph{stored} as the user typed them, \emph{differentiated} out of an expression graph, or
\emph{detected} at runtime and recovered through coloring?
The previous section covered the third answer; this section examines two systems that need no
detection, \texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP}. Both declare the structure of the
problem up front, so extraction reduces to reading off what is already known.

\subsection{\texttt{AMPL}}
\label{sec:ampl}

\texttt{AMPL} \cite{fourer1990ampl} is the oldest system in this review, and its architecture
still defines how most solvers receive nonlinear problems. The AMPL translator runs in its own
process: it parses the model and the data, instantiates every constraint, and writes the
resulting instance to a \texttt{.nl} file \cite{gay2005writing}. The solver is a separate
executable that links the AMPL Solver Library (ASL), reads the file back, and returns its answer
in a \texttt{.sol} file \cite{gay1997hooking}.

\paragraph{The \texttt{.nl} format.}
The \texttt{.nl} file is an instance format, not a model format: sets and indexing are resolved
by the translator, and what remains is one flat record per objective and constraint, in a binary
or ASCII encoding. A header states the dimensions of the problem and the nonzero counts of its
Jacobian and gradients; the body is a sequence of segments keyed by a leading letter.
\texttt{C} and \texttt{O} segments hold the nonlinear parts of constraints and objectives as
expression graphs in Polish prefix notation. \texttt{J} and \texttt{G} segments hold their
linear parts as explicit lists of (variable, coefficient) pairs. \texttt{b} and \texttt{r}
segments hold variable and constraint bounds, and \texttt{k} segments hold column-wise nonzero
counts of the Jacobian. Every linear coefficient is stored exactly as the translator assembled
it, so the solver recovers the linear problem data by reading, at a cost linear in the number
of nonzeros; only the nonlinear parts are ever differentiated.

\paragraph{Example.}
A small quadratic program makes the format concrete:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
var x; var y;
minimize obj: x^2 + x*y + 3*x;
subject to c1: x + 2*y >= 1;
subject to c2: 3*x - y = 2;
\end{lstlisting}
The translator writes the following \texttt{.nl} file (ASCII encoding; annotations and
elisions ours):
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
g3 0 1 0     # text format; 9 more header lines follow
 2 2 1 0 1   # 2 vars, 2 constraints, 1 objective, 0 ranges, 1 eqn
...
r            # constraint bounds: c1 >= 1, c2 = 2
2 1
4 2
...
O0 0         # minimize objective 0; nonlinear part x^2 + x*y
o0           #   add
o5           #     pow
v0           #       variable 0 (x)
n2           #       constant 2
o2           #     multiply
v0           #       variable 0 (x)
v1           #       variable 1 (y)
...
J0 2         # linear Jacobian row of c1: two entries
0 1          #   coefficient 1 on variable 0
1 2          #   coefficient 2 on variable 1
J1 2         # linear Jacobian row of c2
0 3
1 -1
G0 2         # linear part of the objective gradient: 3x
0 3
1 0
\end{lstlisting}
The quadratic terms $x^2 + xy$ appear only as the expression graph of the \texttt{O0} segment;
every linear coefficient, the rows of $A$ and the linear term of the gradient, is spelled out
in the \texttt{J} and \texttt{G} segments. For a linear program, the file \emph{is} the problem
data.

\paragraph{Solver-side evaluation.}
Inside the solver, ASL reads the header, rebuilds the expression graphs, and exposes the
instance through a small evaluation interface: \texttt{objval} and \texttt{objgrd} for the
objective and its gradient, \texttt{conval} and \texttt{jacval} for the constraints and their
Jacobian, and \texttt{sphsetup} and \texttt{sphes} for the sparse Hessian of the Lagrangian. Gradients and
Jacobian entries of the nonlinear parts come from reverse-mode AD on the rebuilt graphs
\cite{gay1991automatic}. The sparse Hessian exploits \emph{partial separability}: each nonlinear
function is recognized as a sum of terms $f_i(A_i x)$ with few variables each, so the Hessian
assembles as a sum of small dense blocks $A_i^\top \nabla^2 f_i \, A_i$, and \texttt{sphsetup}
produces the sparsity pattern from this structure alone \cite{gay1996more}. There is no runtime
detection and no coloring anywhere in the process.

\paragraph{The file as contract.}
Any modeling system that writes \texttt{.nl} reaches
every solver that links ASL, and the format has stayed backward compatible since 1990, so old
instances run against today's solvers. An instance is an ordinary file: it can be archived,
shipped to reproduce a bug, and rerun across machines and versions.
The price is the decoupling itself: every solve serializes the instance
to disk and rebuilds its graphs in the solver process.

\paragraph{Relation to the diff engine.}
\texttt{AMPL} is the closest prior art to the diff engine: both exploit known structure rather
than discovering it at runtime. The difference is granularity. ASL derives its sparsity from
the partial separability of a scalar expression tape; the diff engine reads it off per atom of
a vectorized DCP graph, as whole sparse blocks known analytically.

\subsection{\texttt{JuMP}}
\label{sec:jump}

\texttt{JuMP} \cite{dunning2017jump, lubin2023jump10recent} is an algebraic modeling language
embedded in Julia; its abstraction layer between models and solvers is
\texttt{MathOptInterface} (MOI) \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}.
Where \texttt{AMPL} decouples modeler and solver through a
file, \texttt{JuMP} decouples them through a typed in-memory interface. For the linear,
quadratic, and conic problems considered here, coefficients are stored as the user types them,
and no differentiation occurs anywhere on the problem-data path.

\paragraph{Macros and term lists.}
The macros of \texttt{JuMP} rewrite each expression as it is entered: \texttt{@objective} and
\texttt{@constraint} build affine and quadratic expression objects term by term, merging
duplicates eagerly, so the model never holds an expression tree, only coefficient lists. These
lists are typed by MOI. A \texttt{VectorAffineFunction} is a list of (row, variable,
coefficient) terms plus a constant vector; a \texttt{ScalarQuadraticFunction} adds a list of
(variable, variable, coefficient) triples. A constraint pairs one such function with a set, for
instance a nonnegative orthant or a second-order cone: the functional conic form of
\S\ref{sec:bg-canonicalization}.

\paragraph{Extraction.}
Extraction is serialization: when the model is passed to a solver, MOI copies each constraint
into the solver's own representation (\texttt{copy\_to}); solvers that consume matrix data use
the \texttt{MatrixOfConstraints} utility, which concatenates the stored term lists into one
sparse matrix in the solver's format. The cost of extraction is this copy: a pure
data-structure transformation, linear in the number of terms, with no analysis of any graph.
% TODO(oscar): confirm copy_to + MatrixOfConstraints is the canonical path for conic solver
% wrappers (SCS.jl, Clarabel.jl), and whether any differentiation can occur on this path.

\paragraph{Bridges.}
When a solver does not support a constraint type natively, MOI reformulates it through its
\emph{bridge} system: a bridge is a structural rewrite from one function-set pair to another,
for instance from a rotated to a plain second-order cone, chosen by a shortest-path search over
the graph of available bridges \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}. Bridging manipulates stored
coefficients and adds auxiliary variables and constraints. This mirrors \texttt{CVXPY}'s
canonicalization (\S\ref{sec:canonicalization}) with one difference: \texttt{JuMP} rewrites
problems whose data is already in matrix form, while \texttt{CVXPY} must first produce that
data from a DCP atom tree, which is the work this thesis accelerates.

\paragraph{The nonlinear path.}
\texttt{JuMP} also has a nonlinear path, in which expression graphs are retained and derivatives
come from reverse-mode AD with coloring for sparse Hessians
(the \texttt{Nonlinear.ReverseAD} module); it serves general nonlinear solvers and is out of
scope for this thesis.

\paragraph{References.}
The MathOptInterface paper \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface} is the best reference on the
design of \texttt{JuMP}'s solver abstraction layer and its bridge system.
David Gay's technical reports \cite{gay1997hooking, gay2005writing} remain the authoritative
description of the \texttt{.nl} format and the \texttt{AMPL} solver interface.
