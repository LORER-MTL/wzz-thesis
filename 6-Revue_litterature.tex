\Chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}\label{sec:RevLitt}

This chapter reviews the implementation of \texttt{CVXPY}'s matrix stuffing
(\S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}) and of other modelling language approaches, from sparse AD such as
\texttt{CasADi} (\S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}) to coefficient extraction in \texttt{AMPL}
and \texttt{JuMP} (\S\ref{sec:declared-structure}). These modelling languages all produce the
same output as \texttt{CVXPY}, the solver problem data, but differ in the inputs they accept
and in how they compute the coefficients. For example, \texttt{CasADi} accepts vectorized expressions as input, but differentiates
them through matrix--vector products, so the structure of the derivatives is never materialized
and must be detected at runtime, over many sweeps of the expression graph.
\texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP} let the user declare vectorized expressions but scalarize them
when processing the data into coefficients.
On the other hand, the canonicalization backends of \texttt{CVXPY} keep the expressions
vectorized and need no detection step (for this reason, we only compare against these backends
in Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme2}).

\section{Matrix stuffing in \texttt{CVXPY}}\label{sec:matrix-stuffing}

Matrix stuffing converts the conic-form expression trees into the problem data. After
canonicalization every expression is affine, so the Jacobian of
each node is a constant matrix, which matrix calculus \cite{magnus2019matrix} gives in
closed form. Computing the problem data reduces to composing these constant Jacobians over
the tree. This section reviews the derivative rules along with two design
choices of the \texttt{CVXPY} implementation: the storage of the coefficients during the
pass, and the caching of the problem data across parameter changes. Finally,
we describe \texttt{CVXPY}'s current backends and their limitations.

% \paragraph{Matrix calculus.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Matrix calculus extends differentiation to functions whose arguments and values are vectors and
matrices. Systematic rules for such derivatives date back to the 1940s \cite{dwyer1948symbolic}.
The simplest convention is to vectorize \cite{magnus2019matrix}: every expression is identified
with
\BEQ\label{eq:vec}
\vect(\mmat{X}) = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & \cdots & x_{m1} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{mn} \end{bmatrix}^\top,
\EEQ
the vector that stacks the columns of $\mmat{X} \in \reals^{m \times n}$, and the derivative of one
vectorized expression with respect to another is an ordinary Jacobian matrix. Under this
convention, matrix operations become
linear operators on the vectorized arguments, and their Jacobians are structured.
For example, the matrix product $\mmat{F}\mmat{X}$ has the
Kronecker-structured, block-diagonal
Jacobian $\mmat{I} \otimes \mmat{F}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:vec-variable}).

\subsection{Composing linear operators}\label{sec:ms-composing}
Every node of a conic-form tree, not only the root, computes an affine function of the problem
variables and can be written in the vectorized form
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-form}
f(\mvec{z}) = \mmat{A}_f\,\mvec{z} + \mvec{b}_f
     = \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
      \mvec{z} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where $\mvec{z}$ is the stacked vector of all the problem variables, $\mmat{A}_f$ is the
Jacobian of $f$ with respect to $\mvec{z}$, and $\mvec{b}_f$ is a
constant vector. Stacking the augmented matrices of the constraint trees yields the problem
data $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$ of \eqref{e:quad-conic-form}.

Matrix stuffing computes the augmented matrix in one bottom-up pass over the
tree. A variable leaf contributes an identity block and a constant leaf contributes its value
to the last column (Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing}). An interior node with a single argument
applies a constant linear operator $\mmat{L}$ to the argument, which has the affine form
\eqref{eq:linop-form} with augmented matrix $[\mmat{A}_1\ \mvec{b}_1]$; applying $\mmat{L}$ gives
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule-unary}
\begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix}
  = \mmat{L} \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_1 & \mvec{b}_1 \end{bmatrix}.
\EEQ
The operator $\mmat{L}$ is the Jacobian of the node with respect to its argument, so
\eqref{eq:linop-chain-rule-unary} is exactly the chain rule of matrix calculus.
A node with $k$ arguments applies one operator per argument,
$f = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mmat{L}_i f_i$, which gives
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule}
\begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix}
  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mmat{L}_i \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_i & \mvec{b}_i \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
so the node costs one matrix product per argument.

% \paragraph{Views and copies.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
In \texttt{CVXPY}, the linear operators divide into views and copies \cite{cvxpy2026source}. A view performs a
simple manipulation of the coefficients of its argument. Atoms with one constant argument,
such as
\texttt{matmul} and \texttt{multiply}, make copies instead. The operator is a constant
matrix built from the constant argument, and its product with the augmented matrix of the
other argument is stored as a new coefficient matrix.
Table~\ref{tab:views-copies} classifies a subset of the operators.

\subsection{Example}\label{sec:ms-example}

Matrix stuffing then runs bottom-up over the objective and the three constraint trees
(Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing}). Each leaf of $\mvec{x}$
contributes an identity block of size $n$ at the column offset of $\mvec{x}$, and the constant
leaf $\mvec{g}$ contributes its value to the last column. The interior nodes apply their
operators to the matrices of their children; the product node $\mmat{F}\mvec{x}$, for example,
multiplies the identity block of its argument by $\mmat{F}$. Finally, the root stacks the
augmented matrices of its children at their constraint row offsets into $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$.

% \paragraph{Local versus global derivatives.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The augmented matrices of \eqref{eq:linop-form} can be stored in two ways
(Figure~\ref{fig:local-vs-global}). In the global layout, drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing}, each node stores one matrix whose columns span the whole
stacked vector $\mvec{z}$, with the column offsets applied from the start. In the local
layout, which \texttt{CVXPY} uses \cite{cvxpy2026source}, each node keeps one coefficient block per variable in its
subtree, and one for the constant data. The offsets are applied only after the pass, when
each block is placed at its variable's columns and the blocks are concatenated into the full
matrix $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$. The local layout stores only the columns of the variables
present in a subtree; the tradeoff is that each interior node applies its operator once per
stored block, rather than once per argument.

\subsection{Parameter caching}\label{sec:ms-parameters}
% \paragraph{Parameters and tensors.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
In a problem family the structure is fixed and only the numeric parameter values change
between solves. Under additional rules on how the parameters enter the expressions, known as
\ac{DPP}, the problem data is affine in the parameters \cite{agrawal2019dpp}.
To keep track of the parameter dependence, \texttt{CVXPY} adds a third dimension to the
problem data, indexed by the parameter entries. Every coefficient matrix throughout the
bottom-up pass becomes a tensor. At the root nodes we thus get the cached parameter maps
\BEQ\label{eq:param-maps}
\mvec{c} = \mmat{Q}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta},
\qquad
\vect([\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]) = \mmat{R}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta},
\qquad
\gvec{\tilde\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \gvec{\theta} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where $\mmat{Q} \in \reals^{n \times (p+1)}$ and
$\mmat{R} \in \reals^{m(n+1) \times (p+1)}$. The objective map $\mmat{Q}$ is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-dpp} for problem \eqref{eq:example}. After a parameter change, a
re-solve costs one sparse matrix--vector product per map.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
$
\mmat{Q} =
\begin{blockarray}{cccc}
        & \mvec{g}^\top & \mu & 1 \\
\begin{block}{c[ccc]}
  t_1   & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & \plotzero & 1         \\
  t_2   & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & 1         & \plotzero \\
  \mvec{x}     & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & \plotzero & \plotzero \\
\end{block}
\end{blockarray}
\qquad
\mvec{c} = \mmat{Q}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta}
= \begin{bmatrix}1\\ \mu\\ \plotzero\end{bmatrix}
$
\caption{The DPP path on the objective data of problem \eqref{eq:example}: the parameter map
$\mmat{Q}$
is built and cached once, and each re-solve recovers $\mvec{c}$ with the single sparse
product
$\mmat{Q}\gvec{\tilde\theta}$.}
\label{fig:param-map-dpp}
\end{figure}

% \paragraph{The \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Problems that do not conform to the DPP grammar, or that opt out of it with
\texttt{ignore\_dpp=True}, take a different approach to matrix stuffing.
The reduction \texttt{EvalParams} replaces each
parameter leaf by a constant holding its current value, and the resulting parameter-free tree
goes through the pass as if the problem had no parameters
(Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-evalparams}). The result is the same objective data $\mvec{c}$ as in
Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-dpp}, now obtained by repeating the whole matrix stuffing instead
of applying the cached product $\mmat{Q}\gvec{\tilde\theta}$. The \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path is
more general, as it supports arbitrary functions of the parameters.

% \paragraph{The three backends.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
% (subsection heading "The canonicalization backends" merged into Parameter caching, 2026-08-03)
\texttt{CVXPY} implements the pass in three canonicalization backends that differ in how
they represent the parameter axis \cite{cvxpydocs}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the C++ backend \texttt{cvxcore} keeps one sparse matrix per parameter slice and
loops over the slices at every operator;
\item the \texttt{SciPy} backend stacks the slices vertically into a single strided
two-dimensional sparse matrix;
\item the \texttt{COO} backend does not form the tensor explicitly, and instead stores
each nonzero with an explicit parameter index alongside its row and column.
\end{itemize}
When canonicalization remains too slow, \texttt{CVXPYgen} can compile the problem ahead of
time \cite{schaller2022cvxpygen}. It emits problem-specific C code that embeds the cached
parameter maps \eqref{eq:param-maps} of a DPP problem.

The pass of this section produces the affine data $\mvec{c}$ and $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$.
For a quadratic objective, \texttt{CVXPY} replaces the affine argument of each quadratic
atom by a new variable, adding the corresponding equality constraint; the objective becomes
a sum of quadratic forms with constant matrices. A second matrix stuffing pass then
assembles these matrices into the diagonal blocks of the matrix $\mmat{P}$ of
\eqref{e:quad-conic-form} \cite{cvxpy2026source}.

% \paragraph{Implementation details.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
All three backends share two limitations. Firstly, they delegate their matrix operations to a
general-purpose sparse library, Eigen \cite{guennebaud2010eigen} in \texttt{cvxcore} and
\texttt{scipy.sparse} \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth} in the Python backends.
The storage formats and kernels are fixed by the
library, so structure known at canonicalization time cannot be exploited. In
\texttt{cvxcore} the views of Table~\ref{tab:views-copies} also materialize explicit sparse
operators. For example, a reshape builds a sparse identity matrix and multiplies the
coefficients by it.

Secondly, the canonicalization backends store dense constant matrices as sparse matrices.
When dense blocks appear in the problem data, they are both stored in an inconvenient format
and computed without the optimized BLAS kernels of \S\ref{sec:bg-blas}.
Our approach in Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme1} addresses both limitations.

\section{Sparse derivatives from detected structure}
\label{sec:computing-derivatives}

This section reviews methods that compute the full sparse Jacobian of a vector-valued
function, given as an arbitrary program in a general-purpose language. No modelling language
declares the structure of the program, so the sparsity of the derivatives must be
detected \cite{hill2025sparser}. Modern surveys of AD address dense derivatives, or the
gradient of a scalar function, which reverse mode computes in a single sweep over the
computational graph \cite{baydin2017ADsurvey, blondel2024elements}. In those
settings, AD refers to the evaluation of matrix--vector products with the Jacobian or the
Hessian, and the explicit matrix is never formed. Factorizations, direct linear solves, and
many optimization solvers instead require the explicit matrix \cite{hill2025sparser}.
The methods of this section recover the full sparse matrix from multiple matrix--vector
products. To reduce the number of products, graph colouring groups the vectors that can be
propagated together, so that one product recovers several columns at once.

\subsection{Matrix-vector products and accumulation}\label{sec:matrix-free-diff}
% \paragraph{Modes and products.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
AD computes one matrix--vector product with the derivative per sweep of the computational
graph, a traversal that applies the chain rule node by node at about the cost of one function
evaluation \cite{griewank2008evaluating}. Forward mode starts from a seed vector $\mvec{v}$ at
the inputs and propagates tangents, and the \ac{JVP} $\mmat{J}\mvec{v}$ appears at the outputs.
Reverse mode starts from a seed $\mvec{w}$ at the outputs and propagates adjoints, and the
\ac{VJP} $\mmat{J}^\top \mvec{w}$ appears at the inputs
(Figures~\ref{fig:forward-sweep} and~\ref{fig:reverse-sweep}). For the Hessian, the analogous
Hessian-vector product $\mmat{H}\mvec{v}$ comes from one forward sweep over one reverse sweep.

% \paragraph{Recovering the Jacobian.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Recovering the full matrix takes a sequence of sweeps, one per seed vector. The $n$ unit seed
vectors recover $\mmat{J}$ column by column in forward mode,
and the $m$ unit seed vectors recover it row by
row in reverse mode \cite{griewank2008evaluating}. The other approach is accumulation, which
combines the local derivatives of the graph directly into $\mmat{J}$ by eliminating one
intermediate vertex at a time (Figure~\ref{fig:vertex-elimination})
\cite{griewank1991markowitz}; finding the optimal elimination
order is NP-complete \cite{naumann2008optimal, griewank2008evaluating}. Batching the $n$
unit seeds into one forward sweep gives each node a whole tangent, the derivative of that
node with respect to all the inputs. Eliminating the vertices in the order of evaluation
stores the same derivative at each node, so the batched sweep and forward-order elimination
perform the same computation.

In practice, AD tools such as ADOL-C \cite{griewank1996adolc} and CppAD \cite{bell2012cppad}
record the scalar graph as a runtime trace, one record per elementary operation
\cite{griewank2008evaluating, Naumann2011}. A single matrix--vector product
unrolls into one scalar multiplication for every entry of the matrix, together with the
additions that sum them, so the trace of a realistic problem can sometimes reach millions of nodes.
On such a trace, every intermediate vertex would store its derivative with respect to all
the inputs, so accumulation is rarely used in practice.
When $\mmat{J}$ is sparse, most of each sweep is then wasted on
zeros, and the cost of recovering the matrix scales with its dimension rather than with its
number of nonzeros.

\subsection{Compression by graph colouring}\label{sec:graph-colouring}
% \paragraph{Graph colouring.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Several columns of a sparse Jacobian can share one seed \cite{curtis1974estimation}. Two
columns with no nonzero in a common row are structurally orthogonal, and one sweep
recovers both, each entry landing in a distinct row of the product. Finding the fewest
such groups was later formalized as a colouring of the column intersection graph, which
has one vertex per column and an edge between two columns that share a row
\cite{coleman1983estimation, gebremedhin2005color}.
Figure~\ref{fig:jacobian-coloring} colours the graph of a $6 \times 8$ Jacobian and
recovers the matrix in three sweeps instead of eight. The approach extends to the Hessian,
which compresses further because its symmetry allows dedicated star and acyclic colourings
\cite{powell1979estimation, Gebremedhin2009SparseHessians}.

same graph, replacing each arithmetic operation by a union of index sets
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}. The pattern and its colouring are computed once and reused,
so their cost is amortized across repeated evaluations of the same function.

\subsection{\texttt{CasADi}}
\label{sec:casadi}

\texttt{CasADi} \cite{andersson2019} is an open-source framework for nonlinear optimization
and optimal control built around AD. It is widely used in academia and industry, notably for
model predictive control, trajectory optimization, robotics, and energy systems
\cite{casadidocs}. Its core is written in C++ and is used from Python, MATLAB, or Octave. It
provides the building blocks of gradient-based optimization: symbolic expressions,
derivatives, integrators, and interfaces to NLP and QP solvers. The
gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians these solvers require are obtained by differentiating the
model.
% (qpsol listing + dense-block worst-case ¶ cut 2026-08-04; archived in
% plans/casadi_qp_extraction_cut.md.)

% \paragraph{Expression types.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
\texttt{CasADi} supports two types of expressions, and both return the matrix derivatives in
a sparse format, which is expected by most solvers \cite{casadidocs}. An
\texttt{SX} graph is scalar, with exact node-by-node sparsity, but its size grows with the
problem data. An \texttt{MX} graph is vectorized. Its nodes are whole
matrix operations, so the graph stays small even when the model is large. Either graph can
be exported as self-contained C code and compiled ahead of time for embedded applications.

% \paragraph{Jacobian assembly.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
\texttt{CasADi} computes its derivatives by the detection, colouring, and batched sweeps of
this section, and caches the pattern, the colouring, and the compiled derivative function
across evaluations. The following listing rebuilds the conic-form trees of
Figure~\ref{fig:conic-form-tree}, obtained after canonicalization, as \texttt{MX}
expressions; differentiating them recovers the problem data that matrix stuffing assembles
in \S\ref{sec:ms-example}.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import casadi as cs
t1, t2 = cs.MX.sym("t1"), cs.MX.sym("t2")
x = cs.MX.sym("x", n)
g, mu = cs.MX.sym("g", m), cs.MX.sym("mu")
z = cs.vertcat(t1, t2, x)                   # stacked variables
expr = cs.vertcat(x, t1, F @ x - g, t2, x)  # stacked affine expressions
A = -cs.jacobian(expr, z)                   # the matrix A
b = cs.substitute(expr, z, 0)               # the vector b
c = cs.gradient(t1 + mu * t2, z)            # the objective vector c
\end{lstlisting}
The Jacobian with respect to the stacked variable $\mvec{z}$ recovers the matrix $\mmat{A}$
of Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing} entry by entry; differentiation is the equivalent of matrix
stuffing, with the coefficients obtained by those sweeps rather than by a bottom-up pass
over the tree. The parameters remain symbols, so $\mvec{b}$ and $\mvec{c}$
are expressions of $\mvec{g}$ and $\mu$, re-evaluated for each parameter value. This is how
the \texttt{qpsol} interface builds its problem data internally, for problems with a
quadratic objective and affine constraints. A model written at the same high level as in
\texttt{CVXPY}, with the norms of \eqref{eq:example} kept in the expression graph, is solved
through \texttt{nlpsol} instead. The same sweeps assemble the gradient, constraint
Jacobian, and Hessian of the Lagrangian that the NLP solver queries at each iteration.

\section{Coefficient extraction from declared structure}
\label{sec:declared-structure}

The systems of the previous section detect the structure of the problem at runtime and recover
its coefficients through colouring. \texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP} need no detection, because
the structure is declared up front and the coefficients are stored as the user types them;
extraction reduces to reading the stored coefficients back
(Table~\ref{tab:modeling-systems}).

\subsection{\texttt{AMPL}}
\label{sec:ampl}

\texttt{AMPL} \cite{fourer1990ampl} is the oldest system in this review, and its architecture
still defines how most solvers receive nonlinear problems. The AMPL translator runs in its own
process. It parses the model and the data, instantiates every constraint, and writes the
resulting instance to a \texttt{.nl} file \cite{gay2005writing}, which the solver, a separate
executable linking the \ac{ASL}, reads back \cite{gay1997hooking}.

% \paragraph{The \texttt{.nl} format.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The \texttt{.nl} file is an instance format rather than a model format. Sets and indexing are
resolved by the translator, and what remains is one flat record per objective and constraint. A header
states the dimensions of the problem and the nonzero counts of its Jacobian and gradients; the
body is a sequence of segments keyed by a leading letter. \texttt{C} and \texttt{O} segments
hold the nonlinear parts of constraints and objectives as expression graphs in Polish prefix
notation; \texttt{J} and \texttt{G} segments hold their linear parts as explicit lists of
(variable, coefficient) pairs; the remaining segments hold bounds and nonzero counts.

% \paragraph{Example.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The following quadratic program shows the syntax of \texttt{.nl} files.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Ampl]
var x; var y;
minimize obj: x^2 + x*y + 3*x;
subject to c1: x + 2*y >= 1;
\end{lstlisting}
The translator writes the following \texttt{.nl} file (ASCII encoding; annotations and
elisions ours):
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
g3 0 1 0     # text format; 9 more header lines follow
 2 1 1 0 0   # 2 vars, 1 constraint, 1 objective, 0 ranges, 0 eqns
...
r            # constraint bounds: c1 >= 1
2 1
...
O0 0         # minimize objective 0; nonlinear part x^2 + x*y
o0           #   add
o5           #     pow
v0           #       variable 0 (x)
n2           #       constant 2
...          #     multiply subtree of x*y
J0 2         # linear Jacobian row of c1: two entries
0 1          #   coefficient 1 on variable 0
1 2          #   coefficient 2 on variable 1
G0 2         # linear part of the objective gradient: 3x
0 3
1 0
\end{lstlisting}
The quadratic terms $x^2 + xy$ appear only as the expression graph of the \texttt{O0} segment;
every linear coefficient is spelled out in the \texttt{J} and \texttt{G} segments. For a linear
program, the file therefore contains the complete problem data.

% \paragraph{Solver-side evaluation.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Inside the solver, ASL reads the linear coefficients and bounds directly from the file, so for
a linear or quadratic-over-linear instance extraction consists only of this read, at a cost
linear in the number of nonzeros. Only the rebuilt nonlinear graphs are differentiated, by
reverse-mode AD \cite{gay1991automatic}, with sparse Hessians assembled from partial
separability \cite{gay1996more}.

% \paragraph{Advantages of the file format.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The format has three benefits: any system that writes \texttt{.nl} reaches every solver that
links ASL; the format has stayed backward compatible since 1990; and an instance is an
ordinary file that can be archived, shipped to reproduce a bug, and rerun across machines.
The decoupling also has a cost. Every solve serializes the instance to disk and rebuilds
its expression graphs in the solver process.

\subsection{\texttt{JuMP}}
\label{sec:jump}

\texttt{JuMP} \cite{dunning2017jump, lubin2023jump10recent} is an algebraic modelling language
embedded in Julia; its abstraction layer between models and solvers is
\ac{MOI} \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}.
Where \texttt{AMPL} decouples modeller and solver through a
file, \texttt{JuMP} decouples them through a typed in-memory interface.

% \paragraph{Macros and term lists.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
The macros of \texttt{JuMP} rewrite each expression as it is entered. \texttt{@objective} and
\texttt{@constraint} build affine and quadratic expression objects term by term, merging
duplicates eagerly, so the model stores coefficient lists instead of an expression tree. These
lists are typed by MOI. A \texttt{VectorAffineFunction} is a list of (row, variable,
coefficient) terms plus a constant vector; a \texttt{ScalarQuadraticFunction} adds a list of
(variable, variable, coefficient) triples. A constraint pairs one such function with a set, for
instance a nonnegative orthant or a second-order cone. This is the functional conic form of
\S\ref{sec:bg-canonicalization}.
The constraint of the \texttt{AMPL} example, entered as
\lstinline|@constraint(model, x + 2y >= 1)|, is stored as the pair
\begin{lstlisting}
ScalarAffineFunction([(1.0, x), (2.0, y)], 0.0)   in   GreaterThan(1.0)
\end{lstlisting}
a term list, a constant, and a set; the expression itself is not retained after the macro
returns.

% \paragraph{Extraction.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
When the model is passed to a solver, MOI copies each constraint
into the solver's own representation (\texttt{copy\_to}). Solvers that consume matrix data use
the \texttt{MatrixOfConstraints} utility, which concatenates the stored term lists into one
sparse matrix in the solver's format. The cost of extraction is the cost of this copy, which
is linear in the number of terms and requires no analysis of any graph.
The assembled matrix is the same problem data that \texttt{CVXPY}'s matrix stuffing computes
(\S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}); \texttt{JuMP} arrives at it by copying stored coefficients
rather than by differentiating conic-form trees.
% TODO(oscar): confirm copy_to + MatrixOfConstraints is the canonical path for conic solver
% wrappers (SCS.jl, Clarabel.jl), and whether any differentiation can occur on this path.

% \paragraph{Bridges.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
When a solver does not support a constraint type natively, MOI reformulates it through its
\emph{bridge} system. A bridge is a structural rewrite from one function-set pair to another,
for instance from a rotated to a plain second-order cone, chosen by a shortest-path search over
the graph of available bridges \cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}. Bridging manipulates stored
coefficients and adds auxiliary variables and constraints. It is similar to
\texttt{CVXPY}'s canonicalization (\S\ref{sec:canonicalization}), except that
\texttt{JuMP} rewrites
problems whose data is already in matrix form, while \texttt{CVXPY} must first produce that
data from a DCP atom tree; producing this data is the work this thesis accelerates.

\texttt{JuMP} also has a nonlinear path, in which expression graphs are retained and
derivatives come from sparse AD in the style of \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}; it serves
general nonlinear solvers and is out of scope for this thesis.
