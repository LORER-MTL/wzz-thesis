\Chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}\label{sec:RevLitt}

This chapter reviews the implementation of \texttt{CVXPY}'s matrix stuffing and of other
modelling language approaches, from sparse \ac{AD} in \texttt{CasADi} to coefficient extraction in
\texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP}. These modelling languages all produce the
same output as \texttt{CVXPY}, the solver problem data, but differ in the inputs they accept
and in how they compute the coefficients. For example, \texttt{CasADi} accepts vectorized
expressions as input, but differentiates them through matrix-vector products, so the structure
of the derivatives must be detected at runtime, over many sweeps of the expression graph.
\texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP} let the user declare vectorized expressions but scalarize them
when processing the data into coefficients.
The canonicalization backends of \texttt{CVXPY}, in contrast, keep the expressions
vectorized and need no detection step. These differences are summarized in
\S\ref{sec:research-gap}.

\section{Matrix stuffing in \texttt{CVXPY}}\label{sec:matrix-stuffing}

Matrix stuffing converts the conic-form expression trees into the problem data. After
canonicalization every expression is affine, so the Jacobian of
each node is a constant matrix, which matrix calculus~\cite{magnus2019matrix} gives in
closed form. Computing the problem data reduces to composing these constant Jacobians over
the tree. This section reviews the derivative rules along with two design
choices of the \texttt{CVXPY} implementation: coefficient storage during the
pass, and caching of the problem data across parameter changes. Finally,
we describe \texttt{CVXPY}'s current backends and their limitations.

% \paragraph{Matrix calculus.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Matrix calculus extends differentiation to functions whose arguments and values are vectors and
matrices. Systematic rules for such derivatives date back to the 1940s~\cite{dwyer1948symbolic}.
The simplest convention is to vectorize~\cite{magnus2019matrix}: every expression is identified
with
\BEQ\label{eq:vec}
\vect(\mmat{X}) = \begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & \cdots & x_{m1} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{mn}
\end{bmatrix}^\top,
\EEQ
the vector that stacks the columns of $\mmat{X} \in \reals^{m \times n}$, and the derivative of a
vectorized expression with respect to another is an ordinary Jacobian matrix. Under this
convention, matrix operations become
linear operators on the vectorized arguments, and their Jacobians are structured.
For example, the matrix product $\mmat{F}\mmat{X}$ has the
Kronecker-structured, block-diagonal
Jacobian $\mmat{I} \otimes \mmat{F}$, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vec-variable}.

\subsection{Composing linear operators}\label{sec:ms-composing}
Every node of a conic-form tree, not only the root, computes an affine function of the problem
variables and can be written in the vectorized form
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-form}
f(\mvec{z}) = \mmat{A}_f\,\mvec{z} + \mvec{b}_f
     = \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
      \mvec{z} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where $\mvec{z}$ is the stacked vector of all the problem variables, $\mmat{A}_f$ is the
Jacobian of $f$ with respect to $\mvec{z}$, and $\mvec{b}_f$ is a
constant vector. Stacking the augmented matrices of the constraint trees yields the problem
data $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$ of~\eqref{e:quad-conic-form}.
Matrix stuffing computes the augmented matrix in one bottom-up pass over the
tree. A variable leaf contributes an identity block and a constant leaf contributes its value
to the last column, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing}. An interior node with a
single argument applies a constant linear operator $\mmat{L}$ to the argument, which has the
affine form~\eqref{eq:linop-form}
with augmented matrix $[\mmat{A}_1\ \mvec{b}_1]$. Applying $\mmat{L}$ gives
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule-unary}
\begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix}
  = \mmat{L} \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_1 & \mvec{b}_1 \end{bmatrix}.
\EEQ
The operator $\mmat{L}$ is the Jacobian of the node with respect to its argument,
so~\eqref{eq:linop-chain-rule-unary}
is exactly the chain rule of matrix calculus.
A node with $k$ arguments applies one operator per argument,
$f = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mmat{L}_i f_i$, which gives
\BEQ\label{eq:linop-chain-rule}
\begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_f & \mvec{b}_f \end{bmatrix}
  = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mmat{L}_i \begin{bmatrix} \mmat{A}_i & \mvec{b}_i \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
so the node costs one matrix product per argument.

% \paragraph{Views and copies.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
In \texttt{CVXPY}, the linear operators divide into views and copies~\cite{cvxpy2026source}. A
view performs a
simple manipulation of the coefficients of its argument. Atoms with one constant argument,
such as
\texttt{matmul} and \texttt{multiply}, make copies instead. The operator is a constant
matrix built from the constant argument, and its product with the augmented matrix of the
other argument is stored as a new coefficient matrix.
Table~\ref{tab:views-copies} classifies a subset of the operators.

\subsection{Example}\label{sec:ms-example}
We continue the example of \S\ref{sec:canon-example}, which canonicalized problem~\eqref{eq:example}

compute the problem data of these trees. The variables are first stacked into
$\mvec{z} = (t_1, t_2, \mvec{x})$. Each variable then occupies a contiguous block of columns of
$\mmat{A}_f$, starting at its column offset within $\mvec{z}$. The columns of the variables

shown in Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing}. Each leaf of $\mvec{x}$

operators to the matrices of their children. The product node $\mmat{F}\mvec{x}$, for example,
multiplies the identity block of its argument by $\mmat{F}$. Finally, the root stacks the
augmented matrices of its children at their constraint row offsets into $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$.

The augmented matrices of~\eqref{eq:linop-form} can be stored in two ways,
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:local-vs-global}. In the global layout, drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing}, each node stores one matrix whose columns span the whole

layout, which \texttt{CVXPY} uses~\cite{cvxpy2026source}, each node keeps one coefficient block

each block is placed at its variable's columns and the blocks are concatenated into the full
matrix $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$. The local layout stores only the columns of the variables
present in a subtree. The tradeoff is that each interior node applies its operator once per
stored block, rather than once per argument.

\subsection{Parameter caching}\label{sec:ms-parameters}
% \paragraph{Parameters and tensors.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
In a problem family the structure is fixed and only the numeric parameter values change
between solves. Under additional rules on how the parameters enter the expressions, known as
\ac{DPP}, the problem data is affine in the parameters~\cite{agrawal2019dpp}.
The rules classify each parameter as affine and forbid products of two parametrized
expressions, so that the dependence can be represented by the maps of~\eqref{eq:param-maps}.
To keep track of the parameter dependence, \texttt{CVXPY} adds a third dimension to the
problem data, indexed by the parameter entries. Every coefficient matrix throughout the
bottom-up pass becomes a tensor. At the root nodes we thus get the cached parameter maps
\BEQ\label{eq:param-maps}
\mvec{c} = \mmat{Q}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta},
\qquad
\vect([\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]) = \mmat{R}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta},
\qquad
\gvec{\tilde\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \gvec{\theta} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\EEQ
where $\mmat{Q} \in \reals^{n \times (p+1)}$ and
$\mmat{R} \in \reals^{m(n+1) \times (p+1)}$. The objective map $\mmat{Q}$ is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-dpp} for~\eqref{eq:example}. After a parameter change, a
re-solve costs one sparse matrix-vector product per map.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
$
\mmat{Q} =
\begin{blockarray}{cccc}
        & \mvec{g}^\top & \mu & 1 \\
\begin{block}{c[ccc]}
  t_1   & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & \plotzero & 1         \\
  t_2   & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & 1         & \plotzero \\
  \mvec{x}     & \plotzero \cdots \plotzero & \plotzero & \plotzero \\
\end{block}
\end{blockarray}
\qquad
\mvec{c} = \mmat{Q}\,\gvec{\tilde\theta}
= \begin{bmatrix}1\\ \mu\\ \plotzero\end{bmatrix}
$
\caption{The DPP path on the objective data of~\eqref{eq:example}: the parameter map
$\mmat{Q}$
is built and cached once, and each re-solve recovers $\mvec{c}$ with the single sparse
product
$\mmat{Q}\gvec{\tilde\theta}$.}
\label{fig:param-map-dpp}
\end{figure}

% \paragraph{The \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Problems that do not conform to the DPP grammar, or that opt out of it with the option
\texttt{ignore\_dpp}, take a different approach to matrix stuffing.
The reduction \texttt{EvalParams} replaces each
parameter leaf by a constant holding its current value, and the resulting parameter-free tree
goes through the pass as if the problem had no parameters, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-evalparams}. The result is the same objective data $\mvec{c}$ as in
Figure~\ref{fig:param-map-dpp}, now obtained by repeating the whole matrix stuffing instead
of applying the cached parameter map $\mmat{Q}\gvec{\tilde\theta}$. The \texttt{ignore\_dpp} path is
more general, as it supports arbitrary functions of the parameters.

% \paragraph{The three backends.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
% (subsection heading "The canonicalization backends" merged into Parameter caching, 2026-08-03;
% example-last reorder with a separate "Backends and limitations" subsection tried and
% reverted 2026-08-12: it left Parameter caching too short)
\texttt{CVXPY} implements the pass in three canonicalization backends that differ in how
they represent the parameter axis~\cite{cvxpydocs}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the C++ backend \texttt{cvxcore} keeps one sparse matrix per parameter slice and
loops over the slices at every operator. It began as \texttt{CVXcanon}~\cite{miller2015cvxcanon},
a C++ library written to replace the Python matrix stuffing of early versions of
\texttt{CVXPY}, and was renamed \texttt{cvxcore} in 2018.
\item the \texttt{SCIPY} backend stacks the slices vertically into a single strided
two-dimensional sparse matrix. The backend is named after the \texttt{scipy.sparse}
library that the matrix is stored in (we write \texttt{SCIPY} for the backend and
\texttt{scipy.sparse} for the library).
\item the \texttt{COO} backend does not form the tensor explicitly. It is named after the
\ac{COO} format and extends its (row, column, value) triples with a parameter index (we write
\texttt{COO} for the backend and \ac{COO} for the format).
\end{itemize}

%  cvxcore: ", so the work of every operator grows with the number of parameter entries";
%  SciPy: ", so every operation acts on a matrix whose row dimension is multiplied by the
%  number of parameter entries";
%  COO: ", so its cost follows the number of stored nonzeros and not the parameter count".)
% (default-selection sentence reinstated 2026-08-12: ch. 4 cut its Backend substitution
%  paragraph and now references this passage.)
When no backend is requested, \texttt{CVXPY} uses \texttt{cvxcore}, and substitutes
\texttt{COO} for a DPP problem with at least $1000$ parameter entries in total.
When canonicalization remains too slow, \texttt{CVXPYgen} can compile a DPP problem ahead of
time~\cite{schaller2022cvxpygen}. It generates C code specific to the problem family, with
the entries of the cached maps~\eqref{eq:param-maps} written into the source as constants,
so a re-solve applies the parameter maps in compiled code.
\texttt{CVXPYgen} follows the earlier code generator
\texttt{CVXGEN}~\cite{mattingley2012cvxgen}, which generates a custom C solver for a family
of small quadratic programs.

The pass of this section produces the affine data $\mvec{c}$ and $[\mmat{A}\ \mvec{b}]$.
For a quadratic objective, \texttt{CVXPY} replaces the affine argument of each quadratic
atom by a new variable, adding the corresponding equality constraint. The objective becomes
a sum of quadratic forms with constant matrices. A second matrix stuffing pass then
assembles these matrices into the diagonal blocks of the matrix $\mmat{P}$
of~\eqref{e:quad-conic-form}
~\cite{cvxpy2026source}.

% \paragraph{Implementation details.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
All three backends share two limitations. Firstly, they delegate their matrix operations to a
general-purpose sparse library, \texttt{Eigen}~\cite{guennebaud2010eigen} in \texttt{cvxcore} and
\texttt{scipy.sparse}~\cite{2020SciPy-NMeth} in the Python backends.
The storage formats and kernels are fixed by the
library, so structure known at canonicalization time cannot be used. In
\texttt{cvxcore}, the views of Table~\ref{tab:views-copies} also materialize explicit sparse
operators. For example, a reshape builds a sparse identity matrix and multiplies the
coefficients by it.
Secondly, the canonicalization backends store dense constant matrices as sparse matrices.
When dense blocks appear in the problem data, they are both stored in an inconvenient format
and computed without optimized BLAS kernels.
Our approach in Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme1} addresses these limitations.

\section{Sparse derivatives from detected structure}
\label{sec:computing-derivatives}

This section reviews methods that compute the full sparse Jacobian of a vector-valued
function, given as an arbitrary program in a general-purpose language. No modelling language
declares the structure of the program, so the sparsity of the derivatives must be
detected~\cite{hill2025sparser}. Modern surveys of AD address dense derivatives, or the
gradient of a scalar function, which reverse mode computes in a single sweep over the
computational graph~\cite{baydin2017ADsurvey, blondel2024elements}. In those
settings, AD refers to the evaluation of matrix-vector products with the Jacobian or the
Hessian, and the explicit matrix is never formed. Factorizations, direct linear solves, and
many optimization solvers instead require the explicit matrix~\cite{hill2025sparser}.
In convex optimization, Diamond and Boyd take the matrix-free
view~\cite{diamond2016matrixfree}. Instead of forming~$\mmat{A}$, they represent the problem
as a graph of linear operators, available only through their forward and adjoint products,
which suits first-order solvers. The solvers that \texttt{CVXPY} calls by default instead
factorize a matrix built from~$\mmat{A}$ and~$\mmat{P}$.
The methods of this section recover the full sparse matrix from multiple matrix-vector
products~\cite{griewank2008evaluating}. To reduce the number of products, graph colouring
groups the vectors that can be
propagated together, so that one product recovers several columns at once.

\subsection{Matrix-vector products and accumulation}\label{sec:matrix-free-diff}
% \paragraph{Modes and products.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
AD evaluates derivatives by sweeps of the computational graph. A sweep visits every node
once and applies the chain rule at each, at about the cost of one function
evaluation~\cite{griewank2008evaluating}. Each sweep computes one matrix-vector product
with the derivative. Forward mode starts from a seed vector $\mvec{v}$ at
the inputs and propagates tangents, and the \ac{JVP} $\mmat{J}\mvec{v}$ appears at the outputs.
Reverse mode starts from a seed $\mvec{w}$ at the outputs and propagates adjoints, and the
\ac{VJP} $\mmat{J}^\top \mvec{w}$ appears at the inputs, as shown in
Figures~\ref{fig:forward-sweep} and~\ref{fig:reverse-sweep}. For the Hessian, the analogous
Hessian-vector product $\mmat{H}\mvec{v}$ comes from one forward sweep over one reverse sweep.

% \paragraph{Recovering the Jacobian.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Recovering the full matrix takes a sequence of sweeps, one per seed vector. The $n$ unit seed
vectors recover $\mmat{J}$ column by column in forward mode,
and the $m$ unit seed vectors recover it row by
row in reverse mode~\cite{griewank2008evaluating}. The other approach is
accumulation~\cite{griewank1991markowitz}, which combines the local derivatives of the graph
directly into $\mmat{J}$ by eliminating one intermediate vertex at a time, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:vertex-elimination}. Finding the optimal elimination
order is NP-complete~\cite{naumann2008optimal, griewank2008evaluating}. Batching the $n$
unit seeds into one forward sweep gives each node a whole tangent, the derivative of that
node with respect to all the inputs. Eliminating the vertices in the order of evaluation
stores the same derivative at each node, so the batched sweep and forward-order elimination
perform the same computation.

In practice, AD tools such as \texttt{ADOL-C}~\cite{griewank1996adolc} and
\texttt{CppAD}~\cite{bell2012cppad}
record the scalar graph as a runtime trace, one record per elementary
operation~\cite{griewank2008evaluating, Naumann2011}. A single matrix-vector product
unrolls into one scalar multiplication for every entry of the matrix, together with the
additions that sum them, so the trace of a realistic problem can sometimes reach millions of nodes.
On such a trace, every intermediate vertex would store its derivative with respect to all
the inputs, so accumulation is rarely used in practice.
When $\mmat{J}$ is sparse, most of each sweep is then wasted on
zeros, and the cost of recovering the matrix scales with its dimension rather than with its
number of nonzeros.

\subsection{Compression by graph colouring}\label{sec:graph-colouring}
% \paragraph{Graph colouring.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
Several columns of a sparse Jacobian can share one seed~\cite{curtis1974estimation}. Two
columns with no nonzero in a common row are structurally orthogonal, and one sweep
recovers both, each entry landing in a distinct row of the product. Finding the fewest
such groups was later formalized as a colouring of the column intersection graph, which
has one vertex per column and an edge between two columns that share a
row~\cite{coleman1983estimation, gebremedhin2005color}.
Figure~\ref{fig:jacobian-coloring} colours the graph of a $6 \times 8$ Jacobian and
recovers the matrix in three sweeps instead of eight. The approach extends to the Hessian,
which compresses further because its symmetry allows dedicated star and acyclic
colourings~\cite{powell1979estimation, Gebremedhin2009SparseHessians}.

so their cost is spread across repeated evaluations of the same function.

\subsection{\texttt{CasADi}}
\label{sec:casadi}

\texttt{CasADi}~\cite{andersson2019} is an open-source framework for nonlinear optimization
and optimal control built around AD. It is widely used in academia and industry, notably for
model predictive control, trajectory optimization, robotics, and energy
systems~\cite{casadidocs}. Its core is written in C++ and is used from Python, MATLAB, or
Octave. It
provides the building blocks of gradient-based optimization: symbolic expressions,
derivatives, integrators, and interfaces to \ac{NLP} and \ac{QP} solvers. The
gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians these solvers require are obtained by differentiating the
model.
% (qpsol listing + dense-block worst-case ¶ cut 2026-08-04; archived in
% plans/casadi_qp_extraction_cut.md.)

% \paragraph{Expression types.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
\texttt{CasADi} supports two types of expressions, and both return the matrix derivatives in
a sparse format, which is expected by most solvers~\cite{casadidocs}. An
\texttt{SX} graph is scalar, with exact node-by-node sparsity, but its size grows with the
problem data. An \texttt{MX} graph is vectorized. Its nodes are whole
matrix operations, so the graph stays small even when the model is large. Either graph can
be exported as self-contained C code and compiled ahead of time for embedded applications.

% \paragraph{Jacobian assembly.}  (heading removed per advisor style; kept for reference)
\texttt{CasADi} computes its derivatives by the detection, colouring, and batched sweeps of
this section, and caches the pattern, the colouring, and the compiled derivative function
across evaluations. Listing~\ref{lst:casadi-conic} rebuilds the conic-form trees of
Figure~\ref{fig:conic-form-tree}, obtained after canonicalization, as \texttt{MX}
expressions. Differentiating them recovers the problem data that matrix stuffing assembles
in \S\ref{sec:ms-example}.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={The conic form of the running example in
\texttt{CasADi}.}, label=lst:casadi-conic]
import casadi as cs
t1, t2 = cs.MX.sym("t1"), cs.MX.sym("t2")
x = cs.MX.sym("x", n)
g, mu = cs.MX.sym("g", m), cs.MX.sym("mu")
z = cs.vertcat(t1, t2, x)                   # stacked variables
expr = cs.vertcat(x, t1, F @ x - g, t2, x)  # stacked affine expressions
A = -cs.jacobian(expr, z)                   # the matrix A
b = cs.substitute(expr, z, 0)               # the vector b
c = cs.gradient(t1 + mu * t2, z)            # the objective vector c
\end{lstlisting}
The Jacobian with respect to the stacked variable $\mvec{z}$ recovers the matrix $\mmat{A}$
of Figure~\ref{fig:matrix-stuffing} entry by entry. Sparse differentiation is the equivalent of
the matrix stuffing of \S\ref{sec:matrix-stuffing}, with the coefficients obtained by these
sweeps rather than by a bottom-up pass over the tree. The parameters remain symbols, so
$\mvec{b}$ and $\mvec{c}$
are expressions of $\mvec{g}$ and $\mu$, re-evaluated for each parameter value. This is how
the \texttt{qpsol} interface builds its problem data internally, for problems with a
quadratic objective and affine constraints. A model written at the same high level as in
\texttt{CVXPY}, with the norms of~\eqref{eq:example} kept in the expression graph, is solved
through \texttt{nlpsol} instead. The same sweeps assemble the gradient, constraint
Jacobian, and Hessian of the Lagrangian that the \ac{NLP} solver queries at each iteration.

\section{Coefficient extraction from declared structure}
\label{sec:declared-structure}

In contrast to \S\ref{sec:computing-derivatives}, the methods of this section require the user
to declare the structure of the problem. In \texttt{AMPL} and \texttt{JuMP}, the user writes
each objective and constraint as a sum of terms, and the coefficient of every linear and
quadratic term is stored as it is typed. Neither language has an atom library or a
canonicalization step, so a nonlinear expression such as a norm is left to the solver
(\texttt{AMPL}) or written in conic form by the user (\texttt{JuMP}). In place of
differentiation, \texttt{AMPL} writes the stored coefficients to a~\texttt{.nl} file that the
solver parses, and \texttt{JuMP} keeps them as typed in-memory term lists that are copied to
the solver.

\subsection{\texttt{AMPL}}
\label{sec:ampl}

\texttt{AMPL}~\cite{fourer1990ampl} is a commercial algebraic modelling language for
large-scale mathematical programming, developed at Bell Laboratories in the 1980s. It is one
of the most widely used modelling languages in academia and industry, applied to production
planning, scheduling, logistics, and energy systems~\cite{fourer2003ampl}.
A model is written declaratively, close
to its mathematical statement, from sets, indexed parameters, indexed variables, and algebraic
expressions over them. Data is supplied separately, so one model can be used for
many problem instances.
The name \texttt{AMPL} denotes both the modelling language and the commercial system that
processes it. Models are written in the language directly or built through its Python
interface~\cite{amplpy}, and reach a large collection of commercial and open-source solvers
through a single instance format.

The \texttt{AMPL} system splits the work between two programs, which its documentation calls
the translator and the driver. The translator runs as its own process. It parses the model and
the data, expands every indexed declaration into individual variables and constraints with
numeric coefficients, and writes the result to a~\texttt{.nl} file~\cite{gay2005writing}.
The driver is a separate executable that reads the~\texttt{.nl} file
and calls the solver, and each solver is reached through its own driver.
A driver is built on one of two libraries. The \ac{ASL} is the original, a C library that
parses the \texttt{.nl} format and evaluates the expressions it holds~\cite{gay1997hooking}.
\texttt{MP} is the newer
one, which \texttt{AMPL} supplies for solvers that need no derivatives~\cite{mpdocs}. This division
between a translator that writes an instance and a driver that reads it still defines how most
solvers receive nonlinear problems.

The \texttt{.nl} file is an instance format rather than a model format. Sets and indexing are
resolved by the translator, and what remains is one flat record per objective and constraint.
An indexed declaration such as \texttt{var x \{1..n\}} becomes $n$ scalar variables, and an
indexed constraint becomes one record per index, so the file has no notion of a vector.
The linear part of each record is an explicit list of (variable, coefficient) pairs, stored
alongside the bounds and the nonzero counts of the instance. Only the nonlinear parts are kept
as expression graphs, written in prefix notation~\cite{gay2005writing}.

The language has no norm atom and no canonicalization step, so the norms of~\eqref{eq:example}
are entered as square roots of sums of squares. What the solver receives
depends on its driver.
An \texttt{MP}-based driver rewrites
the two norms into second-order cones for a conic solver, and an ASL-based driver passes on a
general nonlinear program. Listing~\ref{lst:ampl-example} states the model.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Ampl, caption={The running example~\eqref{eq:example} in
\texttt{AMPL}.}, label=lst:ampl-example]
param m; param n;
param F {1..m, 1..n};
param g {1..m};
param mu >= 0;
var x {1..n} >= 0;
minimize obj:
    sqrt(sum {i in 1..m} (sum {j in 1..n} F[i,j]*x[j] - g[i])^2)
  + mu * sqrt(sum {j in 1..n} x[j]^2);
\end{lstlisting}
For an instance with $m = 3$, $n = 2$, and $\mu = 0.1$, the translator writes the
\texttt{.nl} file of Listing~\ref{lst:nl-file}. The comments are added by hand to make the
records readable and are not written by the translator.
\begin{lstlisting}[language={}, caption={An abbreviated \texttt{.nl} file for an instance
of the running example.}, label=lst:nl-file]
g3 1 1 0     # text format; 9 more header lines follow
 2 0 1 0 0   # 2 vars, 0 constraints, 1 objective, 0 ranges, 0 eqns
...
O0 0         # objective 0, an expression graph in prefix notation
o0           #   add
o39          #     sqrt
o54          #       sum of 3 terms, one per row of F
...          #         affine subtrees F[i,1]*x[1] + F[i,2]*x[2] - g[i]
o2           #   multiply
n0.1         #     the value of mu
...          #     sqrt subtree of the second norm
b            # variable bounds: x[1] >= 0, x[2] >= 0
2 0
2 0
\end{lstlisting}
The whole objective is written as one expression graph, and the value of $\mu$ is folded into
it as the constant \texttt{n0.1}, so a parameter change requires a new file. For a linear or
quadratic program, no graph remains and the file contains the complete problem data as
coefficient lists.
\texttt{Pyomo}, which also writes \texttt{.nl} files, avoids a new file per parameter change
for solvers with an in-memory interface: its persistent solver interfaces keep the model
inside the solver and update the changed parameters and constraints in
place~\cite{pyomopersistent}.

Inside the driver, the bounds and the linear coefficient lists are read directly from the file,
at a cost linear in the number of nonzeros, and only the nonlinear parts are rebuilt as
expression graphs. What happens to those graphs depends on the library. \texttt{MP} matches
the second-order and exponential cones of \S\ref{sec:bg-canonicalization} in the rebuilt
graphs. A conic solver such as \texttt{Gurobi} or
\texttt{MOSEK} then receives cone constraints rather than the square roots the model states.
ASL instead differentiates the graphs by reverse-mode AD~\cite{gay1991automatic},
with sparse Hessians assembled from partial separability~\cite{gay1996more}.

Any system that writes \texttt{.nl} reaches every solver that reads it. The format has grown
since, with extensions such as logical constraints, and \texttt{AMPL} omits the added header
fields when a model does not use them, so older drivers still read the files it
writes~\cite{gay2005writing}. An instance is an ordinary file, so it can be archived, shipped to
reproduce a bug, and rerun on another machine.
However, serializing the instance to disk can be slow, and every solve repeats it, rebuilding
any expression graphs from scratch.

\subsection{\texttt{JuMP}}
\label{sec:jump}

\texttt{JuMP}~\cite{dunning2017jump, lubin2023jump10recent} is an open-source algebraic
modelling language embedded in Julia, developed at MIT and first released in 2013. It is the
most widely used modelling language in Julia, applied in academia and industry to power
systems, portfolio optimization, scheduling, and routing~\cite{lubin2023jump10recent}.
A model is written with the macros
\texttt{@variable}, \texttt{@constraint}, and \texttt{@objective}. The abstraction layer
between models and solvers is \ac{MOI}~\cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}, and through it the
same model can be passed to any of more than fifty supported solvers~\cite{jumpdocs}.

The macros of \texttt{JuMP} rewrite each expression as it is entered. \texttt{@objective} and
\texttt{@constraint} build affine and quadratic expression objects term by term, so the model
stores coefficient lists instead of an expression tree. These lists are typed by \ac{MOI}. A
\texttt{VectorAffineFunction} is a list of (row, coefficient, variable) terms and a constant
vector. A \texttt{ScalarQuadraticFunction} adds a list of (coefficient, variable, variable)
triples. A constraint states that one such function lies in a set. For a nonnegative orthant
or a second-order cone, this is exactly the functional conic form
of \S\ref{sec:bg-canonicalization}. MOI also supports other sets, such as integrality,
special ordered sets, and indicator constraints~\cite{moidocs}.

The user declares the epigraph variables and cone memberships of~\eqref{eq:graph}
directly, as in Listing~\ref{lst:jump-example}.
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The running example~\eqref{eq:example} in \texttt{JuMP}.},
label=lst:jump-example]
using JuMP
model = Model()
@variable(model, x[1:n] >= 0)
@variable(model, t1); @variable(model, t2)
@constraint(model, [t1; F * x - g] in SecondOrderCone())
@constraint(model, [t2; x] in SecondOrderCone())
@objective(model, Min, t1 + mu * t2)
\end{lstlisting}
The first cone constraint is stored as shown in Listing~\ref{lst:moi-storage}.
\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The first cone constraint as stored by \texttt{MOI}.},
label=lst:moi-storage]
VectorAffineFunction([(1, 1.0, t1), (2, F[1,1], x[1]), ...],
                     [0.0, -g[1], ..., -g[m]])   in   SecondOrderCone(m + 1)
\end{lstlisting}
The expression itself is not retained after the macro returns.

When the model is passed to a solver, MOI copies each constraint into the solver's own
representation with a \texttt{copy\_to} function. Solvers that want matrix data commonly implement
that copy through the \texttt{MatrixOfConstraints} utility, which concatenates the stored term
lists into one sparse matrix in the solver's format~\cite{moidocs}, with no expression graph to
visit and no differentiation. Only the nonlinear path of \texttt{JuMP} retains expression graphs,
and differentiates them by sparse AD.

When a solver does not support a constraint or objective type natively, MOI reformulates it
through its bridge system. A bridge is a structural rewrite from one function-set pair to
another, for instance from a rotated to a standard second-order cone. For a conic solver, a
bridge rewrites a quadratic objective into a second-order cone constraint with an epigraph
variable. MOI selects and composes bridges automatically~\cite{legat2020mathoptinterface}.
Bridging manipulates stored coefficients and adds
auxiliary variables and constraints. A parameter is a variable in the \texttt{Parameter} set. A
parameter multiplied by a variable is stored as a quadratic term, so keeping such a model affine
requires \texttt{ParametricOptInterface.jl}, an extension that substitutes the parameter values
before the solve~\cite{jumpdocs}.

\texttt{Convex.jl}~\cite{udell2014convex}, the \ac{DCP} language of the Julia ecosystem, also
targets \ac{MOI}. Since version~0.16, it represents every affine expression as a sparse matrix
and an offset over the solver variables, accumulated as the problem is converted to
\ac{MOI}~\cite{convexjl2024release}. Like the backends of \texttt{CVXPY}, it stores the
coefficients in a general-purpose sparse format.

\section{Research gap}
\label{sec:research-gap}

The four modelling languages reviewed in this chapter obtain the problem data in one of two ways,
by differentiating an expression graph or by reading the coefficients the user declares.
\texttt{CasADi} is built for nonlinear optimization. Its conic interface, \texttt{qpsol}, supports
\acp{QP} with affine constraints and obtains their coefficients by differentiating the expression
graph, with detected sparsity and seeds compressed by colouring. \texttt{CVXPY} composes the
linear operators of the conic-form trees in one bottom-up pass, which is equivalent to
differentiating them. Both keep the model as an expression graph between solves. \texttt{AMPL} and
\texttt{JuMP} take the coefficients as the user declares them, written to the \texttt{.nl} file by
the translator or stored as term lists by the macros. In both, only nonlinear expressions are
kept.

The four modelling languages also differ in how they handle parameter changes. \texttt{CasADi}
keeps the parameters as symbols and splits the work in two phases. The derivative function is
built once, and a parameter change requires only its evaluation, one sweep per colour.
\texttt{CVXPY} caches the parameter-to-data tensor of \S\ref{sec:ms-parameters} and re-evaluates
with one tensor-vector product, but only for problems conforming to the \ac{DPP} ruleset. For
every other problem nothing is cached and the whole pass is repeated. \texttt{AMPL} folds the
values into the instance, so a change requires the translator to write a new \texttt{.nl} file and
the driver to read it. \texttt{JuMP} modifies the term lists in place.

None of the four combines the two-phase split with direct computation of the problem data.
Chapter~\ref{sec:Theme1} presents a new method that does. It splits the work in two phases, just
like \texttt{CasADi}, but instead of matrix-vector products it computes the entire coefficient
matrix directly, in one bottom-up pass over the conic-form trees. Compared with the \texttt{CVXPY}
backends, our proposed method also uses the dense structure of the problem, with dense storage and
computation instead of general-purpose sparse matrix libraries. Table~\ref{tab:systems-summary}
summarizes these differences.
